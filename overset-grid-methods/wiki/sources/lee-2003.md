# Summary

Lee and Baeder replace the usual two-step [domain connectivity](/definitions/domain-connectivity.md)
procedure with **[implicit hole cutting](/methods/implicit-hole-cutting.md)**. The usual procedure
first cuts holes with the walls as cutters, then runs a [donor search](/methods/donor-search.md)
for the list of inter-grid boundary points. Implicit hole cutting is a cell-selection process.
Every point of every grid, boundary and interior, is tested against all overlapping grids. Among
the candidate donor cells the one with the smallest cell volume $J^{-1}$ wins. An interior point
whose own cell is already the smallest stays a discretization point; otherwise it becomes a
receptor. Outer-boundary points get infinite volume, so they always receive. Grids are built with
cells shrinking toward walls, so holes around bodies, and around refinement or vortex-tracking
grids, appear as a by-product. No separate [hole cutting](/methods/hole-cutting.md),
[overlap optimization](/methods/overlap-optimization.md) or grid priority is needed. The method
was first shown in two dimensions (ref. [10]); this paper extends it to three dimensions and gives
pseudo-code for `BoundaryConnect()`, `InteriorConnect()` and `FindDonor()`.

Supporting devices: a hyperbolicity argument that hole points need not be blanked when
[fringe](/definitions/fringe-point.md) layers are thick enough; detection of non-overlapping grid
pairs by testing boundary points first; a stencil walk seeded by the previous point's donor,
without tree structures; a cross/dot-product inside test; Hermite tricubic interpolation of the
coordinates, inverted by Newton iteration; a monotone Hermite cubic
[inter-grid interpolation](/methods/inter-grid-interpolation.md) of the solution on a 6-point
stencil; the parameter `jHoleImmune` that forces near-wall cells to be donors; and a cost count
("curse of dimensionality") of high-order interpolation in three dimensions. The paper discusses
[point classification](/definitions/point-classification.md),
[orphan points](/definitions/orphan-point.md) and [PEGASUS 5](/software/pegasus-5.md) and
[DCF3D](/software/dcf3d.md) as context. The only demonstration is a half-span tilt-rotor-like
model with 39 grids and about 2.5 million points, connected in about 45 s. There is no accuracy
study.

# Key claims

1. Implicit hole cutting is a cell-selection process, not a wall-cutting process. Its holes "are
   both automatically optimum and not affected by any imperfection on the wall surface".
   (Abstract; Implicit Hole Cutting) **Stated**
2. The solver need not blank hole points for the solution update. Hyperbolicity prevents
   contamination, provided the hole-fringe layers are thick enough and completely enclose the
   body. (Abstract; p. 3; pp. 7–9) **Derived** (heuristic argument, no proof). The Chimera
   papers exclude hole points from an implicit update instead; the bundle records the two as a
   difference of setting, not a contradiction
   ([point classification](/definitions/point-classification.md), Qualifications).
3. A structured single-grid flow solver runs in overset mode with no change other than one extra
   array dimension per grid. (p. 3) **Stated**
4. The IBLANK array $i_b$ is still needed only for contour plots, for blanking hole points in the
   residuals of steady convergence checks, and for the residuals of implicit Newton subiterations.
   Explicit unsteady computations need it only for plotting. (p. 3) **Stated**
5. The cell volume $J^{-1}$ is the main selection criterion. The solver already holds it, so the
   criterion costs nothing extra. (p. 3; p. 5) **Stated**
6. Walls are not cutters, so hole-cutting failures from thin bodies or surface gaps cannot occur,
   and orphans or leakage from such failures are eliminated. Orphans from insufficient overlap must
   still be checked. (p. 3) **Stated**
7. The hole is automatically optimum: the cell sizes of the first hole-fringe layer and of its
   donors match as well as the grids allow. There is no hole-optimization step, no user parameter
   (offset, wall distance, donor quality) and no grid priority. (p. 3) **Stated**
8. Implicit hole cutting cuts holes around bodies and around flow features (vortex, shock and
   refinement grids) in one pass. (p. 3; Figs. 1, 15) **Shown** (figures only)
9. Testing boundary points first detects non-overlapping grid pairs, so the cost for interior
   points does not grow with $n_g$. (p. 5–6) **Stated**
10. Grids that share part of a wall (O-H, C-H or one-wall topologies) do not cut each other; the
    pair is flagged `Ioverlap` $=-1$. (p. 6; Fig. 3) **Stated**
11. Seeding each search with the previous point's donor makes tree data structures unnecessary.
    The first point of each grid starts from cell (1,1,1), and accelerating that first search is
    "not really warranted". (pp. 6–7) **Stated**
12. Scanning the six faces of a block without repeating its 8 corners and 12 edges uses face sizes
    $(J_{\max}-2)(K_{\max}-2)$, $I_{\max}K_{\max}$ and $I_{\max}(J_{\max}-2)$ in the three
    directions. (p. 7; Fig. 6) **Derived**
13. Contamination is impossible if the distance information travels from an invalid point within
    $\Delta t$ is less than the thinnest fringe thickness. "First order checks of many cases"
    indicate that common $\Delta t$ are within this limit. (pp. 8–9) **Stated**
14. An implicit method for a hyperbolic equation on a grid with a hole does not significantly
    contaminate the solution, especially with Newton subiterations, provided the fringe condition
    holds and the values at invalid points are not very large. (p. 9) **Derived** (heuristic)
15. The Hermite tricubic interpolant of the coordinates is $C^1$, exact and consistent, and is "the
    lowest order interpolant consistent with the grid metrics used by the solver". Decoupled cubic
    interpolation is not $C^1$; trilinear interpolation ($q=2$) is only $C^0$ and inconsistent with
    the metrics. (p. 4) **Stated**
16. Cubic interpolation allows a larger spacing ratio between two interpolating grids, usually
    about 2:1. (p. 4) **Stated**
17. The monotone Hermite cubic solution interpolation needs a $q=6$ stencil per direction (five
    points for each of the two middle points where first derivatives are needed). Hence the first
    two cells from each grid boundary are not donors. (p. 10) **Stated**
18. Implicit (coupled) interpolation between two outer-boundary points with minimum overlap and a
    6-point stencil is not needed, because the end points of the stencil contribute little.
    Interpolating all grid-boundary points before the hole-fringe points removes the coupling
    between them. (p. 11; Fig. 12) **Stated**
19. Near walls, low-order 4- or 2-point stencils are unavoidable and are flagged. (p. 11; Fig. 13)
    **Stated**
20. Trilinear initial guesses of the interpolation coefficients beat the midpoint 0.5 for
    high-order interpolation; 0.5 is preferred on coarse multigrid levels with linear
    interpolation. (p. 11) **Stated** ("Tests showed", no data)
21. A move to the neighbour cell for an out-of-range point ($s_m<0$ or $s_m>1$) is made only after
    the third Newton iteration, since Newton usually converges in about three. (p. 11) **Stated**
22. From two to three dimensions the cost per point and variable grows by $50\,\%$ for an interior
    point but about $q$ times for an interpolated point: $q+1$ to $q^2+q+1$ operations for
    decoupled interpolation. (p. 12) **Derived**
23. A three-dimensional tricubic interpolation needs $4^3-2^3=56$ derivative evaluations, 32 of
    them cross derivatives; decoupled cubic interpolation needs $2(q^2+q+1)$, i.e. 14, 42 and 86
    for $q=2,4,6$. (p. 12) **Derived**
24. Recommendation: interpolate a single layer of outer-boundary points instead of the usual two;
    biased stencils at the first interior points are then needed with 5-point interior stencils but
    not with a fourth-order compact scheme; hole fringes should have as many layers as possible.
    (p. 12) **Stated**
25. Half-span quad-tilt-rotor-like model, 39 grids and about 2.5 million points: connectivity takes
    about 45 s of CPU on an Alpha 21264 at 667 MHz, and linear instead of cubic interpolation saves
    about 20–25 %. (Example, p. 12; Figs. 14–15) **Shown** (timing only)

# Printed slips

- **p. 10, inside test:** the text writes the test as "$\vec r_i.(\vec n_i\text{ x }\vec r_i)$"
  positive for all six faces. A triple product with a repeated vector is identically zero
  *(compiler check, not in the sources)*, so the test as printed can never pass. Fig. 10 prints
  the test as $(\vec p\times\vec q)\cdot\vec r>0$ with $\vec n=\vec p\times\vec q$, i.e.
  $\vec r_i\cdot\vec n_i>0$.
- **Wall index:** p. 3 places walls at "K=1", p. 11 says "J is normal to wall". This is
  inconsistent wording, not an error.
- Typographical: Fig. 1 caption "Catesian"; "accomodate" (p. 3); "inconsequence" (p. 7);
  "contiminated" (p. 8); "resididual" (p. 9 pseudo-code); "simplfying", "topoplogy" (p. 10);
  "interprolated", "Newton interations" (p. 11); "course spacing" for coarse (p. 12); Fig. 9
  caption "treament".

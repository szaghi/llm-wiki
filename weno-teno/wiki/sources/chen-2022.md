# Summary

A second-order paper: [gradient reconstruction](/methods/unstructured-gradient-reconstruction.md)
for cell-centered finite volumes on unstructured grids, for the Euler and
Navier–Stokes equations. It extends the authors' vertex-based
weighted-least-squares averaged-gradient method (VWLSQ), in which a least-squares
fit of a linear function through the surrounding cell-centre values gives a value
and a gradient at each vertex, and the cell gradient is a convex combination of
the vertex gradients; an iterative treatment (VWLSQ-ITER) adds the boundary-face
values to the fit at boundary vertices.

The two extensions are: (i) for viscous fluxes, the face gradient is the average
of the vertex gradients of the face, which on a uniform Cartesian grid involves
every immediate neighbour and so avoids the odd–even decoupling of averaged cell
gradients (Appendix C); (ii) for shock capturing without a slope limiter, a
"WENO-type" nonlinear weighting: the least-squares weights of the vertex fit are
divided by the squared pressure jump between vertex and cell, which the
authors relate to the solution-dependent least squares of Mandal et al. (the high-order relative in
the bundle is [WLS-ENO](/methods/wls-eno.md)), and the vertex gradients are combined direction by
direction with [WENO-JS](/methods/weno-js-reconstruction.md)-type weights built on
directional [smoothness indicators](/definitions/smoothness-indicator.md). The
paper motivates it by the claim that [TVD](/definitions/total-variation-diminishing.md)-type
limiters lose accuracy at smooth extrema ([critical points](/properties/critical-point-accuracy.md)),
which it does not analyse.

The comparison is with node-averaging Green–Gauss and cell-based least-squares
gradients, with or without a face-gradient correction, and with the MLP-pw slope
limiter. Evidence is numerical: gradient errors on regular, irregular and perturbed
triangles; a boundary-layer profile on aspect-ratio-1000 triangles in the manner of
the [method of manufactured solutions](/definitions/method-of-manufactured-solutions.md);
second order on the [isentropic vortex](/benchmarks/isentropic-vortex.md) with the
[HLLC flux](/methods/hll-riemann-solvers.md); Mach 5 and 10 inviscid cylinder flows,
where only the nonlinear weighting converges fully on the hardest cases;
[double Mach reflection](/benchmarks/double-mach-reflection.md), where the
least-squares gradients with MLP-pw give kinked Mach stems, attributed to the
reconstruction since the flux is fixed (cf. the [carbuncle](/definitions/carbuncle-phenomenon.md)
literature); and two laminar viscous cases. A shorter cylinder wake is read as
added [numerical dissipation](/definitions/numerical-dissipation.md). Nothing is
proved.

# Key claims

Registry notation (see the notation map), as on the
[gradient reconstruction](/methods/unstructured-gradient-reconstruction.md) page: the reconstructed
scalar $\varphi$ is $u$, the source's least-squares cell weights $\omega_i$ are $w_i$, its linear
vertex weights $\gamma_l$ are $d_l$, its nonlinear vertex weights $\tilde\gamma_j$,
$\tilde\omega_a$ are $\alpha_j$, $\omega_a$, its indicators $SI$ are $\beta$, its limiter
coefficient $\phi_i$ is $\theta_i$, and its $\varepsilon$ is $\epsilon$.

1. The vertex gradient comes from a weighted least-squares fit of a linear function through the cell-centre values around vertex $a$, with unknowns $u_a$ and $\nabla u_a$ (one more than the space dimension), and the cell gradient is a convex combination of the vertex gradients (§3.2, Eqs. 10–16). **Stated** (recalled from the authors' earlier work)
2. Node-averaging Green–Gauss gradients (inverse-distance or pseudo-Laplacian weights) lose linear exactness on perturbed triangles in interior and boundary cells; least-squares gradients and VWLSQ-ITER keep it, VWLSQ with lower errors, especially in interior cells (§6.1, Figs. 3–4). **Shown**
3. With $\epsilon=10^{-5}$ the nonlinear weighting reaches the accuracy of linear VWLSQ-ITER on the smooth function $x^2e^y$ (§6.1, Figs. 3–4). **Shown**
4. On regular triangles the least-squares face gradients are better than second order "due to the symmetry", and at least first order on all three grid types; inverse-distance Green–Gauss face gradients lose linear exactness except on regular grids, pseudo-Laplacian ones on perturbed grids (§6.1, Figs. 5–7). **Shown**
5. At boundary faces all Green–Gauss methods and the corrected least-squares methods lose linear exactness because of first-order extrapolated boundary values; VWLSQ-ITER keeps first order because its iteration restores linearly exact boundary values (§6.1, Figs. 8–10). **Shown**
6. On aspect-ratio-1000 perturbed triangles with the manufactured boundary-layer profile, VWLSQ-ITER, with or without nonlinear weighting, gives the lowest error of the wall-normal gradient in boundary cells and faces, whether the boundary values are unknown or exact (§6.2, Figs. 11–12). **Shown**
7. The face-gradient correction (Eqs. 20–21) causes no significant boundary error on these grids, because the vector from the boundary-face centre to the cell centre is nearly parallel to the wall (§6.2). **Derived** (geometric argument)
8. Isentropic vortex (HLLC, four-stage Runge–Kutta, $\Delta t=0.002$, $t=2$): all methods are about second order except inverse-distance Green–Gauss, whose last order is 0.86; VWLSQ-ITER has errors $3.29\times10^{-3}$ to $3.59\times10^{-5}$ (orders 2.46, 2.16, 1.90), the nonlinear variant $4.02\times10^{-3}$ to $3.59\times10^{-5}$ (2.72, 2.19, 1.90), larger only on the coarse meshes (§6.3, Table 1). **Shown**
9. On the Mach-5 inviscid cylinder (van Leer flux-vector splitting, LU-SGS, CFL up to 100) the nonlinear weighting converges in fewer steps and less CPU time than the Green–Gauss and vertex-stencil least-squares methods; the face-stencil least squares does not converge on the coarse grid and diverges on the fine one; on the fine grid only the nonlinear weighting converges fully, VWLSQ-ITER with MLP-pw does not (§6.4, Fig. 15). **Shown**
10. On the fine Mach-5 grid every method but the nonlinear weighting overshoots the post-shock pressure on the stagnation line; only it gives oscillation-free contours behind the shock (§6.4, Figs. 16–17). **Shown**
11. The ratio of nonlinear to linear cell gradient is active mostly at the shock, like the MLP-pw coefficient, so no extra dissipation is added in smooth regions; acting separately in $x$ and $y$, it behaves as a multi-dimensional limiter (§6.4, Fig. 18). **Shown**
12. One reconstruction step on the coarse Mach-5 grid costs 6.77 ms (pseudo-Laplacian Green–Gauss), 23.49 ms (face-stencil least squares), 40.36 ms (vertex-stencil least squares), 17.92 ms (VWLSQ-ITER with MLP-pw) and 14.84 ms (VWLSQ-ITER with nonlinear weighting); no hardware is stated (§6.4, Table 2). **Shown**
13. At Mach 10 on the coarse grid only the nonlinear weighting converges fully; the others diverge or stall (§6.4, Fig. 19). **Shown**
14. In the Mach-10 double Mach reflection (576,910 triangles, van Leer, four-stage Runge–Kutta, $t=0.2$) both least-squares gradients with MLP-pw produce kinked Mach stems, most strongly the vertex-stencil one; VWLSQ-ITER with nonlinear weighting, on the same common-vertex stencil, does not (§6.5, Fig. 22). **Shown**
15. On the laminar flat plate ($M=0.3$, $\mathrm{Re}=10^6$, 14,504 irregular triangles) only the VWLSQ methods and the corrected face-stencil least squares converge fully, the Green–Gauss methods fail, and the latter shows significant errors in velocity profile and skin friction while VWLSQ is accurate with or without the correction (§6.6, Figs. 24–25). **Shown**
16. On the cylinder at $\mathrm{Re}=40$ ($M=0.3$, 20,000 triangles, LU-SGS CFL $10^3$) only the VWLSQ methods converge fully; wake length 2.14 (2.095 with the correction) against 2.13, drag coefficient 1.588 (1.591) against 1.48; the shorter wake with the correction is read as extra numerical dissipation (§6.7, Table 3, Fig. 27). **Shown**
17. In the vertex-based method the nonlinear weighting is cheaper than a slope limiter, because smoothness is measured only for vertex gradients (§7). **Stated** (supported by Table 2)
18. Second-order TVD-based limiters can damage accuracy at smooth critical points, the TVD principle cannot guarantee removing oscillations on unstructured grids, and several common limiters do not strictly guarantee monotonicity (§1, §5). **Stated**
19. On a uniform Cartesian grid, averaging Green–Gauss cell gradients onto faces gives a viscous term that skips the left and right neighbours (Eq. C.3); averaging face-stencil least-squares gradients gives the wide Laplacian $\frac1{4\Delta x}(u_{i+2,j}-2u_{i,j}+u_{i-2,j})$, which skips every immediate neighbour and gives checkerboard wiggles (Eq. C.6); the vertex-averaged face gradient uses the $3\times3$ block around the cell (Eq. C.8) (App. C). **Derived**
20. The vertex-stencil least squares decouples like Green–Gauss (App. C). **Stated** ("straightforward to prove")
21. Because the nonlinear least-squares weights depend on pressure only, the fit matrix is shared by all variables, but it must be re-solved at every step for every vertex (§5). **Stated**
22. A larger $\epsilon$ in Eqs. 24 and 26 gives more accuracy in smooth regions, a smaller one suppresses oscillations better; typical range $10^{-2}$–$10^{-6}$, default $10^{-3}$; the influence of $\epsilon$ is "important" but not studied (§5, §6.7). **Stated**

# Printed slips

- Table 3 labels both reference values "Experiment" and §6.7 calls the computed drag "close to the experimental result", but $C_d=1.48$ is cited to Visbal [95], whose printed title ("Evaluation of an implicit Navier-Stokes solver for some unsteady separated flows") names a computation; 1.588 against 1.48 is a 7.3% difference.
- Table 3 gives identical wake length and drag for the linear and nonlinear variants, yet §6.7 credits the nonlinear weighting with capturing the closed wakes; §5 says face gradients always use linear weights.
- §7 says that with the nonlinear weighting "the present simulations are able to converge without specifically concerning the decoupling problem", while Appendix C attributes the absence of decoupling to the vertex-averaged face gradient, which by §5 uses linear weights.
- Eq. 16 defines $L_{li}$ as the distance from vertex $l$ to cell $i$, while Eq. 15 names the cell $c$.
- Ref. [27] prints volume 422 (2020) with the DOI suffix `j.jcp.2018.10.023` and no page or article number.
- Language: "limiters typically loss their designed accuracy" (§1), "not necessary the best choice" (§6.4), "32th Aerospace Sciences Meetings" (refs. [22], [66]).

# Summary

Shu (2009a) surveys weighted essentially nonoscillatory (WENO) schemes. Its stated
premise is that "at the heart of the WENO schemes is actually an approximation procedure
not directly related to PDEs". §2 presents that procedure on its simplest instance. The
first case is fifth-order WENO interpolation of point values at $x_{i+1/2}$ from three
three-point substencils, with linear weights $d_k=(1/16,5/8,5/16)$. The second is the
fifth-order [WENO-JS reconstruction](/methods/weno-js-reconstruction.md) from cell
averages, with $d_k=(1/10,3/5,3/10)$. Both use the Jiang–Shu
[smoothness indicator](/definitions/smoothness-indicator.md) and the weights
$\alpha_k=d_k/(\epsilon+\beta_k)^2$ with $\epsilon=10^{-6}$ "typically". The section
explains the [ENO property](/definitions/eno-property.md) of the weights against
classical [ENO](/methods/eno-reconstruction.md) stencil selection. It states the accuracy
condition $\omega_k=d_k+O(\Delta x^2)$ and treats negative linear weights through a
WENO-integration example, nonuniform meshes, and WENO derivative and integration.

§3 builds finite-volume and
[conservative finite-difference](/definitions/conservative-finite-difference-scheme.md)
WENO schemes. The ingredients are [monotone fluxes](/definitions/monotone-scheme.md),
including the [Lax–Friedrichs flux and splitting](/methods/lax-friedrichs-flux.md), and
the third-order [SSP Runge–Kutta](/methods/ssp-runge-kutta.md) scheme of
[Shu & Osher (1988)](/sources/shu-1988.md). Shu & Osher's lemma turns finite-difference
fluxes into a reconstruction from point fluxes split by a smooth
[flux splitting](/methods/flux-vector-splitting.md). In 2-D a finite-volume scheme of
order above two is stated to cost two to five times the finite-difference one, which in
turn needs uniform or smooth meshes. The review also states
[scale invariance](/properties/scale-invariance.md) and self-similarity of the
reconstruction when $\epsilon$ is relative to the data. It discusses order loss
downstream of shocks, [characteristic](/methods/characteristic-decomposition.md) against
componentwise reconstruction, staggered [central WENO](/methods/cweno.md), and contact
resolution.

§4 covers WENO for Hamilton–Jacobi equations. §5 relates WENO to the
[discontinuous Galerkin method](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin.md), through
[Hermite WENO](/methods/hermite-weno.md) and [WENO limiters](/methods/weno-limiter-dg.md),
and to compact, spectral, multiresolution, dispersion-optimized and
[hybrid](/methods/hybrid-weno.md) schemes. It also cites the
[mapped weights](/methods/mapped-weno.md) of Henrick et al. for
[smooth extrema](/properties/critical-point-accuracy.md). §6 surveys applications with
figures reproduced from earlier papers: [shock–vortex interaction](/benchmarks/shock-vortex-interaction.md),
Rayleigh–Taylor, [high-Mach jets](/benchmarks/high-mach-astrophysical-jet.md) and traffic
flow. §7 lists open problems. The review proposes no new scheme.

# Key claims

Registry notation. The source numbers substencils $S_1,S_2,S_3$ from the left and writes
$\gamma_j$, $w_j$, $\tilde w_j$, $\varepsilon$. Here they are $S_0,S_1,S_2$ ($S_0$ the most
upwind for $u^-$), $d_k$, $\omega_k$, $\alpha_k$, $\epsilon$. The function $h$ of the
Shu–Osher lemma is the page-local $\phi$, as on the
[conservative finite-difference page](/definitions/conservative-finite-difference-scheme.md).

1. The fifth-order interpolant (2.4) on $\{x_{i-2},\dots,x_{i+2}\}$ equals the combination (2.5) of the three third-order interpolants (2.1)–(2.3) with $d_0=1/16$, $d_1=5/8$, $d_2=5/16$ (§2.1). **Derived**
2. When $u$ is a step with its jump in $(x_i,x_{i+1})$ and every small stencil contains it, the large-stencil interpolant (and the second and third candidates) is monotone on $[x_i,x_{i+1}]$. Its derivative has at most three zeros, lying in $(x_{i-2},x_{i-1})$, $(x_{i-1},x_i)$ and $(x_{i+1},x_{i+2})$ (§2.1, p. 86). The result "also holds when $u(x)$ is a more general piecewise smooth function [67]". **Proved** for the step; general case **Stated**
3. A WENO convex combination of monotone approximations is monotone even when every substencil contains the discontinuity (§2.1, p. 86). **Stated**
4. "It can be verified [78] that, as long as $\omega_k=d_k+O(\Delta x^2)$" (2.7), the WENO interpolation is fifth order where $u$ is smooth on the large stencil. With the weights (2.10), $\omega_k=O(\Delta x^4)$ on a substencil that contains a discontinuity (§2.1, p. 87). **Stated**
5. The smoothness indicator "in most of the WENO papers" is the Jiang–Shu form (2.8), $\beta_k=\sum_{l=1}^{2}\Delta x^{2l-1}\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}\big(p_k^{(l)}\big)^2dx$, "a scaled sum of the square $L^2$ norms of all the derivatives". The factor $\Delta x^{2l-1}$ is there "to make sure that the final explicit formulas ... do not depend on the mesh size" (§2.1). **Stated**
6. The nonlinear weights are $\omega_k=\alpha_k/\sum_l\alpha_l$, $\alpha_k=d_k/(\epsilon+\beta_k)^2$ (2.10). $\epsilon$ "is typically chosen to be" $10^{-6}$ "in actual calculations", or "a small number relative to the size of the typical $u_i$". Other decreasing functions ($\beta_k^{-q}$ with $q\ne2$, or $e^{-c\beta_k}$) "were tested and the choice in (2.10) was made in [78] after considering both cost and performance" (§2.1, p. 87). **Stated**
7. For reconstruction from cell averages, the fifth-order value (2.14) is the combination (2.15) of (2.11)–(2.13) with $d_k=(1/10,3/5,3/10)$. The indicators become (2.17) (§2.2). **Derived**
8. Carlini, Ferretti & Russo [14] prove that the degree-$(2k-1)$ interpolant on the large stencil equals $\sum_{l=1}^kd_l(x)p_l(x)$. Here the $d_l(x)$ are polynomials of degree at most $k-1$, nonnegative on the interval common to all substencils (§2.3.1). **Stated**
9. History: third-order WENO reconstruction appears in the first WENO paper, [Liu et al. (1994)](/sources/liu-1994.md) (finite volume). Fifth order and the general indicator (2.8) appear in [Jiang & Shu (1996)](/sources/jiang-1996.md) (finite difference), higher orders in Balsara & Shu (2000) (§1, §2.3.1). **Stated**
10. Linear weights are fixed by accuracy and may be negative. "If the usual WENO procedure is used without modification, oscillations and instability may appear". The Shi–Hu–Shu technique [146] handles this. The alternative is to lower the target order so that positive weights exist, at a loss of accuracy (§2.3.3). **Stated**
11. WENO integration on the same five-point stencil has sixth-order linear weights $d_0=-17/240$, $d_1=137/120$, $d_2=-17/240$, two of them negative (§2.3.4, Eqs. (2.18)–(2.22)). **Derived**
12. For any flux splitting (3.10) with $\partial f^+/\partial u\ge0$ and $\partial f^-/\partial u\le0$, $h(u^-,u^+)=f^+(u^-)+f^-(u^+)$ is a monotone flux (§3.2, p. 97). **Stated**
13. The fifth-order WENO spatial discretization with first-order forward Euler or second-order Runge–Kutta time stepping "is linearly unstable [178], hence they should not be used even if the problem is steady state" (§3.1, p. 95). **Stated**
14. Lemma 3.1 (Shu & Osher [154]): if $\frac1{\Delta x}\int_{x-\Delta x/2}^{x+\Delta x/2}\phi(\xi)\,d\xi=f(u(x))$, then $\frac1{\Delta x}\big(\phi(x_{i+1/2})-\phi(x_{i-1/2})\big)=\partial_xf(u)\vert_{x_i}$. "The proof is straightforward: just take an $x$ derivative on both sides" (§3.2, Eq. (3.8)). **Proved**
15. Finite-difference WENO "can only be used on uniform or smooth meshes". "A conservative finite difference scheme (3.6) with a local flux (3.7) cannot achieve higher than second order accuracy on arbitrary nonuniform nonsmooth meshes" (Merryman [112]) (§3.2, p. 97). **Stated**
16. In 1-D the cost of finite-volume and finite-difference WENO "is the same". The finite-volume scheme is the more flexible one (any monotone flux, any mesh) (§3.3). **Stated**
17. In 2-D, nonlinear finite volumes need two 1-D reconstructions and a Gauss quadrature per face, "about three times the cost of the one-dimensional case per cell per direction". Finite differences cost "exactly the same as in the one-dimensional case per point per direction". "A finite volume scheme (of order of accuracy higher than two) is two to five times as expensive as a finite difference scheme of the same order ... depending on the specific coding and type of computer", and more in 3-D (§3.4, p. 99; [19] for ENO). **Stated**
18. The finite-volume/finite-difference distinction matters only from third order on, because "the cell average agrees with the value of the function at the cell centroid to second order accuracy" (§3.4, p. 99). **Stated**
19. With $\epsilon$ "a small percentage of the typical size of $u_i$", the WENO reconstruction is scale invariant: it is unchanged when the solution is amplified by a constant or when $\Delta x$ changes. The finite-volume and finite-difference WENO schemes are then self-similar, unchanged when $\Delta x$ and $\Delta t$ change by the same factor (§3.5.1). **Stated**
20. WENO schemes "can be shown to converge with high order accuracy using Strang's framework [158] when the solution of the PDE is smooth [78]". For discontinuous solutions "there is no general proof of stability and convergence" (§3.5.2). **Stated**
21. For scalar problems the design order is observed away from discontinuities. For systems, pointwise errors downstream of a shock can lose order unless subcell resolution is used in the shock cell (§3.5.2). Table 3.1, from Chou & Shu [25], shows a fourth-order residual-distribution WENO scheme on nozzle flow. Its after-shock $L^1$ orders are 1.98–2.83 with regular integration and 3.90–4.93 with subcell integration. **Shown** (reproduced)
22. Componentwise reconstruction "usually" suffices up to third order. Characteristic reconstruction gives better nonoscillatory results at higher order, "albeit with an increased computational cost" (§3.5.3). For very high-order central WENO "characteristic WENO reconstruction is still necessary to obtain stable results [131]" (§3.5.5). **Stated**
23. Contact discontinuities are harder to resolve sharply than shocks because characteristics run parallel to them (Fig. 3.3). The listed remedies are artificial compression [180, 78], subcell resolution [64, 78] and antidiffusive flux correction [42, 175] (§3.5.7). **Stated**
24. For Hamilton–Jacobi equations the finite-difference WENO scheme (4.4) has "no finite volume scheme" counterpart and "does not have the restriction of uniform or smooth meshes" (§4). **Stated**
25. The convergence of a semi-Lagrangian large-time-step WENO scheme with interpolation order up to 9 for convex Hamilton–Jacobi equations [14] is "the best theoretical convergence result obtained so far involving WENO schemes" (§4, p. 107). **Stated**
26. DG with the test function $1_{I_i}$ reduces to the finite-volume scheme (3.4), so DG is a "generalized finite volume scheme". DG is more flexible on unstructured meshes but "less robust in dealing with solutions with strong shocks", because its limiters are less advanced than the WENO procedure (§5.1). **Stated**
27. A high-order WENO scheme "may use 3 to 10 times more CPU time than a second order high resolution scheme" and may still be advantageous (§1, p. 83). **Stated**
28. Traffic flow example (Fig. 6.3, from [186], 9-class LWR model): first-order Lax–Friedrichs needs 25600 points to match fifth-order WENO with 400 points. With 6400 Lax–Friedrichs points the nine "staircases" are missed (§6.4). **Shown** (reproduced)
29. The Liu–Osher third-order nonoscillatory schemes satisfy a strict maximum principle, an advantage over WENO for, e.g., a volume ratio in $[0,1]$. However, "it seems difficult to generalize ... to higher than third order" (§5.6). **Stated**
30. Analysis should target the "unmodulated" WENO schemes. Modifications made "solely for the purpose of stability or convergence analysis" at extra cost or lower quality "should be discouraged", especially if they destroy self-similarity (§7, item 7). **Stated** (author's opinion)

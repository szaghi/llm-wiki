# Summary

The paper builds a sharp-interface method for inviscid compressible
liquid–vapour flow with evaporation and condensation that conserves mass,
momentum and energy exactly. It extends the conservative cut-cell
[level-set method](/methods/level-set-method.md) of Hu et al.: the two phases
are coupled only through an interfacial flux $\hat X$ on the interface segment
$\Delta\Gamma$ of each cut cell, never through ghost-fluid states. The
authors contrast this with ghost-fluid phase-change methods (Houim & Kuo, Das
& Udaykumar, Fechter et al.), which they call inherently non-conservative.
The interfacial flux comes from the two-phase Riemann problem with phase
change of Fechter et al., whose
[jump conditions](/definitions/rankine-hugoniot-condition.md) at the phase
interface carry the latent heat $Q_{\mathrm{lat}}$ and the surface-tension
term $\sigma_{\mathrm{st}}\kappa_{\mathrm c}$. Summing the vapour and liquid
interfacial fluxes leaves exactly the physical surface-tension and
latent-heat sources, the mechanism behind the claim of
[local conservation](/results/local-conservation-flux-form.md).

The exact solver needs an eight-dimensional root finding. The authors replace
it by a four-wave HLL-type
[phase-change Riemann solver](/methods/phase-change-riemann-solver.md): two
outer shocks with Davis speeds, the phase interface and a reconstructed
contact. A density-ratio closure and the Hertz–Knudsen phase-change model
close the jump system, and all star states become closed-form functions of
the mass flux $\dot m$, found by a Steffensen iteration in 2–4 steps. The paper
also shows that $\dot m$ must be evaluated from the star states adjacent to the
phase interface, not from the initial left and right states. Evaluating it
from the initial states converges to a wrong solution.

The tests use fifth-order
[WENO-JS](/methods/weno-js-reconstruction.md), SSP-RK2, CFL 0.6 and wavelet
multiresolution grids. They are evaporation and condensation
[gas–liquid Riemann problems](/benchmarks/gas-liquid-riemann-problems.md)
for n-dodecane (Helmholtz-energy EOS) and water
([stiffened gas](/definitions/stiffened-gas-equation-of-state.md)), compared
with exact solutions; a circular droplet and a circular bubble with phase
change, where the approximate solver runs in 86.99 s and 106.93 s against
318.17 s and 325.82 s for the exact one *(compiler check, not in the sources:
speed-ups 3.66 and 3.05)*; an oscillating droplet (period error 1.47 %); 2D and 3D
[shock–droplet interactions](/benchmarks/shock-water-column.md) with and
without phase change; and the near-wall vapour-bubble collapse cases A–C of
Lauer et al.

# Key claims

Notation of the Notation map: $V$ is the normal velocity, $W^-$ (liquid)
and $W^+$ (vapour) the initial Riemann states, $W^*_{\mathrm L}$,
$W^*_{\mathrm M}$, $W^*_{\mathrm R}$ the star states,
$(\mathrm{liq},\mathrm{vap})$ the pair adjacent to the phase interface,
$s^\pm$ the outer wave speeds, $s_{\mathrm p}$ and $s_{\mathrm c}$ the
phase-interface and contact speeds.

1. In the conservative sharp-interface framework the phases are coupled only through the interfacial flux $\hat X$ on $\Delta\Gamma_{i,j}$. With phase change and surface tension switched off, Eq. (26) recovers the scheme of Hu et al. (§2.3, §3.2, after Eq. (27)). **Stated**
2. Summing the vapour and liquid interfacial fluxes gives $\hat X_{\mathrm{vap}}+\hat X_{\mathrm{liq}}=\big[0,\,-\sigma_{\mathrm{st}}\kappa_{\mathrm c} n_1,\,-\sigma_{\mathrm{st}}\kappa_{\mathrm c} n_2,\,-\sigma_{\mathrm{st}}\kappa_{\mathrm c} n_3,\,jQ_{\mathrm{lat}}-\sigma_{\mathrm{st}}\kappa_{\mathrm c} s_{\mathrm p}\big]^\top$. Mass is therefore conserved exactly, and the momentum and energy residuals are exactly the surface-tension force and the latent-heat and surface-tension work (Eq. (27)). **Derived**
3. Because the approximate solver imposes the vapour-side jump conditions strictly, unlike the approximate solver of Fechter et al. (2018), the total energy including the heat of evaporation or condensation, Eq. (34), is conserved (§3.2, p. 8; Fig. 12). **Shown**
4. The method keeps the "zero-order consistency" of the surface-tension discretization: the total surface-tension force on a drop of constant curvature is zero (§3.2, p. 7). **Stated**
5. The general Riemann problem with phase change has four waves: two outer nonlinear waves, a phase interface and a contact. When the phase interface is subsonic and non-characteristic (Eq. (12)) the jump system is underdetermined and needs a phase-change model for $\dot m$ (§3.1). **Stated**
6. The approximate solver has five constant states separated by four waves: two shocks with Davis speeds $s^-=V^--c^-$ and $s^+=V^++c^+$, the phase interface and a contact. Thirteen independent equations for fifteen unknowns are closed by the density-ratio supplement $\rho^*_{\mathrm{liq}}/\rho^*_{\mathrm{vap}}\approx\rho^-/\rho^+$, Eq. (19), and the phase-change model $\dot m=f_{\mathrm{HK}}(W^*_{\mathrm{liq}},W^*_{\mathrm{vap}})$, Eq. (20) (§3.2, Eqs. (13)–(20)). **Derived**
7. All star states and $s_{\mathrm p}$, $s_{\mathrm c}$ can be written in closed form as functions of $\dot m$ alone (Appendix A, Eq. (A.2)). The problem reduces to the scalar equation $\dot m=f_t(\dot m)$, Eq. (24). **Derived**
8. For $\dot m\to0$ the phase interface coincides with the contact: $s_{\mathrm p}$ and $s_{\mathrm c}$ tend to $(l_2-r_2-\sigma_{\mathrm{st}}\kappa_{\mathrm c})/(l_1-r_1)$ from both sides (Eq. (A.3)). **Derived**
9. Using the initial states $W^-$, $W^+$ in the mass-flux model, as Houim & Kuo and Das & Udaykumar do, converges to an incorrect solution. Using the adjacent star states $W^*_{\mathrm{liq}}$, $W^*_{\mathrm{vap}}$ converges to the exact solution (§4.1.1, Fig. 4). The authors state that this has not been reported before. **Shown**
10. Once the cut-cell input states approach the exact adjacent states, the Riemann problem between them reproduces them (Eq. (29)). A $\dot m$ evaluated from the star states therefore stays consistent, whereas $f_{\mathrm{HK}}(W^-,W^+)\neq f_{\mathrm{HK}}(W^{*,e}_{\mathrm{liq}},W^{*,e}_{\mathrm{vap}})$. The authors claim this for all phase-change models (§4.1.1, p. 9). **Derived** (informal argument)
11. To match the exact solution, the Hertz–Knudsen coefficients differ between the two strategies: $(\lambda_{\mathrm{ev}},\lambda_{\mathrm{co}})=(1.0,0.9)$ with star states and $(0.756,0.680)$ with the initial states (§4.1.1). **Shown**
12. The error of the supplement Eq. (19), measured against exact interfacial Riemann solutions recorded in the cut cell, is about 11.6 % at first and then drops quickly to zero (§4.1.1, Fig. 5). **Shown**
13. The Steffensen iteration (tolerance $\lvert \dot m_{k+1}-\dot m_k\rvert<10^{-6}$) usually needs 3–4 iterations (§3.2). In the 2D droplet case it needs 3 iterations in a few critical cells and later 2 in most cells; in the 2D bubble condensation case, 3 in all cut cells (§4.2, Fig. 11). **Shown**
14. On an AMD EPYC 7351 (16 cores, 2.4 GHz), the circular droplet with evaporation takes 318.165 s with the exact solver against 86.9863 s with the approximate one; the circular bubble with condensation takes 325.824 s against 106.925 s (§4.2.1, §4.2.2). **Shown**
15. The speedup comes from (i) replacing the eight-dimensional root finding by a scalar iteration and (ii) avoiding EOS calls through the HLL approximation, which matters most for the Helmholtz-energy EOS (§4.2.1). **Stated**
16. The method is independent of the EOS, because no EOS form enters the derivation of the approximate solution (§1). **Stated**
17. The star-region temperature is computed as $T^*=T^*(p^*,e^*)$: under the HLL approximation the star density, pressure and energy are not EOS-consistent, and the density carries the error of Eq. (19) (§3.2, p. 7). **Stated**
18. In the condensation Riemann problems the interval between phase interface and contact is very small ($4.9\times10^{-5}$ m for n-dodecane, $2.6\times10^{-6}$ m for water). On 2000 cells it is unresolved, yet produces no spurious waves or instability. A multiresolution grid with effective resolution 409600 resolves the n-dodecane case (§4.1.2, §4.1.4). **Shown**
19. Oscillating droplet: the two-phase Rayleigh formula, Eq. (37), with mode 2 and $R_0=0.4899$ mm gives a period of 0.204 ms; the computed period is 0.207 ms, an error of about 1.47 %. Phase change alters the amplitude but not the frequency (§4.3, Fig. 13). **Shown** ($\omega^2=6\sigma_{\mathrm{st}}/((\rho_{\mathrm{liq}}+\rho_{\mathrm{vap}})R_0^3)$ with $\sigma_{\mathrm{st}}=20$ N/m and $\rho_{\mathrm{liq}}+\rho_{\mathrm{vap}}=1074.29$ kg/m³ gives 0.2039 ms; $R_0=\sqrt{0.6\times0.4}$ mm is the radius of the circle with the area of the initial ellipse, and $(0.207-0.204)/0.204=1.47$ % *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.)
20. A shock–droplet interaction with phase change shows an additional wave from the initial evaporation and condensation after the shock has passed, and a larger interface deformation than without phase change (§4.4.1, Figs. 14–15). **Shown**
21. Near-wall bubble collapse: the results agree qualitatively with Lauer et al. Effective resolution $8192\times8192$ is converged for case A. Jet speeds are about 1800 m/s (A), 900 m/s (B) and 1400 m/s (C); the wall pressure exceeds 10 000 bar (A) and reaches about 31 000 bar near the wall (B) (§4.5). **Shown**
22. Level-set reinitialization can delete under-resolved droplets or bubbles, which violates conservation; remedies are left to future work (§3.2, p. 8). **Stated**
23. The time step follows the full-cell CFL condition; small cut cells are stabilized by the mixing procedure of Hu et al.; a positivity flux limiter may be needed for harder problems (§4). **Stated**
24. The framework does not extend straightforwardly to incompressible flows. An extension through a weakly compressible model may need preconditioning below Mach 0.1 (§2.1, §5). **Stated**

# Printed slips

- Eq. (18) (p. 6) prints $\rho^1_l/\rho^1_v\gg\rho^2_l/\rho^2_l\sim\rho^3_v/\rho^3_v\sim O(1)$. The two right-hand ratios have the same numerator and denominator and are identically 1, so the scale argument behind the supplement Eq. (19) is not stated as intended.
- Appendix A, after Eq. (A.3), p. 26: "When this limit case happens, $W^*_L$ will vanish, and thus the interfacial fluxes are calculated by using $W^*_L$ and $W^*_R$." The state that disappears when the phase interface merges with the contact is the middle state $W^*_M$; the sentence names $W^*_L$ both as vanishing and as used.
- Eqs. (38)–(40) place the droplet centre at $x=0.35$ mm ($\phi=-1.0+\sqrt{(x-0.35)^2+y^2}$ mm), while the Mach 1.5 shock starts at $x=2$ mm and moves in $+x$ (post-shock velocity 130.29 m/s, inflow on the left). A droplet of radius 1 mm centred at 0.35 mm spans $-0.65\le x\le1.35$ mm, entirely inside the post-shock region at $t=0$ *(compiler check, not in the sources)*. The §4.4.1 text also does not state the n-dodecane droplet radius; the level set implies 1 mm.
- Eq. (27) omits the factor $\Delta\Gamma$ that Eq. (26) carries.
- Eq. (1): a stray "s" is typeset before $\partial U/\partial t$.
- §5: "could be extended to c flows using the weakly compressible model": a word is missing.
- Typos: "start region" for "star region" (§3.2, p. 6); "Steffense method" (§3.2, p. 7); "less than %1" (§2.2); ref. [51] prints "steffensen–s".

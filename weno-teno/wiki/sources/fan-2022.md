# Summary

The paper builds a [conservative finite-difference](/definitions/conservative-finite-difference-scheme.md)
WENO scheme for the compressible Navier–Stokes equations that keeps $\rho$ and
$\rho e$ positive ([bound preservation](/properties/bound-preservation.md)). It
extends the positivity-preserving finite-difference WENO of Zhang & Shu (2012)
for Euler, to which it reduces exactly when the viscous flux vanishes. The
obstacle it names is that the Navier–Stokes diffusion operator has no standard
first-order positivity-preserving scheme, so the Euler-based approaches built
on first-order fluxes, such as the
[Hu–Adams–Shu flux limiter](/methods/positivity-preserving-flux-limiter.md), do
not carry over.

The total flux $f=f^a-f^d$ is treated as one convection flux, and the
[Lax–Friedrichs flux splitting](/methods/lax-friedrichs-flux.md) of the
Shu–Osher scheme is replaced by Zhang's (2017) nonlinear splitting
$z^\pm=\tfrac12\big(u\pm f(u,\nabla u)/a_{i+1/2}\big)$, with a generalized
speed $a_{i+1/2}$ that contains the viscous stress and heat flux
([positivity-preserving Navier–Stokes fluxes](/methods/positivity-preserving-navier-stokes-flux.md)).
Read as a formal finite-volume scheme for the auxiliary function of the split
variables, the forward-Euler update decomposes, by an $N_{\mathrm{GL}}$-point
Gauss–Lobatto rule, into a convex combination of states in $G$ (Theorem 1), a
classical decomposition rather than an
[optimal cell-average decomposition](/methods/optimal-cell-average-decomposition.md).
A density-then-internal-energy [Zhang–Shu scaling limiter](/methods/zhang-shu-scaling-limiter.md)
on the reconstructed split values enforces the theorem's hypotheses, 2D
follows dimension by dimension, and [SSP-RK3](/methods/ssp-runge-kutta.md)
with halve-and-restart stepping gives the fully discrete scheme.
Reconstruction is [characteristic](/methods/characteristic-decomposition.md),
with [WENO-JS](/methods/weno-js-reconstruction.md) 5 and 7 and the
[WENO-ZQ](/methods/cweno.md) scheme of Zhu & Qiu. An alternative (§4) splits
only the viscous flux this way.

Tests at $\mathrm{Re}=1000$ and $\infty$ show design order with the limiter
active, and robust runs of the
[double rarefaction](/benchmarks/double-rarefaction-problem.md), 1D and 2D
[Sedov](/benchmarks/sedov-blast-wave.md), [Leblanc](/benchmarks/leblanc-shock-tube.md),
Mach 5.09 [shock diffraction](/benchmarks/shock-diffraction.md), Mach 2000
[jet](/benchmarks/high-mach-astrophysical-jet.md) and a Mach 10 reflection
whose reflection part is the [double Mach reflection](/benchmarks/double-mach-reflection.md).
The paper asserts that the unlimited schemes blow up on every test and that
the positivity treatment adds no excessive
[artificial viscosity](/methods/artificial-viscosity.md).

# Key claims

Registry notation (see the notation map): the source's $\mathbf U$ is $u$
(finite-difference point values $u_i$), its velocity $\mathbf u$ is $v$, its
$\mathbf S=\nabla\mathbf U$ is $\nabla u$, its splitting coefficient
$\beta_{i+1/2}$ is $a_{i+1/2}$, its viscous stress and heat flux
$\boldsymbol\tau$, $\mathbf q$ are $\tau_{\mathrm v}$, $q_{\mathrm h}$, its number
of Gauss–Lobatto points $N$ is $N_{\mathrm{GL}}$, its WENO $\varepsilon$ is
$\epsilon$.

1. With negative density or pressure the linearized Euler equations are not hyperbolic and the linearized initial-value problem is ill-posed; a conservative positivity-preserving Eulerian scheme on fixed meshes is $L^1$-stable for $\rho$ and $E$ (§1, citing Zhang 2017). **Stated**
2. $G=\{\rho>0,\ \rho e(u)>0\}$, $\rho e=E-\tfrac12\rho\lvert v\rvert^2$, is convex for any equation of state because $\rho e(u)$ is concave for $\rho>0$; for other equations of state (e.g. JWL) $G$ no longer ensures $p>0$, but positive $\rho$ and $e$ suffice for robustness (§1, Eqs. (1.2)–(1.3)). **Stated**
3. Positivity-preserving finite-difference WENO methods for Euler rely on first-order positivity-preserving schemes (exact Godunov, flux-vector splitting, Lax–Friedrichs, HLLE, gas-kinetic); the lack of a standard low-order positivity-preserving scheme for the Navier–Stokes diffusion operator is "the key difficulty" (§1.1). **Stated**
4. WENO for diffusion becomes more robust when negative linear weights are avoided, but such schemes still do not preserve positivity of density and pressure on demanding tests (§1.1). **Stated**
5. A positivity-preserving finite-difference scheme for 1D Navier–Stokes is "quite straightforward"; the main difficulty is the multidimensional stress tensor (§1.2). **Stated**
6. The finite-difference scheme is more efficient and easier to implement than the positivity-preserving DG of Zhang (2017) and the finite-volume HWENO of Fan et al. (2021), with less memory than DG and lower cost, especially in multi-D (§1.3, abstract). **Stated** (no timings)
7. When $f^d\equiv0$ the scheme reduces exactly to the positivity-preserving finite-difference WENO of Zhang & Shu (2012) for Euler; the splitting is nonlinear, so its artificial-viscosity properties are less understood than those of Lax–Friedrichs splitting, but a nonlinear splitting "seems more suitable" for nonlinear WENO reconstruction (§1.3, §3). **Stated**
8. If $u\in G$ and $a_{i+1/2}>\max\big[\lvert v\rvert+\frac1{2\rho^2e}\big(\sqrt{\rho^2q_{\mathrm h}^2+2\rho^2e\lvert\tau_{\mathrm v}-p\rvert^2}+\rho\lvert q_{\mathrm h}\rvert\big)\big]$ over the stencil (3.3), then $z^\pm=\tfrac12\big(u\pm a_{i+1/2}^{-1}f(u,\nabla u)\big)\in G$ for any gradient (§3.2, "proved in Lemma 6" of Zhang 2017). **Stated** (cited)
9. **Theorem 1:** the $(2r+1)$-th order finite-difference WENO scheme (3.1), (3.5) is positivity-preserving, $u^n_i\in G\Rightarrow u^{n+1}_i\in G$, if the reconstructed split values at both interfaces of every cell and the two residual states $P^{\pm,*}_i$ (3.11) lie in $G$, under $\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\max_ia_{i+1/2}\le\frac1{2N_{\mathrm{GL}}(N_{\mathrm{GL}}-1)}$, $N_{\mathrm{GL}}=\lceil\frac{2r+3}2\rceil$ (Eqs. (3.9)–(3.11)). The proof splits the update into two halves and writes each as a convex combination of four states in $G$ with the Gauss–Lobatto rule applied to an interpolating polynomial of degree $2r$ (§3.2). **Proved**. *(compiler check, not in the sources: the normalized endpoint weight of the $N_{\mathrm{GL}}$-point Gauss–Lobatto rule is $\frac1{N_{\mathrm{GL}}(N_{\mathrm{GL}}-1)}$; for WENO5, $r=2$, $N_{\mathrm{GL}}=4$ and the bound is $1/24$; for WENO7, $r=3$, $N_{\mathrm{GL}}=5$ and it is $1/40$.)*
10. Theorem 1 is a weak positivity result: (3.1), (3.5) is not positivity-preserving unless (3.9) is enforced by an extra limiter; the sufficient condition is intrinsic to any finite-difference scheme read as a finite-volume scheme for an auxiliary variable, and the polynomials $P^\pm_i$ are never used in the implementation (Remarks 3.2–3.3). **Stated**
11. For smooth solutions (3.10) gives $\Delta t=O(\Delta x)$, which does not meet linear stability $\Delta t=O(\mathrm{Re}\,\Delta x^2)$ of explicit convection–diffusion; the latter must always hold and (3.10) be enforced only when positivity is lost; for shocks far from vacuum (3.10) is more stringent than needed (Remark 3.3, §3.5). **Derived**
12. The scaling limiter (3.12)–(3.13), applied at each interface to the two reconstructed split values, enforces (3.9); it is "essentially the same" as Zhang's limiter applied to the two formal finite-volume schemes (3.7)–(3.8), and the modified scheme (3.1), (3.14) is positivity-preserving (§3.3). **Derived**
13. The limiter does not destroy high-order accuracy for smooth solutions with $\min\rho>0$, $\min\rho e>0$ when $\Delta x$ is small, since the auxiliary functions then lie in $G$ (§3.3). **Stated** ("straightforward to show")
14. In 2D, with directional coefficients $a^{(1)}$, $a^{(2)}$ (3.21)–(3.22), $u^{n+1}_{ij}$ is the average of two formal 1D updates with doubled $\Delta t$ (3.23); Theorem 1 applies to each, so positivity and the limiter extend dimension by dimension (§3.4). **Derived**. *(compiler check, not in the sources: applying (3.10) to the doubled step gives $\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\max a^{(1)}\le\frac1{4N_{\mathrm{GL}}(N_{\mathrm{GL}}-1)}$ and the same in $y$; the paper does not write the 2D bound.)*
15. Any SSP Runge–Kutta method, a convex combination of forward-Euler steps, keeps positivity since $G$ is convex; SSP-RK3 (3.24) is used with the limiter at every stage (§3.5). **Stated**
16. Algorithm 2: $\Delta t=\min\{0.6\,\Delta x/a,\ 0.001\,\mathrm{Re}\,\Delta x^2\}$, $a=\max_i(\lvert v_i\rvert+c_i)$; if a stage leaves $G$, $\Delta t\leftarrow\Delta t/2$ and the step restarts; the restart cannot loop forever, because it ends once (3.10) holds for each forward-Euler step (Algorithm 2, Remark 3.4). **Derived**
17. Theorem 1 holds for any approximation of $\nabla u$, but a positivity-preserving scheme can still oscillate: a high-order linear approximation of $v_x$, $e_x$ produced oscillations, so a WENO finite-difference derivative of the conserved variables is used, with quotient and product rules (Remark 3.5). **Shown** (asserted from tests, no figure)
18. **Theorem 2 (§4):** with global Lax–Friedrichs splitting of $f^a$ as in Zhang & Shu (2012) and the sign-flipped splitting $z^{d,\pm}=\tfrac12(u\mp f^d/a^d_{i+1/2})$ (4.3)–(4.4), the diffusive half-update is positivity-preserving under analogous hypotheses and $\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\max_ia^d_{i+1/2}\le\frac1{4N_{\mathrm{GL}}(N_{\mathrm{GL}}-1)}$; the advective half-update is the Euler scheme with a factor 2 (§4.2). **Proved** (sketched, "the same lines")
19. The approaches of §3 and §4 show "no significant difference"; only §3 is shown (§5). **Shown** (asserted)
20. Without the positivity-preserving flux and limiter, WENO-JS5, WENO-JS7 and WENO-ZQ5 "will blow up for all one- and two-dimensional examples in this section"; with them few cells are limited and the cost does not increase much (§5). **Shown** (asserted; no blow-up figures or timings)
21. 1D accuracy (Table 5.1, $\mathrm{Re}=1000$, $T=0.1$, $L^1$ errors at 320 points): WENO-JS5 $5.96\times10^{-8}$ (order 4.36) with $\epsilon=10^{-15}$, $2.99\times10^{-8}$ (5.10) with $\epsilon=\Delta x^2$; WENO-JS7 $6.78\times10^{-10}$ (6.50) / $9.96\times10^{-11}$ (7.36); WENO-ZQ5 $4.93\times10^{-8}$ (5.23) / $8.23\times10^{-9}$ (5.31). Limited-cell fractions at 320: 0.91%, 1.00%, 0.87% ($10^{-15}$); 0.20%, 0.88%, 1.25% ($\Delta x^2$). **Shown**
22. 2D accuracy (Table 5.2, $320\times320$): WENO-JS5 $1.07\times10^{-7}$ (4.88) / $6.20\times10^{-8}$ (5.12); WENO-JS7 $1.27\times10^{-9}$ (6.51) / $2.32\times10^{-10}$ (7.47); WENO-ZQ5 $2.14\times10^{-7}$ (4.78) / $1.92\times10^{-8}$ (5.31). Limited fractions 0.75%, 0.37%, 0.60% / 0.01%, 0.31%, 1.04%. **Shown**
23. The text reads the tables as: WENO-JS5 and WENO-ZQ5 reach fifth order with both $\epsilon$; WENO-JS7 has smaller errors and "certain order loss" with $\epsilon=10^{-15}$ but optimal seventh order with $\epsilon=\Delta x^2$; the flux and limiter do not destroy high order (§5, p. 14). **Shown**. The paper's own Tables 5.1–5.2 print, for WENO-JS7 with $10^{-15}$, orders 6.99, 7.38, 6.50 from 80 to 320 points (the order loss is at the finest pair only), 3.14 at $160^2$ for WENO-ZQ5 in 2D with $10^{-15}$, and a larger WENO-JS7 than WENO-JS5 error at 10 points ($1.94\times10^{-1}$ against $4.65\times10^{-2}$). *(compiler check, not in the sources: recomputing $\log_2(e_N/e_{2N})$ from the printed errors reproduces every printed order to within $\pm0.01$.)*
24. $\epsilon=\Delta x^2$ in the WENO-JS weights gives optimal order (citing Aràndiga et al.); $\epsilon=10^{-15}$ can reduce artificial viscosity on fine meshes for shocks; elsewhere the choice makes a "marginal difference", so only $\epsilon=10^{-15}$ is shown (§5). **Stated** / **Shown** (asserted)
25. Double rarefaction: results comparable to the positivity-preserving DG of Zhang (2017); WENO-ZQ5 is better than JS5 and JS7 in the low-density centre (Example 5.2, Fig. 5.1, 400 points). **Shown**
26. Leblanc: WENO-ZQ5 "produces more oscillation possibly due to its wider stencil in reconstruction" (Example 5.4, Fig. 5.3, 3200 points). **Shown**
27. Average limited-cell fractions per step in 2D (JS5/JS7/ZQ5): Sedov 0.303%, 0.248%, 0.299% ($\mathrm{Re}=\infty$) and 0.309%, 0.119%, 0.139% ($\mathrm{Re}=1000$); shock diffraction 0.0024%, 0.0026%, 0.0125% and 0.0005%, 0.0010%, 0.0079%; Mach 2000 jet 0.178%, 0.230%, 0.416% and 0.103%, 0.070%, 0.225%; Mach 10 reflection–diffraction 0.0017%, 0.0016%, 0.0034% and 0.0002%, 0.0001%, 0.0009% (Examples 5.5–5.8). **Shown**
28. Mach 2000 jet: negative density and pressure never appear; "WENO schemes without any positivity treatment will simply blow up for this test" (Example 5.7, $\Delta x=1/640$). **Shown** (blow-up asserted)
29. Mach 10 reflection–diffraction: at $\mathrm{Re}=1000$ the Kelvin–Helmholtz fine structure is smeared by numerical plus physical viscosity, but the positivity flux and limiter do not induce excessive numerical viscosity; WENO-ZQ5 has lower artificial viscosity than JS5 and JS7 (Example 5.8, Fig. 5.7, $\Delta x=1/480$). **Shown** (qualitative)

# Printed slips

- Heat-flux sign (p. 9): (3.16) $\mathbf F^d=(0,\boldsymbol\tau,\mathbf u\cdot\boldsymbol\tau-\mathbf q)^T$ with (3.17) $\mathbf q=\frac1{\mathrm{Re}}\frac\gamma{\mathrm{Pr}}(e_x,e_y)^T$ gives the energy diffusive flux $\frac1{\mathrm{Re}}(\tau_{xx}u+\tau_{yx}v-\frac{\gamma e_x}{\mathrm{Pr}})$, but (3.18) prints $(E+p)u-\frac1{\mathrm{Re}}(\tau_{xx}u+\tau_{yx}v+\frac{\gamma e_x}{\mathrm{Pr}})$. (3.18) agrees with the 1D (2.6), $u\tau+q$ with $q=\frac\gamma{\mathrm{Pr}}e_x$, where $q$ is the negative of the Fourier heat flux; (1.1) calls $\mathbf q$ "the heat flux". The coefficient (2.9)/(3.3) uses $\lvert q\rvert$ and $q^2$ and is sign-insensitive.
- Theorem 2 (p. 12): "where $N=\lceil2r+3\rceil$", against $N=\lceil\frac{2r+3}2\rceil$ in Theorem 1, whose proof Theorem 2 follows "the same lines"; the Gauss–Lobatto exactness argument ($2N-3\ge2r$) gives $\lceil\frac{2r+3}2\rceil$. As printed the bound is valid but far more restrictive.
- Algorithm 2, line 13 (p. 10): after the final stage computes $\mathbf U^{n+1}$ the test reads "if $\mathbf U^{(1)}\in G$ then"; it should test $\mathbf U^{n+1}$.
- Algorithm 1 (p. 9): two steps labelled "Step IV"; the flux step is Step V.
- §3.1 (p. 5): "$A=L\Lambda R$"; with $\mathbf T=L\mathbf H$ and $\mathbf H=R\mathbf T$ the diagonalization is $A=R\Lambda L$.
- §2.1 (p. 4): "$z^+=\alpha f^+$ and $z^-=-\alpha f^-$"; from $z^\pm=\frac12(u\pm f/\alpha)$ and $f^\pm=\frac12(f\pm\alpha u)$ one has $z^+=f^+/\alpha$, $z^-=-f^-/\alpha$.
- §5 (p. 12): WENO-ZQ5 linear weights "$\gamma_1=0.98,\ \gamma_1=0.01,\ \gamma_1=0.01$", the index repeated.
- Example 5.8 (p. 17): domain "the union of $[0,1]\times[0,1]$ and $[-1,1]\times[1,3]$", but the reflective wall "$x=1$, $-1\le y\le0$" lies outside that union; the boundary data fit a second block $[1,3]\times[-1,1]$.
- §2.2 (p. 4): the coefficient $\eta$ in $\tau=\eta u_x$ is never defined.
- §3.3: the density lower bound is called $a$, while $a=0.6$ is the CFL constant of Algorithm 2; stray commas in "$(\rho^*_i,m^*_i,E^*_i,)^T$" (p. 8); "$\mathbf S=\nabla U$" with non-bold $U$ (Remark 3.5).
- Typos: "Double Rarefraction problem" (Fig. 5.1), "on longer ensures" (p. 2), "much stringent" (§3.5), "positive-preserving" (§3), "there don't increase a lot of computational cost" (§5), "mach 10" (Example 5.8), "seven-order" (§5).

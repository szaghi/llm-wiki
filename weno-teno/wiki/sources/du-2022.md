# Summary

The paper modifies the simple
[WENO limiter for discontinuous Galerkin](/methods/weno-limiter-dg.md) of
Zhong & Shu (2013) and its triangular-mesh extension by Zhu, Zhong, Shu & Qiu
(2013) for Runge–Kutta [DG](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin.md) on 2D
hyperbolic systems. The limiter replaces the polynomial of a troubled cell by a
nonlinear convex combination of its own polynomial and the neighbours'
polynomials shifted to the target-cell average, with simple linear weights and
Jiang–Shu-type [smoothness indicators](/definitions/smoothness-indicator.md)
(the [WENO-JS](/methods/weno-js-reconstruction.md) weight formula). For
systems the original limiter maps, for each edge direction, all four
polynomials of a triangle and its neighbours to the
[characteristic fields](/methods/characteristic-decomposition.md) of the
normal Jacobian. The improved one transforms and combines only two, the
target-cell polynomial and the neighbour across that edge, and averages the
directional results with neighbour-area weights. On polygonal meshes built
from arbitrarily distributed point clouds the saving is larger: two
polynomials per direction instead of $N_j+1$, $N_j$ the number of
neighbours.

No theory is given. The claims are shown on the 2D Euler equations with
$\mathbb P^1$–$\mathbb P^3$: accuracy tests (a sine density wave and the
[isentropic vortex](/benchmarks/isentropic-vortex.md)) with the KXRCF
[troubled-cell indicator](/definitions/troubled-cell-indicator.md)
threshold deliberately lowered so that most cells are limited, and shock
tests ([double Mach reflection](/benchmarks/double-mach-reflection.md),
[shock diffraction](/benchmarks/shock-diffraction.md), the
[Mach 3 forward step](/benchmarks/forward-facing-step.md) and a transonic
NACA0012). Time stepping is [SSP Runge–Kutta](/methods/ssp-runge-kutta.md).
CPU time saved ranges about 6–55% on shocked problems, largest for
$\mathbb P^2$ and point clouds, with less smearing at the double-Mach stem and
a weaker spurious Mach stem in the forward-step $\mathbb P^2$ run. Earlier
[Hermite WENO](/methods/hermite-weno.md) and
[multi-resolution WENO](/methods/multi-resolution-weno.md) limiters and
[subcell finite-volume limiting](/methods/subcell-finite-volume-limiting.md)
are cited as alternatives; on point clouds the
[positivity-preserving](/properties/bound-preservation.md) limiter of Du &
Shu (a [Zhang–Shu-type](/methods/zhang-shu-scaling-limiter.md) scaling) is
used for $\mathbb P^2$.

# Key claims

Registry notation (see the notation map): the source's DG degree $k$ is $N$,
its linear weights $\gamma_0,\gamma_1$ are $d_0,d_1$, its unnormalized
weights $\tilde\omega_i$ are $\alpha_i$, its $\varepsilon$ is $\epsilon$, its
velocity $(\mu,\nu)$ is $(v_1,v_2)$.

1. The improved limiter transforms only two polynomial vectors per direction to characteristic fields on triangles, where the original transforms four, and two instead of $N_j+1$ on point-cloud polygonal meshes (§3.1 after Eq. 3.3; end of §3.2; Abstract). **Stated** (by construction)
2. "This step is the main difference and improvement of this new limiter from the original one": the reduction of transformed polynomials per direction (§3.1, after Eq. 3.3). **Stated**
3. Any positive linear weights with $d_0+d_1=1$ suffice for accuracy because both combined polynomials are $(N+1)$th-order approximations in smooth regions; a large $d_0$ is used for a non-oscillatory shock transition (§3.1, after Eq. 3.6). **Stated** (argued, no proof)
4. The limiter preserves the cell average of the target polynomial because each neighbour polynomial is shifted by Eq. (3.1) to the target-cell average (§3.1, §3.2 step 2(a)). **Derived**
5. Linear weights $d_0=0.999$, $d_1=0.001$ on triangles (§4.1) and $d_0=0.9995$, $d_1=0.0005$ on point clouds (§4.2); $\epsilon=10^{-6}$ (after Eq. 3.5). **Stated** (parameter choice)
6. On the sine-wave accuracy test (Ex. 4.1) both limiters keep the design order $N+1$ and the error level of the unlimited DG even with up to 78.94% ($\mathbb P^1$), 90.85% ($\mathbb P^2$) and 81.73% ($\mathbb P^3$) of the cells flagged on the coarsest mesh; at $h=2\pi/160$ the three variants coincide (e.g. $L^2$ errors 1.97E-05, 1.08E-07, 1.73E-10) (Tables 4.1–4.3). **Shown**
7. The improved limiter perturbs the unlimited DG error less than the original: $\mathbb P^1$, $h=2\pi/10$, $L^2$ 2.46E-02 (improved) against 3.87E-02 (original) and 5.63E-03 (no limiter); $\mathbb P^3$, $h=2\pi/10$, 2.74E-05 against 6.94E-05 and 1.26E-05 (Tables 4.1, 4.3). **Shown**
8. CPU time saved on Ex. 4.1 falls with refinement as the flagged fraction falls: $\mathbb P^1$ 36.25% to 12.21%, $\mathbb P^2$ about 40–44% on coarse meshes to 1.77%, $\mathbb P^3$ 41.35% to 0.00% (Tables 4.1–4.3). **Shown** (the text's "decreasing as the mesh is refined" is not strictly monotone in Table 4.2; see Printed slips)
9. Even with no cell flagged the limited runs cost more than unlimited DG, because of the troubled-cell detection (§4.1; e.g. $\mathbb P^3$, $h=2\pi/160$, 19221.72 s against 14721.41 s). **Shown**
10. Isentropic vortex (Ex. 4.2, $t=0.2$, $C_k=10^{-4}$): both limiters keep the order and error level; the improved one perturbs less and saves 13.07–26.28% ($\mathbb P^1$), 14.73–35.60% ($\mathbb P^2$) and 0.13–32.69% ($\mathbb P^3$) CPU (Tables 4.4–4.6). **Shown** (the unlimited $\mathbb P^2$ $L^2$ orders on this test are 2.64–2.70, Table 4.5, not commented on)
11. Double Mach reflection on triangles ($h=1/200$, $C_k=0.01$): equally good at $\mathbb P^1$; at $\mathbb P^2$ the improved limiter "has bettor [sic] control on the oscillations" and better resolution around the double-Mach region; time saved 12.34% ($\mathbb P^1$) and 17.88% ($\mathbb P^2$) (Ex. 4.3, Figs. 4.3–4.4, Table 4.7). **Shown**
12. Shock diffraction on triangles ($h=1/16$): almost identical results; time saved 6.6% ($\mathbb P^1$) and 11.73% ($\mathbb P^2$), although the improved limiter flags more cells at $\mathbb P^2$ (12.63% against 9.48%) (Ex. 4.4, Figs. 4.6–4.7, Table 4.8). **Shown**
13. Mach 3 forward step ($h=1/400$ near the corner, $1/100$ elsewhere): the spurious Mach stem at the bottom wall of the original limiter's $\mathbb P^2$ result "decreases when using the improved limiter"; time saved 9.35% ($\mathbb P^1$) and 33.96% ($\mathbb P^2$) (Ex. 4.5, Fig. 4.9, Table 4.9). **Shown**
14. Transonic NACA0012 ($M_\infty=0.8$, $1.25^\circ$; $M_\infty=0.85$, $1^\circ$; 5780 triangles): almost the same results; the improved limiter saves at least 20% CPU (20.19%, 35.49%, 21.45%, 36.25%) and flags fewer cells (Ex. 4.6, Table 4.10). **Shown**
15. On point-cloud meshes errors are larger and observed orders lower than on triangles "due to the complexity of the randomly generated point cloud", but both limiters keep the order and error level of the unlimited DG (Ex. 4.7, Tables 4.11–4.13). **Shown**
16. Point-cloud CPU savings on accuracy tests reach 58.27% ($\mathbb P^3$, 400 points, Table 4.13) and 57.82% ($\mathbb P^2$ vortex, 1600 points, Table 4.15), and are slightly negative ($-0.25\%$) where almost no cells are flagged (Tables 4.11, 4.16). **Shown**
17. Double Mach reflection on point clouds (400000 points $\mathbb P^1$, 800000 points $\mathbb P^2$; MPI with 10 and 30 processes): time saved 36.11% and 54.92%; the $\mathbb P^2$ Mach stem is better resolved with the improved limiter, and the original limiter needed 1664000 points in Du & Shu (2018) for similar resolution (Ex. 4.9, Table 4.17, Figs. 4.14–4.15). **Shown** (the comparison with the earlier paper **Stated**)
18. Shock diffraction on point clouds (219200 and 493200 points): same quality for both limiters, more oscillatory than on triangles; time saved 9.90% ($\mathbb P^1$) and 40.91% ($\mathbb P^2$) (Ex. 4.10, Fig. 4.16, Table 4.18). **Shown**
19. The improved limiter is "as insensitive to the troubled cell indicators as previous WENO limiters" (§3; Ex. 4.7). **Shown** only in the sense that accuracy survives heavy over-flagging
20. "Less smearing and higher resolution due to less information being used for reconstruction in each direction" (Abstract; §1; end of §3.2). **Stated** as cause; the resolution gain is **Shown** for some examples (double Mach reflection and forward step at $\mathbb P^2$) and absent elsewhere
21. The idea "may be generalized to other limiters with a characteristic-wise limiting procedure" and to higher dimensions (§5). **Stated**
22. The positivity-preserving limiter of Du & Shu (2018) is used for the $\mathbb P^2$ DG on point clouds in the discontinuous examples (§4.2). **Stated** (usage)

# Printed slips

- Ref. [37] duplicates ref. [34] (same authors, "J.-X. Qiu" against "J. Qiu", same title, J. Comput. Phys. 248 (2013) 200–220); §4 cites the original limiter on triangles as "[37]", §§1 and 3 as "[34]".
- Table 4.6 ($\mathbb P^3$ vortex): the "without limiter" CPU column is identical to that of Table 4.5 ($\mathbb P^2$), 6.46E-02, 1.81E-01, 1.19, 7.67, 79.54, and so appears copied; with it the limited $\mathbb P^3$ runs would cost about four times the unlimited one with 0% flagged cells (e.g. 326.75 s at $h=14/160$), against about 1.3 times in Ex. 4.1.
- After Eq. (3.5): "$\beta_0,\beta_l$ are the so-called smoothness indicators"; Eq. (3.6) defines $\beta_i$, $i=0,1$, so $\beta_l$ should be $\beta_1$.
- §4.1: "The percentage of time saved is decreasing as the mesh is refined" is not monotone in Table 4.2 (40.61%, 44.29%, 40.63%, 27.91%, 1.77%).
- Typos: "Double Mach refection" (Ex. 4.3 heading, Table 4.7 and Figs. 4.3–4.4 captions, Ex. 4.9 heading); "bettor control" (Ex. 4.3); "as shown in 4.15" (Ex. 4.9, missing "Fig."); "angle of attack $\alpha=1.25$" without the degree sign at first mention (Ex. 4.6).

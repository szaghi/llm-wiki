# Summary

The paper builds a nonlinear limiter and a troubled-cell indicator for the
nodal [spectral difference method](/methods/spectral-difference-method.md) at
third and fourth order (SD3, SD4; $N+1$ solution points per direction,
polynomial degree $N=2,3$). The limiter acts dimension by dimension along
lines of solution points and uses only the target cell and its two face
neighbours. Its candidates are [Hermite](/methods/hermite-weno.md)
polynomials built from cell averages and cell-averaged first and second
derivatives of the neighbouring SD polynomials, all obtained from nodal values
by quadrature. Instead of limiting low-order moments one at a time, as earlier
compact Hermite [WENO limiters](/methods/weno-limiter-dg.md) do, it forms a
direct convex combination of high-order candidates: three Hermite quadratics
(3-DoF reconstruction) and, for SD4, four Hermite cubics on top (4-DoF). The
linear weights follow from Taylor matching of cell-averaged derivatives and
depend on the solution-point set. The nonlinear weights are either
WENO-JS-type or [TENO](/methods/targeted-eno.md) weights with the adaptive
cut-off of [adaptive TENO](/methods/adaptive-teno.md); in the fourth-order
limiter a TENO-style selection falls back from the cubics to the quadratics.
A five-point [effective scaled wavenumber](/definitions/scale-sensor.md)
triggers a linear compression towards the cell average at strong shocks, and a
[Zhang–Shu](/methods/zhang-shu-scaling-limiter.md) positivity limiter at flux
points is needed only for the double Mach reflection. Systems are limited in
[characteristic variables](/methods/characteristic-decomposition.md).

The [troubled-cell indicator](/definitions/troubled-cell-indicator.md) adapts
Fu's TENO indicator: it compares normalized
[smoothness indicators](/definitions/smoothness-indicator.md) of the SD
polynomials of three consecutive cells with a sharp cut-off and flags the $x$
and $y$ directions independently. Evidence is numerical:
[density-wave advection](/benchmarks/density-wave-advection.md) (design order
for all limiters, TENO-A errors about half those of WENO),
[Sod and Lax tubes](/benchmarks/sod-and-lax-shock-tubes.md), the
[Shu–Osher problem](/benchmarks/shu-osher-problem.md), the
[double Mach reflection](/benchmarks/double-mach-reflection.md), the
[triple point](/benchmarks/triple-point-problem.md), a
[2D Riemann problem](/benchmarks/two-dimensional-riemann-problems.md),
[Rayleigh–Taylor](/benchmarks/rayleigh-taylor-instability.md) and a
[shock–bubble interaction](/benchmarks/shock-bubble-interaction.md). TENO-A
is consistently sharper than the WENO limiter; the TENO indicator matches
KXRCF on shock tubes, catches the Shu–Osher shocklets that KXRCF misses and
flags fewer, better-placed cells in 2D.

# Key claims

Registry notation (see the notation map): the source's $N$ solution points per
direction are $N+1$ here ($N$ the polynomial degree), its $h$ is $\Delta x$,
its $w_k$ is $\omega_k$, its TENO-A $m$, $\eta_i$, $\alpha_{1,2}$ and exponent
$\beta$ are $\mu_d$, $\sigma_i$, $b_{1,2}$ and $b$.

1. The limiter is a direct convex combination of high-order Hermite polynomials, not a moment-by-moment limiting as in earlier compact HWENO limiters, which the authors state makes it more efficient at higher order (§1, §10, abstract). **Stated**
2. The limiter uses only nodal values of the target cell and its face neighbours and is applied dimension by dimension along lines of solution points (§1, §7, Fig. 2, Algorithm 1); earlier RKDG WENO/TENO limiters need 8 stencils, each containing at least one cell that shares no edge or face with the target cell (§7, Fig. 3). **Stated** (stencils shown by figure)
3. In 2D a modal representation of an order-$(N+1)$ method needs $(N+1)(N+2)/2$ degrees of freedom per cell and a nodal one $(N+1)^2$, so nodal methods need a tailored limiting strategy (§1). **Stated**
4. SD3 3-DoF linear weights: $d_0=8/31$, $d_1=15/31$, $d_2=8/31$ (Eq. (49)), from Eqs. (38), (43), (48). **Derived**
5. SD4 3-DoF linear weights: $d_0=1/4$, $d_1=1/2$, $d_2=1/4$ (Eq. (50)); other solution-point sets give other coefficients in Eqs. (30)–(35). **Derived**
6. SD4 4-DoF linear weights: $d_3=d_4=5/14$, $d_5=d_6=1/7$ (Eq. (54)), from the constraints of Eq. (53). **Derived**
7. The TENO troubled-cell indicator uses $q=6$, $\epsilon=10^{-4}$ (1D) and $10^{-3}$ (2D), cut-off $10^{-5}$ (1D) and $5\times10^{-4}$ (2D); a very small $\epsilon$ (e.g. $10^{-12}$) is "not good" because high-wavenumber noise of extremely low amplitude would be flagged (§4.2, Eqs. (16)–(20)). **Stated**
8. In 2D the larger $\epsilon$ is justified because a nodal tensor polynomial has $(N+1)^2$ basis functions and degree $2N$, so small-amplitude noise is more likely; a larger cut-off flags more cells and enhances stability (§4.2). **Stated**
9. TENO-A parameters: 3-DoF $C_r=0.5$, exponent of $\sigma_i$ equal to 1.0, $b_1=8$, $b_2=4$; 4-DoF $C_r=0.6$, exponent 2.0, $b_1=7$, $b_2=4$. In what the authors call "traditional scenarios" higher order lowers the bounds of $C_T$ and $C_r$; here the 4-DoF reconstruction is less robust and needs "an elevated upper bound" on $C_T$; a larger exponent helps stability (§5.5). **Stated** (from "numerical experiments", not shown)
10. Linear compression with cut-off $\vartheta_{\mathrm{ESW}}>0.6$ improves the stability of the WENO and TENO-A limiters at strong shocks; with the amplitude-dependent $\epsilon_{\mathrm s}$ the sensor stays below 1 over the whole wavenumber range when the local amplitude is at most $0.001$ (§5.6, Eqs. (66)–(69), citing Li, Chen & Ren 2022). **Stated**
11. All limiters leave the cell averages unchanged (Eq. (75), §8). **Stated** (follows from $\sum_kd_k=1$ and the cell-average constraint of every candidate)
12. Pure advection (2D Euler density wave, all cells forced troubled, no compression): all limiters reach design order, e.g. SD3+TENO3-A $L_1$ orders 3.03 and 3.08, SD4+TENO4-A 4.13 and 4.09, and the TENO-A error is "about half" that of WENO (Table 1, §9.1). **Shown**
13. Unlimited SD3 shows $L_1$ orders 2.68 and 2.89 on $20^2\to40^2\to80^2$, and SD3+TENO3-A has a smaller $L_1$ error on $80^2$ (1.99E-6) than unlimited SD3 (2.60E-6) (Table 1). **Shown** (not commented by the authors)
14. Lax and Sod tubes: SD3+TENO3-A with the TENO indicator is oscillation-free on 100, 200 and 400 cells; both indicators separate rarefaction, contact and shock; the share of troubled cells decreases with refinement; TENO-A gives sharper contacts and shocks than WENO; KXRCF and the TENO indicator give nearly identical results (§9.2, Figs. 5–8). **Shown**
15. Shu–Osher: the TENO indicator separates smooth scales from shocklets, while KXRCF detects only the strong initial shock; the TENO indicator marks slightly more cells in high-wavenumber regions without significant loss of accuracy; TENO-A beats WENO near local extrema and fourth order beats third order (§9.3, Figs. 9–10). **Shown**
16. Double Mach reflection ($800\times200$, $1600\times400$): TENO resolves more small-scale vortices than WENO; the TENO indicator marks discontinuities more precisely than KXRCF near complex structures; lowering $C_T$ reduces dissipation (Fig. 13); TENO-A improves on constant-$C_T$ TENO; TENO4-A resolves small scales better than TENO3-A (§9.4, Figs. 11–15). **Shown**
17. TENO3-A combined with SD4 degrades the solution relative to TENO3-A with SD3, so a fourth-order limiter is needed for high resolution with strong shocks and small scales (§9.4). **Stated** (from "our numerical experiments", no figure)
18. Only the double Mach reflection needs the positivity limiter (pressure only; no negative density observed); SD3 uses it only at the first step, SD4 in the first few steps; at $t=0$ in Fig. 11c, 0.59 % of cells are flagged in $x$, 0.43 % in $y$, 0.12 % have the positivity limiter active, with mean $\theta=0.96$ (§9, Fig. 4). **Shown**
19. The TENO indicator can leave cells crossed by a discontinuity unflagged in a direction when three consecutive cells are crossed with "completely the same discontinuous degree", so the TENO limiter alone cannot guarantee positive pressure (§9, Fig. 4). **Stated** (example in Fig. 4)
20. Triple point ($700\times300$): TENO-A resolves vortex roll-up much better than WENO at both orders; both indicators capture the interfaces well (§9.5, Figs. 16–18). **Shown**
21. 2D Riemann problem ($1200\times1200$): TENO-A resolves more vortices; the TENO indicator flags troubled cells "with low noise" while KXRCF flags many more, which the authors attribute to relative comparisons of local smoothness instead of absolute thresholds on inter-element jumps (§9.6, Figs. 19–21). **Shown** (attribution **Stated**)
22. Rayleigh–Taylor ($128\times512$): TENO-A gives richer small-scale structures than WENO at both orders (§9.7, Fig. 22). **Shown**
23. Shock–bubble ($1300\times356$): TENO3-A resolves more than WENO3, but TENO4-A does not improve significantly on third order; the TENO indicator marks only the fronts and tails of interfaces and leaves the high-wavenumber centre unmarked, which the authors say holds for other cases too (§9.8, Figs. 23–25). **Shown**
24. The TENO indicator "slightly outperforms" KXRCF when strong discontinuities and small scales coexist and "exhibits enhanced robustness" (abstract, §10). **Shown** (qualitative, by figures)
25. The SD method is stable without filtering or de-aliasing owing to its staggered flux points (§1, citing earlier work). **Stated**

# Printed slips

- Eq. (3) and text call the solution points "the Gauss points", but
  $X_s=-\tfrac12\cos\big(\tfrac{2s-1}{2N}\pi\big)$ gives Chebyshev–Gauss points:
  for three points $X_s=0,\pm\sqrt3/4\approx\pm0.433$, whereas the
  Gauss–Legendre points on $[-1/2,1/2]$ are $0,\pm\sqrt{15}/10\approx\pm0.387$.
  The printed linear weights correspond to the printed formula. *(compiler
  check, not in the sources.)*
- Eq. (67): the input set of $\kappa_{i-1/2}$ has four entries for five slots,
  "$\{Q^+_{i-3/2},\overline Q_{i-1/2},Q_{i+1/2},Q_{i+1}\}$". *(Compiler check,
  not in the sources: the mirror image of the printed $\kappa_{i+1/2}$ set
  $\{\overline Q_{i-1/2},Q_i,\overline Q_{i+1/2},Q_{i+1},Q^-_{i+3/2}\}$ is
  $\{Q^+_{i-3/2},Q_{i-1},\overline Q_{i-1/2},Q_i,\overline Q_{i+1/2}\}$.)*
  $Q_i$ is not defined in §5.6; in Eq. (64) it is the exact value
  $\mathcal Q(x_i)$, which cannot be what is computed.
- Eq. (83): $\theta_2=\min\{p(\overline{\mathbf Q}_{i,j})/(p(\overline{\mathbf Q}_{i,j})-p^{\mathrm{FPs}}_{\min})\}$
  is a minimum over one argument (compare Eq. (80), $\min\{\cdot,1\}$), and no
  pressure floor is included. *(Compiler check, not in the sources: the limiter
  is applied only when some flux-point pressure is negative, and there the
  ratio is below 1, so the missing bound 1 does not change the result.)*
- Eq. (82) carries $\mathbf Q^h_{i,j}$ on the right while the text says it acts
  on the density-limited $\hat{\mathbf Q}^h_{i,j}$; Eq. (80) writes the density
  floor $\epsilon_\rho$, the text $\varepsilon_\rho$.
- §5.7, fourth-order algorithm: "Compute smoothness indicators according to
  Eq. (57)"; Eq. (57) defines $\gamma_k$, the indicators are Eq. (56).
- $\overline Q^{(2)}_{i\pm1}$ is required by the cubics $p_5$, $p_6$ (Eq. (51))
  but neither its Taylor expansion (Eqs. (30)–(35) stop at first derivatives)
  nor its characteristic projection (Eq. (71)) is printed.
- §5.4.1 sets $N_{st}=7$ stencils for WENO4 but does not say how the WENO
  weights combine three quadratics and four cubics; the fourth-order rule of
  §5.7 relies on the TENO flags $\delta_k$.
- Ref. [75] lists "C.-W. Shu" twice among its authors.

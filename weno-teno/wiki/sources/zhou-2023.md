# Summary

The paper builds a six-point explicit
[weighted compact nonlinear scheme (WCNS)](/methods/weighted-compact-nonlinear-scheme.md)
for single- and two-species compressible Euler flows. Its linear part is a
six-point midpoint interpolation with two free parameters, one for dispersion
and one for dissipation, obtained by freeing the end coefficients of the
sixth-order central interpolant (so the interpolation is fourth order). Its
Fourier symbol is that of the [MDCD schemes](/methods/mdcd-scheme.md), with
$c_{\mathrm{disp}}$ and $c_{\mathrm{diss}}$ (see the notation map). Interface
HLLC fluxes are differenced to the nodes by Deng et al.'s sixth-order explicit
midpoint-and-node-to-node formula. The interpolation is split into four
three-point candidates, one of them downwind, combined with Wong & Lele's
weights $\alpha_k=d_k\big(C+(\tau_6/(\beta_k+\epsilon))^q\big)$ built on the
[WENO-CU6](/methods/weno-cu6.md) full-stencil indicator.

The dispersion parameter is optimized in two steps. It is first set by
minimizing Sun et al.'s weighted integrated dispersion error, not of the
linear scheme but of the full nonlinear scheme, through Pirozzoli's
[approximate dispersion relation](/definitions/approximate-dispersion-relation.md).
The optimum depends on $c_{\mathrm{diss}}$ for small $C$ and stops depending on
it near $C=10^9$, where the nonlinear scheme resolves up to $\vartheta\approx1.6$
against about 1.25 for WCNS-LD and ONL6. Then $C$ and $c_{\mathrm{diss}}$ are
switched pointwise by Li et al.'s second-derivative [scale sensor](/definitions/scale-sensor.md):
large $C$ and almost no linear dissipation at low wavenumbers, small $C$ and
growing dissipation at high wavenumbers (WCNS-SA). The design target is an
"idealized" [dissipation](/definitions/numerical-dissipation.md) curve, zero
below a critical wavenumber and rising steeply above it. Two species are
handled by Abgrall & Karni's double-flux algorithm on the
[multicomponent Euler equations](/definitions/multicomponent-euler-equations.md).

Eleven tests (Sod, Lax, [Shu–Osher](/benchmarks/shu-osher-problem.md),
[Woodward–Colella](/benchmarks/woodward-colella-blast-wave.md), two-species Sod,
[double Mach reflection](/benchmarks/double-mach-reflection.md), a
[2D Riemann problem](/benchmarks/two-dimensional-riemann-problems.md),
[Rayleigh–Taylor](/benchmarks/rayleigh-taylor-instability.md),
[shock–bubble](/benchmarks/shock-bubble-interaction.md) and inviscid, viscous
and supersonic [Taylor–Green](/benchmarks/taylor-green-vortex.md) vortices)
compare WCNS-SA with WCNS-LD (Wong & Lele 2017) and ONL6 (Jin et al. 2018) on
the same stencil. WCNS-SA gives sharper discontinuities and more small-scale
structure at 3% extra cost. The evidence is numerical; there is no convergence
test.

# Key claims

Registry notation (see the notation map): the source's dispersion and
dissipation parameters $\eta$, $\xi$ are the page-local $\eta_{\mathrm Z}$ and
$\xi_{\mathrm Z}$ of the [WCNS](/methods/weighted-compact-nonlinear-scheme.md) and
[MDCD](/methods/mdcd-scheme.md) pages, with $\eta_{\mathrm Z}=\tfrac{45}{64}c_{\mathrm{disp}}$
and $\xi_{\mathrm Z}=\tfrac{45}{64}c_{\mathrm{diss}}$; values are quoted as printed.
The sixth-order central scheme is $\eta_{\mathrm Z}=3/128$, $\xi_{\mathrm Z}=0$; the
fifth-order upwind scheme has both equal to $3/128$. The reduced wavenumber is
$\vartheta$, the sensor of Li et al. is $\theta^{\mathrm L}$, the exponent of the
error weight is $\nu_{\mathrm w}$.

1. The nonlinear WCNS optimized with the ADR "can completely remedy the dispersion property deterioration caused by nonlinear mechanism": with the dispersion parameter optimized at $C=10^9$, the truncated wavenumber of the nonlinear scheme is "approximately 1.6, which is nearly identical to that of the corresponding linear scheme" (§4.1, Figs. 4b, 5; Conclusion). **Shown** (ADR computation)
2. WCNS-LD and ONL6 depart from the spectral dispersion curve at about $\vartheta=1.25$, the optimized WCNS only at $\vartheta=1.6$ (§4.1, Fig. 5). **Shown**
3. For the linear six-point scheme, $\tilde\vartheta_r$ depends only on $c_{\mathrm{disp}}$ and $\tilde\vartheta_i$ only on $c_{\mathrm{diss}}$ (Eq. 24), so "the dispersion and dissipation properties of a linear scheme can be optimized independently" (§3). **Derived** *(compiler check, not in the sources: with $c_{\mathrm{disp}}=\tfrac{64}{45}\eta$ and $c_{\mathrm{diss}}=\tfrac{64}{45}\xi$, Eq. (24) is $\tilde\vartheta_r=c_{\mathrm{disp}}\sin3\vartheta-(4c_{\mathrm{disp}}+\tfrac16)\sin2\vartheta+(5c_{\mathrm{disp}}+\tfrac43)\sin\vartheta$ and $\tilde\vartheta_i=4c_{\mathrm{diss}}(\cos\vartheta-1)^3$, the modified wavenumber of the MDCD scheme.)*
4. For the nonlinear scheme, dispersion "mainly relies on" the dispersion parameter and the effect of the dissipation parameter is "negligible"; dissipation depends mainly on $c_{\mathrm{diss}}$ and $C$ (§4, relayed from Sun et al.). **Stated**
5. Optimal dispersion parameter of the linear scheme for $\nu_{\mathrm w}=4,5,6,7,8$: $\eta_{\mathrm Z}=0.0502,0.0432,0.0384,0.0350,0.0326$, with peak dispersion errors 3.72%, 1.99%, 1.08%, 0.60%, 0.34% and resolving efficiencies $\vartheta_c/\pi=$ 60.86%, 54.95%, 49.66%, 45.03%, 41.03% (Table 1). **Derived**
6. The dispersion parameter optimized with the ADR of the nonlinear scheme differs from the linear value and depends on $(C,c_{\mathrm{diss}})$. With $\xi_{\mathrm Z}=0$ and $3/128$ respectively, $\eta_{\mathrm Z}$ is $0.0675$, $0.0539$ at $C=10$; $0.0489$, $0.0429$ at $10^3$; $0.0394$, $0.0373$ at $10^5$; $0.0383$, $0.0377$ at $10^7$; $0.0385$, $0.0385$ at $10^9$; $0.0384$, $0.0384$ at $10^{11}$ (Table 2). Near $C=10^9$ the optimum is "nearly regardless" of $c_{\mathrm{diss}}$, so it is set at $C=10^9$ (§4.1). **Shown** ($\nu_{\mathrm w}$ and $q$ of Table 2 are not printed)
7. Larger $C$ lowers the dissipation at all wavenumbers and moves the dissipation curve to higher wavenumbers; for each $C$ the dissipation grows non-monotonically with $\vartheta$, which the paper attributes to the first-derivative term of the smoothness indicator (§4.2, Fig. 6a). **Shown**
8. Larger $c_{\mathrm{diss}}$ raises the dissipation everywhere; $c_{\mathrm{diss}}=0$ (sixth-order central) has zero linear dissipation, the fifth-order upwind value much more (§3, Fig. 3; §4.2, Fig. 6b). **Shown**
9. "A fixed $C$ or $\xi$ (even though it takes an optimized value) cannot produce satisfactory dissipation property at the whole wavenumber range" (§4.2, Conclusion). **Shown** (spectral curves only)
10. The ideal dissipation curve for compressible turbulence and aeroacoustics is zero for $\vartheta<\vartheta_c$ and rises quickly at $\vartheta_c$; a slow rise beyond $\vartheta_c$ "would not attain adequate dissipation at higher wavenumbers, causing numerical instability" (§4.2, Fig. 7a). **Stated**
11. A scheme optimized under Hu et al.'s dispersion–dissipation condition (parameter 10) "has insufficient dissipation at the high-wavenumber region and thus exhibits weak shock-capturing robustness"; the condition "needs to be improved for actual simulation, although it is physically reasonable" (§4.2–4.3, Fig. 7b). **Shown** (spectral curve only; no simulation with that scheme)
12. Of three strategies (adapt $c_{\mathrm{diss}}$ at fixed $C$, adapt $C$ at fixed $c_{\mathrm{diss}}$, adapt both), adapting both "exhibits the best dissipation property" (§4.3). **Stated** (no figure for the first two)
13. The dissipation of WCNS-SA grows almost monotonically for $\vartheta>1.9$, whereas that of WCNS-LD "decreases evidently" for $\vartheta>2.4$, so WCNS-SA "has stronger shock-capturing robustness"; WCNS-SA is less dissipative than ONL6 at medium wavenumbers (§4.3, Fig. 7b). **Shown** (ADR)
14. Li et al.'s sensor increases monotonically from 0 to 1 with the wavenumber and does not depend on the phase of a single sinusoid (§4.3, citing Li et al.). **Stated** *(compiler check, not in the sources: for $u_j=\cos(\vartheta j)$, Eqs. (29)–(30) give $\theta^{\mathrm L}=\sin^2(\vartheta/2)/(1+\cos^2(\vartheta/2))$, so the thresholds $r_c=0.5$ and $r_\xi=0.71$ correspond to $\vartheta=1.911$ and $\vartheta=2.293$.)*
15. Interpolation-based schemes (WCNS) admit both flux-vector and flux-difference splitting, "while reconstruction-based schemes can only take the flux vector splitting technique"; in curvilinear coordinates flux-vector splitting "contains grid metrics and cannot be split into two upwind parts, violating the geometric conservation law" (§1). **Stated**
16. "Traditional WCNS may yield negative density or pressure during the nonlinear interpolation process, and thus shows less robustness than WENO"; a first-order interpolation is used wherever the interpolated density or pressure could be negative (§2.2). **Stated**
17. For difference schemes of fifth or higher order the interface-to-node formula has little influence on resolution, and the spectral properties of WCNS are "mainly determined by the interpolation scheme rather than the difference scheme"; hence only the interpolation is optimized (§2.2, §3). **Stated**
18. WCNS "preserves the advantages of both WENO (strong shock-capturing robustness) and central compact schemes (low dissipation)" but "is still too dissipative to resolve full-scale turbulence" (§1). **Stated**
19. Sod (100 points) and two-species Sod (200 points): WCNS-SA gives the sharpest shock and contact or material interface, measured by the thickness $\Delta\rho/\max_j\lvert\rho_{j+1}-\rho_j\rvert$ (§5.1, §5.4, Figs. 9a, 14). **Shown**
20. Lax problem: WCNS-LD overshoots at the contact; WCNS-SA gives the sharpest contact "but a slightly thicker shock wave" (§5.1, Figs. 9b, 10). **Shown**
21. Velocity overshoots at the tail of the rarefaction appear with every scheme, also with fifth-order WENO, and are "caused by numerical errors rather than instabilities" (§5.1). **Stated**
22. Shu–Osher (200 points): ONL6 damps the post-shock high-wavenumber waves "due to the ignorance of nonlinear mechanism during the scheme optimization"; WCNS-LD overshoots near the shocklets; WCNS-SA is best (§5.2, Fig. 11). **Shown**
23. 2D Riemann problem: WCNS-LD and WCNS-SA capture more vortices than ONL6 "but meanwhile they produce more noises, especially behind the shock wave", attributed to their larger $q$ and $C$ (§5.6, Fig. 16). **Shown**
24. Double Mach reflection on $960\times240$: WCNS-SA resolves the slip-line vortices with clearer cores, "similar to" a hybrid weighted compact scheme on $1920\times480$ (§5.5, Fig. 15). **Shown** (cross-paper visual comparison)
25. Inviscid Taylor–Green: WCNS-SA on $64^3$ keeps the kinetic energy better than ONL6 and WCNS-LD on $128^3$; on $128^3$ it is "nearly identical" to TENO8 on the same mesh, and it best matches the $-5/3$ spectrum at $t=8,10$ (§5.9, Figs. 21–23). **Shown**
26. Viscous Taylor–Green: only WCNS-SA matches Brachet's DNS dissipation rate at $\mathrm{Re}=100$ on $32^3$ and at $\mathrm{Re}=200,400$ on $64^3$; at $\mathrm{Re}=1600$ it matches the DNS before $t=8$ (§5.9, Fig. 24). **Shown**
27. Supersonic Taylor–Green ($M=1.25$, $\mathrm{Re}=1600$, $128^3$): all schemes underestimate the total viscous dissipation; WCNS-SA and WCNS-LD do better than ONL6 (§5.9, Fig. 25). **Shown**
28. Runtime of 1000 steps of the supersonic Taylor–Green vortex on 32 MPI cores (Intel Xeon Gold 6154): ONL6 4592 s, WCNS-LD 4630 s, WCNS-SA 4713 s, ratios 1.00, 1.01, 1.03 (Table 4). **Shown**

# Printed slips

- The Table 1 caption calls $\vartheta_c$ "the critical wavenumber at which $\tau(\varphi_c)=-\tau_p$", the text of §3 calls it the "truncated wavenumber" with $\lvert\tau(\varphi_c)\rvert=\tau_p$: two names for one quantity.
- §4.3: "$r_c$ is a threshold for the onset of dispersion attenuation"; $r_c$ switches $C$ and the dissipation parameter, and the parallel sentence for $r_\xi$ speaks of "dissipation attenuation".
- The exponent $q$ of Eq. (14) is never given a value, although §5.6 attributes noise to "the higher values of parameters $q$ and $C$". The weight exponent $\nu_{\mathrm w}$ of the optimized scheme and of Table 2 is not printed (Table 2's large-$C$ limit 0.0384 is the Table 1 value at $\nu_{\mathrm w}=6$). The variable fed to the sensor is not stated.
- Eq. (28) reuses $r$, the stencil half-width elsewhere, as the parameter of the dispersion–dissipation condition.
- Ref. [8] (Tsoutsanis et al., J. Sci. Comput. 89:64) is printed with year 2023; the same work is the bundle's `tsoutsanis-2021`.
- "weighing function" (§2.3), "wavenumbes" (§4.3), "DNS date" (§5.9).

# Summary

Zuo & Zhu (2024b) build finite-difference hybrid multi-resolution WENO schemes (HMR-WENO) of orders
5, 7 and 9 in one to three dimensions. Smooth cells use "high-order simple linear upwind schemes" of
the same order (not written out). Troubled cells use the
[multi-resolution WENO](/methods/multi-resolution-weno.md) of Zhu & Shu on nested central stencils
with [WENO-Z](/methods/weno-z.md)-type weights. Like
[Zuo & Zhu (2024a)](/sources/zuo-2024.md), the paper is a [hybrid scheme](/methods/hybrid-weno.md)
driven by a parameter-free [troubled-cell indicator](/definitions/troubled-cell-indicator.md), but
the indicator now locates the extrema exactly instead of bounding the derivative.

The nodal density on the big stencil of $n_{\mathrm g}+1$ points is interpolated by a polynomial $\mathsf g$ of
degree $n_{\mathrm g}$ (the source's $2r$). A cell is troubled if $\mathsf g$ has an extremum inside the smallest interval
$[x_{i-1/2},x_{i+1/2}]$. To find all zeros of $\mathsf g'$ there, a **hierarchical bisection** walks
down the derivative chain. The linear $\mathsf g^{(n_{\mathrm g}-1)}$ has at most one zero in the cell, found
by a sign test and bisection. It splits the cell into pieces on which $\mathsf g^{(n_{\mathrm g}-2)}$ is
monotonic, so a sign test at the ends of each piece decides whether that piece holds a zero. The
step repeats down to $\mathsf g'$, whose sign changes are counted. Earlier extremum indicators worked
on $[x_{i-5/2},x_{i+5/2}]$ with closed-form roots of a cubic (Zhu & Qiu; Zhao et al.) or on
$[x_{i-3/2},x_{i+3/2}]$ (Wang, Tian, Zhu & Zhao 2023). The authors say these stopped at fifth order and were less
precise.

The evidence is numerical:
[density-wave advection](/benchmarks/density-wave-advection.md) in 1D to 3D at the design orders,
[Woodward–Colella blast waves](/benchmarks/woodward-colella-blast-wave.md),
[double Mach reflection](/benchmarks/double-mach-reflection.md), the
[Mach 3 forward-facing step](/benchmarks/forward-facing-step.md) and the inviscid
[Taylor–Green vortex](/benchmarks/taylor-green-vortex.md). Resolution is equal to or better than
MR-WENO at CPU-time ratios HMR/MR of 0.25–0.78. No theorem is proved.

# Key claims

Registry notation: the source's $h$ is $\Delta x$; its velocities $\mu,\nu,w$ (and $\omega_0$ in
Eq. 3.5) are $v_1,v_2,v_3$; its linear weights $\gamma_{\ell,\tilde\ell}$ are $d_{\ell,\tilde\ell}$;
its $\bar\omega$ is $\alpha$; its $\tau_{\ell_2}$ is $\tau_{2\ell_2+1}$; its indicator polynomial
$g$ is $\mathsf g$ (page-local).

1. HMR-WENO saves "about 22%-75% CPU time" against MR-WENO of the same order; the ratios HMR/MR are 0.36, 0.45, 0.78 (blast waves, orders 5/7/9), 0.32, 0.35, 0.44 (double Mach), 0.44, 0.44, 0.71 (forward step) and 0.27, 0.25, 0.29 (Taylor–Green) (Abstract, §3, Table 3.1; Lenovo XiaoXinAir 14 IAP8, Visual Studio 2017). **Shown** *(compiler check, not in the sources: the ratios of the printed times are 0.3588 to 0.7751, consistent with the rounding; the savings span 22.5–74.6%.)*
2. The indicator contains no manual or problem-dependent parameter (Abstract, §1, §4). **Stated** (by construction, a sign test)
3. The hierarchical bisection finds all zeros of the derivatives of an arbitrarily high-degree interpolant inside $[x_{i-1/2},x_{i+1/2}]$, hence all its extrema there: on a piece where $\mathsf g^{(l)}$ is monotonic it has at most one zero, detected by an endpoint sign change, and the zeros of $\mathsf g^{(l+1)}$ split the cell into such pieces (§1, §2.2 Steps 2.1–2.4, Remark 2, Algorithm 1). **Derived** (no tolerance or iteration count for the bisection, and no rule for a product exactly zero, is given)
4. The authors claim this is "the first time" monotonicity intervals of an arbitrary-degree polynomial are located by a hierarchical bisection (§1). **Stated**
5. A cell is troubled iff $\mathsf g$ has at least one extremum in $[x_{i-1/2},x_{i+1/2}]$ (icount $>0$); Flag $=1$ selects MR-WENO, Flag $=0$ the linear upwind scheme (Eq. 2.19). **Stated** (definition) *(compiler check, not in the sources: a cell holding a smooth extremum of the density is therefore always troubled, and a jump across which the interpolant stays monotonic in the cell is not flagged there; detection relies on the oscillations of the interpolant near the jump.)*
6. The earlier extremum indicators search $[x_{i-5/2},x_{i+5/2}]$ with closed-form roots, which for quintic or higher-degree polynomials is "not only time-consuming but also unavailable" (§1, Remark 1). **Stated**
7. Restricting detection to $[x_{i-1/2},x_{i+1/2}]$ makes it "more precise"; the wider intervals "could bring about an inaccurate identification of the troubled cells" (§1, Remark 1, §4). **Stated** (no comparison with the earlier indicators is shown)
8. The indicator extends to seventh order (sextic $\mathsf g$, derivatives up to the fifth) and ninth order (octic, up to the seventh), and to 2D and 3D dimension by dimension (Remark 3). **Stated**; seventh- and ninth-order schemes **Shown** in §3
9. HMR-WENO5/7/9 reach their design orders on the 1D density wave ($t=2$, $N_x=100$–150) (Table 3.2). **Shown**
10. In 1D, HMR-WENO5 has the $L_1$ errors of MR-WENO5 but smaller $L_\infty$ errors ($N_x=100$: $1.81\times10^{-8}$ against $4.13\times10^{-8}$); at order 7 the MR-WENO and HMR-WENO errors coincide except for three entries, at order 9 everywhere (Table 3.2) *(compiler check, not in the sources: entry-by-entry comparison)*. **Shown**
11. 2D density wave ($10^2$ to $60^2$): HMR-WENO5 orders 4.89–5.00 against erratic MR-WENO5 orders (2.52, 7.58, 5.98, 8.66, 5.55 in $L_1$), with smaller errors on coarse grids ($20^2$: $6.57\times10^{-5}$ against $8.23\times10^{-4}$) (Table 3.3). **Shown**
12. 3D density wave: the same pattern, MR-WENO5 $L_1$ orders 4.73, 5.69, 7.60, 5.00, 4.98 against 4.88–4.99; at ninth order the two coincide except at one entry, $L_\infty$ at $10^3$ (Table 3.4) *(compiler check, not in the sources: entry-by-entry comparison)*. **Shown**
13. HMR-WENO is "more efficient", with less CPU time for a given error on the smooth tests (Figs. 3.1–3.3). **Shown** (figures only)
14. Blast waves (800 points, reference WENO-JS5 on 20000): "the HMR-WENO schemes are adept at identifying the troubled cells" (Example 3.4, Figs. 3.4–3.5). **Shown**
15. Double Mach reflection ($1200\times400$): "more sophisticated flow structures" near the Mach stems and "higher resolutions of the density" than MR-WENO (Example 3.5, Figs. 3.6–3.8). **Shown** (qualitative)
16. Mach 3 forward step ($600\times200$, $t=4$): HMR-WENO "outperform" MR-WENO, "especially for addressing the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability and roll-up of the contact line" (Example 3.6). **Shown** (qualitative)
17. Inviscid Taylor–Green vortex ($128^3$): the mean kinetic energy stays closer to its initial 0.125 than with MR-WENO, "substantially lower dissipations" (Example 3.7, Figs. 3.11–3.12). **Shown** *(compiler check: the printed initial velocity with $L=V_0=\rho_0=1$ has mean kinetic energy exactly $1/8$.)*
18. HMR-WENO "combine the robustness, shock-capture ability, high-efficiency, and high-resolution" of MR-WENO and of linear upwind schemes (Remark 4). **Stated**
19. Unstructured grids are ongoing work (§4). **Stated**
20. MR-WENO uses the same big stencils as WENO-JS of the same order (§2.1). **Stated**

# Printed slips

- Abstract and §4 list "one zeroth degree reconstruction polynomial" among those used for the indicators; the indicator of §2.2 uses only the degree-$n_{\mathrm g}$ interpolant.
- Step 2.1 applies "the new hierarchical bisection method" to the zero of the linear $\mathsf g^{(n_{\mathrm g}-1)}$, which is explicit.
- "octave reconstruction polynomial" (Abstract, Remark 3, §4) for the degree-8 polynomial; "Broges et al. [3]" for Borges; "CUP time" (twice, §3); "the gird points" (Example 3.4); "Adams et al. [1]" for the two-author Adams & Shariff.
- Eq. (3.5): $p_0$ is both the initial pressure field and the constant 100 on its right-hand side, and the $z$-velocity is written $\omega_0$ (it is $w$ in Example 3.3; $\omega$ is the nonlinear weight in Eq. 2.12).

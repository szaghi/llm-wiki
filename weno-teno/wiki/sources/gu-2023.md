# Summary

Gu et al. treat the remapping step of indirect arbitrary
Lagrangian–Eulerian (ALE) methods: the cell averages of the conserved
quantities are transferred from an old, distorted Lagrangian mesh to a new,
rezoned mesh with the same cells and connectivity
([conservative remapping](/methods/conservative-remapping.md)). They do not
intersect old and new cells, and they do not advect over a fixed
pseudo-time. Instead they solve $\partial_tu=0$ on a mesh whose nodes move
on straight lines from the old position at $t=0$ to the new one at a
pseudo-time $t=T_{\mathrm r}$. In integral form, by the Reynolds transport
theorem, this is a linear conservation law with flux $-v_{\mathrm g}u$,
$v_{\mathrm g}$ being the mesh velocity, constant at each node.

The discretization is a third-order finite-volume scheme on quadrilaterals.
It has an upwind flux at three Gauss–Lobatto points per edge, the
third-order [multi-resolution WENO](/methods/multi-resolution-weno.md)
reconstruction of Zhu & Shu (a constant on the cell and a constrained
least-squares quadratic on the $3\times3$ block, with
[WENO-Z](/methods/weno-z.md)-type weights and linear weights $1/11$,
$10/11$), and SSP-RK3 with time-dependent cell areas. The linear weights do
not depend on the cell geometry, so they need not be recomputed as the mesh
moves. Following the moving-mesh analysis of Klingenberg et al. and Li et
al., high order needs only bounded, Lipschitz mesh velocities, which the
choice of $T_{\mathrm r}$ provides. Positivity of density and internal
energy ([bound preservation](/properties/bound-preservation.md)) comes from
the Zhang–Shu framework: a cell-average decomposition on the $3\times3$
Gauss–Lobatto points of the mapped cell, a time-step bound (Theorem 2.2),
and the [linear scaling limiter](/methods/zhang-shu-scaling-limiter.md). In
practice the code runs at a CFL step and halves it when a cell average
leaves the admissible set.

On smoothly moving, randomly moving and "flipping" meshes (nodes moving
more than one cell) the observed orders are about 3 to 3.8. The remap is
non-oscillatory, the limiter removes every negative cell average, and for
mild mesh changes each remap takes 3 pseudo-time steps under refinement.
ALE runs with the third-order positivity-preserving ENO Lagrangian scheme
of Cheng & Shu are robust on the [Sedov](/benchmarks/sedov-blast-wave.md),
Saltzman, [Noh](/benchmarks/noh-implosion-problem.md),
[Leblanc](/benchmarks/leblanc-shock-tube.md) and two-material
[Sod](/benchmarks/sod-and-lax-shock-tubes.md) problems.

# Key claims

Registry notation: cell $I_{i,j}$, cell average $\bar u_{i,j}$, linear
weights $d_l$, smoothness indicators $\beta_l$, nonlinear weights $\omega_l$
(stencils numbered $l=1,2$ as in the source), regularization $\epsilon$,
Zhang–Shu factor $\theta$, admissible set $G$, conserved state
$u=(\rho,\rho v,E)$. Page-local symbols: $v_{\mathrm g}$ (mesh velocity),
$T_{\mathrm r}$ (final pseudo-time), $\lvert I_{i,j}\rvert$ (cell area),
$\lvert e_k\rvert$ and $n_k$ (length and outward normal of edge $k$), $C_0$ and
$C_{0,1}$ (bound and Lipschitz constant of $v_{\mathrm g}$), $h$ (minimum
inscribed-circle diameter) and $\mathsf w_q$ (Gauss–Lobatto weights
$1/6,2/3,1/6$).

1. Remapping is recast as the solution of $\partial_tu=0$ on a mesh moving from the old mesh at $t=0$ to the new one at $t=T_{\mathrm r}$. This needs no cell intersections and puts no limit on the size of the node displacement, only the same mesh topology (§1, §2, Eqs. (2.1)–(2.2)). **Derived**
2. The method applies to any polygonal mesh with matching topology, such as triangles, though only quadrilaterals are shown (§2.1). **Stated**
3. High order requires only a bounded mesh velocity, $\lvert v_{\mathrm g,1}\rvert,\lvert v_{\mathrm g,2}\rvert\le C_0$, with bounded first derivatives, $\le C_{0,1}$, citing [13, 14] (Klingenberg et al.). Both can always be met by taking $T_{\mathrm r}$ large enough (§2.2). **Stated** (from [13, 14, 19], not proved here)
4. $C_0=C_{0,1}=10$ in the experiments (§2.2). **Stated**
5. The edges use Gauss–Lobatto rather than Gauss quadrature (three points for third order), because it eases the positivity-preserving decomposition (§2.3). **Stated**
6. The multi-resolution WENO linear weights can be any positive numbers that sum to one, so they need not be recomputed as the cells change shape, which makes the reconstruction "particularly suitable and efficient for the remapping problem" (§1, §2.4). **Stated**
7. The indicator (2.6) would give $\beta_1=0$ for the constant on the cell. Following [37], $\beta_1$ is instead the minimum of the sums of squared differences to two adjacent neighbours, because $\beta_1=0$ "does lead to more smearing for problems containing strong shocks or contact discontinuities" (§2.4, Step 3). **Stated** (from [37])
8. **Theorem 2.1:** the first-order scheme (2.9) is positivity-preserving under $\Delta t^n\le\frac12\min_{i,j}\Big[\lvert I^n_{i,j}\rvert\big/\big(\sum_{k=1}^4\lvert e_k\rvert\big)\Big/\max_k\lvert v_{\mathrm g}^{(k)}\cdot n_k\rvert\Big]$, with $v_{\mathrm g}^{(k)}$ the mesh velocity at the edge midpoint (§2.6.1). **Stated** (proof referred to [7, 35])
9. **Theorem 2.2:** if $\bar u^n_{i,j}\in G$ and the nine point values of the reconstruction lie in $G$, the forward-Euler high-order scheme (2.10) is positivity-preserving under (2.12), $\Delta t\le\frac{\mathsf w_1}2\cdot\frac12\min_{i,j}\big\{\lvert J\rvert_{i,j}\big/\sum_k\lvert e_k\rvert\big/\max_{k,m_1}\lvert v_{\mathrm g}^{(k,m_1)}\cdot n_k\rvert\big\}$, with $\mathsf w_1=1/6$ and $\lvert J\rvert_{i,j}$ the minimum Jacobian of the map to $[-\frac12,\frac12]^2$ over the boundary points (§2.6.2). **Derived** (the decomposition (2.11) is shown; the detailed proof is referred to [7, 35])
10. SSP-RK3 (2.7) preserves positivity because its stages are convex combinations of forward-Euler steps and $G$ is convex (§2.6.2). **Stated**
11. In practice the scheme runs at the CFL step (2.8), $\Delta t=\nu h/a$ with $\nu=0.8$ and $a$ the largest normal mesh speed at the edge points, which is "more relaxed" than (2.12). When a new cell average leaves $G$, the step is redone with $\Delta t/2$, and Theorem 2.2 guarantees finitely many retries (Remark, §2.6.2). **Stated**
12. A step from (2.8) larger than $5h$ is capped at $5h$ for temporal accuracy, following [19] (§2.5). **Stated**
13. The linear scaling limiter keeps the cell average, conservation and accuracy (§2.6.3, proof in [34]). **Stated**
14. Smoothly moving meshes, $u=\sin^2(2\pi x)\sin^2(2\pi y)$, 10 remaps on $20^2$ to $320^2$: the WENO $L^1$ error falls from $3.403\times10^{-2}$ to $5.225\times10^{-6}$, with orders 2.67, 3.63, 2.99 and 3.37; the $L^\infty$ orders are 1.54, 3.79, 3.79 and 3.80. P-WENO is nearly identical. WENO produces negative cell averages (0.03 % of cells at $40^2$) and P-WENO none (Table 3.1). **Shown**
15. Randomly moving meshes ($C_r=0.5$): WENO $L^1$ orders 3.59, 4.20, 3.46 and 3.73, with $L^1$ error $6.172\times10^{-7}$ at $320^2$. The share of negative cells falls from 2.86 % at $20^2$ to 0.03 % at $320^2$; P-WENO has none (Table 3.2). **Shown**
16. Flipping mesh (cell sizes reversed, nodes moving more than one cell): WENO $L^1$ orders 2.48, 3.44, 3.08 and 3.14, with up to 5.67 % negative cells; P-WENO orders 2.54, 3.42, 3.05 and 3.13 with none. The method keeps "expected third order accuracy … when the old and new meshes are not close" (Table 3.3, §3.1). **Shown**
17. A discontinuous test (states 10 and 0.1 and a smooth sinusoidal region, $80^2$, 10 remaps on random meshes) shows no oscillations near the discontinuities for WENO or P-WENO (§3.2, Fig. 6). **Shown**
18. Step function (3.2) on $80^2$: WENO gives 13 negative cell averages along $j=40$, the smallest $-3.83\times10^{-4}$ at the 51st cell; P-WENO none (§3.3.1, Figs. 7–8). **Shown**
19. Cylinder-plus-cone profile (3.3) on $80^2$: WENO gives 19 negative cell averages along the diagonal, the smallest $-1.88\times10^{-3}$ at $i=45$; on the flipping mesh at $160^2$, 35 negative cells, the smallest at the 92nd cell. P-WENO stays positive in both (§3.3.2, Figs. 9–12). **Shown**
20. Cost: for the mild mesh (3.4) each remap takes 3 pseudo-time steps whatever the resolution (6 steps for 2 remaps on every mesh from $20^2$ to $320^2$), because $T_{\mathrm r}$ and the step (2.8) both halve under refinement. The cost scales with $N_x\times N_y$: WENO 0.641 s ($20^2$) to 69.470 s ($320^2$), P-WENO 0.641 s to 70.078 s (Table 3.4, §3.4; hardware not stated). **Shown**
21. This contrasts with "most existing transport equation based remapping methods in which the final pseudo-time is fixed as a constant such as 1" (§3.4). **Stated**
22. In an indirect ALE method with the third-order positivity-preserving ENO Lagrangian scheme of [7], rezoning and remapping every 20 steps, P-WENO remapping gives robust results on Sedov ($t=1$), Saltzman (to $t=0.925$; the pure Lagrangian scheme fails at a later time than $t=0.6$), Noh ($t=0.6$; the pure Lagrangian run fails after $t=0.54$), Leblanc ($t=6$) and the two-material Sod problem ($t=0.2$) (§3.5, Figs. 13–20). **Shown**
23. In the Noh ALE run the positivity limiter of the remap is needed: without it, negative internal energy and pressure stop the code. The limited cells are marked in Fig. 17 (§3.5.3). **Shown** (no run without the limiter is displayed)

# Printed slips

- Fig. 3 caption: "the black solid mesh is the smoothly moving mesh $\mathcal M^7_R$", while the text (p. 10) says "smoothly moving mesh $\mathcal M^7_S$ to smoothly moving mesh $\mathcal M^8_S$".
- Fig. 2, right panel: the bottom-right vertex of the reference square $[-\frac12,\frac12]^2$ is labelled "(−0.5,0.5)", the same as the top-left one.
- §3.5.4, Leblanc data: the state vector is $(\rho,u,v,e)$, but the left state is printed with five components, "(1, 0, 0, 0, 0.1)", and the right one with four, "(0.001, 0, 0, $10^{-7}$)". Read as $(\rho,v_1,v_2,e)=(1,0,0,0.1)$ and $(10^{-3},0,0,10^{-7})$ with $\gamma=1.4$, these are $p=0.04$ and $p=4\times10^{-11}$: the states printed by [Deng (2023)](/sources/deng-2023.md) on $[0,9]$ but with $\gamma=1.4$ instead of $5/3$ *(compiler check, not in the sources)*; the [Leblanc page](/benchmarks/leblanc-shock-tube.md) records both variants.
- §3.5.5 gives "201 × 3 points" for the two-material Sod problem, and the Fig. 20 caption "$N_x=200$, $N_y=3$". 201 points make 200 cells in $x$, but 3 points make 2 cells in $y$, so the caption's $N_y=3$ does not match the text if $N$ counts cells, as elsewhere in the paper.
- §2.6.3 writes "$\tilde U^{m_1,m_2}_{i,j}\in G_{i,j}$", but $G_{i,j}$ is defined as the set of quadrature points; the admissible set is $G$.
- "as excepted" for "as expected" (§3.5.2).
- Ref. [11] (Hirt, Amsden & Cook) prints "J. Comput. Phys. 14 (1997) 227–253", recorded as printed. Ref. [36] (Zhang, Xia & Shu) prints "J. Comput. Phys. 50 (2012) 29–62" for the work the bibliography holds as J. Sci. Comput. 50:29–62 (`zhang-2012-c`).

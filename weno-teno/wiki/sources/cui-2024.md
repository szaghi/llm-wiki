# Summary

The Zhang–Shu framework makes high-order DG and finite-volume schemes
[bound preserving](/properties/bound-preservation.md) in three steps:

1. a first-order scheme with a monotone flux, such as the
   [Lax–Friedrichs flux](/methods/lax-friedrichs-flux.md), stays in the admissible set $G$ under
   $a\Delta t/\Delta x\le c_0$;
2. the cell average $\bar u_i$ is written as a convex combination of point values that includes
   the interface values, a **cell average decomposition** (CAD);
3. the [Zhang–Shu scaling limiter](/methods/zhang-shu-scaling-limiter.md) puts those point values
   in $G$.

The smallest boundary weight $\varpi$ of the decomposition sets the bound-preserving CFL condition.
The paper poses the [optimal cell average decomposition](/methods/optimal-cell-average-decomposition.md)
(OCAD) problem: maximize that weight over all feasible CADs, meaning CADs that are exact on the
polynomial space, have positive weights, and have their internal nodes in the cell. It then solves
the problem in several cases:

- **One dimension.** The classic Gauss–Lobatto CAD with $L=\lceil(N+3)/2\rceil$ points is optimal
  and unique for every $\mathbb P^N$.
- **Two dimensions on Cartesian cells.** An OCAD exists, the set of feasible weights is convex, and
  one may restrict to symmetric CADs. The problem then reduces to one weight
  $\varpi_\star(\varkappa,\mathbb V^N)$, where $\varkappa$ is the directional
  anisotropy.
- **Optimality certificates.** A CAD is optimal if a squared polynomial of half degree vanishes at
  all its internal nodes; the sum-of-squares bound is a generalized eigenvalue.
- **Results by space.** The classic tensor CAD is optimal for $\mathbb Q^N$. It is not optimal for
  $\mathbb P^N$, for which the paper gives:
  - closed-form OCADs for $N\le7$;
  - a numerical algorithm for $N\ge8$;
  - a quasi-optimal CAD that reaches at least 95% of the optimum.

Applied to $\mathbb P^N$-[DG](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin.md) at $\varkappa=0$, the
bound-preserving CFL number rises from $0.1667$ to $0.25$ for $N=2,3$ and from $0.0833$ to
$0.1292$ for $N=4,5$.

Time stepping is [SSP Runge–Kutta](/methods/ssp-runge-kutta.md) or SSP multistep. Five tests show
the same accuracy and robustness at 20–50% less CPU time:

- linear advection;
- Burgers;
- a near-vacuum [shock–vortex interaction](/benchmarks/shock-vortex-interaction.md), built on the
  [isentropic vortex](/benchmarks/isentropic-vortex.md);
- a relativistic Riemann problem;
- a relativistic [jet](/benchmarks/high-mach-astrophysical-jet.md).

The authors relate the supporting lines of the feasible-weight set to
[geometric quasilinearization](/methods/geometric-quasilinearization.md).

# Key claims

Registry notation: the anisotropy $\theta$ is $\varkappa$ and the
Gauss–Lobatto nodes and weights are numbered from 0 (the source's
$\omega^{GL}_1$ is $\mathsf w^{\mathrm{GL}}_0$); see the notation map.

1. In one dimension, the classic Gauss–Lobatto CAD with $L=\lceil(N+3)/2\rceil$ points is the OCAD
   for every $\mathbb P^N$, $N\ge1$. Its optimal boundary weights are unique:
   $\varpi^\pm_\star=\mathsf w^{\mathrm{GL}}_0=1/(L(L-1))$. The proof tests with
   $\prod_{\ell=1}^{L-2}(x-x^{\mathrm{GL}}_\ell)^2$, which vanishes at the internal nodes.
   (Thm. 2.3) **Proved**
2. Theorem 2.3 confirms Zhang–Shu's Remark 2.7, which had been checked only for $2\le N\le3$. (§2)
   **Proved**
3. A two-dimensional OCAD exists for any $\mathbb V^N$ (Thm. 3.4, via Lemmas 3.1–3.3: Carathéodory
   bounds the number of internal nodes by $\dim\mathbb V^N+1$, and the feasible set is compact). The
   OCAD can be chosen with balanced weights,
   $\varpi^\pm_{1,\star}\Delta x/a^{(1)}=\varpi^\pm_{2,\star}\Delta y/a^{(2)}$ (3.8). **Proved**
4. A convex combination of two feasible CADs is feasible, so the set of feasible boundary weights is
   convex. (Lemma 3.5) **Proved**
5. There is always a symmetric OCAD on $[-1,1]^2$ (Thm. 3.12). The problem therefore reduces to
   maximizing one weight $\varpi_\star(\varkappa,\mathbb V^N)$, with
   $\varkappa=\dfrac{a^{(1)}/\Delta x-a^{(2)}/\Delta y}{a^{(1)}/\Delta x+a^{(2)}/\Delta y}\in[-1,1]$
   (Problem 3.13). The optimal CFL condition is
   $\Delta t\le c_0\varpi_\star/(a^{(1)}/\Delta x+a^{(2)}/\Delta y)$ (3.38). **Proved**
6. A symmetric CAD exact on the reflection-invariant subspace is exact on all of $\mathbb V^N$. For
   example, $\dim\mathbb P^3(G_s)=3$ against $\dim\mathbb P^3=10$. (Lemma 3.10, Remark 3.11)
   **Proved**
7. $\varpi_\star$ is non-increasing in $N$ (Lemma 3.14), equal for $\mathbb V^{2N}$ and
   $\mathbb V^{2N+1}$ (Lemma 3.15), and even in $\varkappa$ (Lemma 3.18). **Proved**
8. Upper bound:
   $\varpi_\star\le\phi_\star=\inf_{p\in\mathbb V^N_+}\langle p\rangle/[(1+\varkappa)\langle p\rangle^x+(1-\varkappa)\langle p\rangle^y]$.
   (Lemma 3.20) **Proved**
9. Four optimality criteria are proved (Thms. 3.24–3.27). Criterion #3 is the one used in
   practice: if some nonzero $q_\star\in\mathbb V^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}$ vanishes at all internal
   nodes, the CAD is a symmetric OCAD and $q_\star^2$ is a critical positive polynomial.
   **Proved**
10. The sum-of-squares bound has the closed form
    $\phi_\star(\varkappa,(\mathbb V^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor})^2)=1/\|M^{-1/2}M_\varkappa M^{-1/2}\|_2$.
    Equivalently, it is the smallest real root of $\det(M-\phi M_\varkappa)$, with Gram matrices
    $M$ and $M_\varkappa$. (Thm. 3.29) **Proved**
11. Conjectures 3.1–3.2 state that $\varpi_\star=\phi_\star$ and that a squared polynomial attains
    the infimum. They are proved for all $\mathbb Q^N$ and for $\mathbb P^N$ with $N\le7$, and
    checked numerically for $8\le N\le15$. (§3.7) **Proved** / **Shown**
12. The classic two-dimensional Zhang–Shu CAD, with $L=\lceil(N+3)/2\rceil$ Gauss–Lobatto points and
    $Q\ge(N+1)/2$ Gauss points, is an OCAD for $\mathbb Q^N$ for all $N$ and all
    $\varkappa$. Its weight is $\varpi_\star=\mathsf w^{\mathrm{GL}}_0=1/(L(L-1))$.
    (Thm. 4.1) **Proved**
13. For $\mathbb P^N$ the classic CAD is optimal at $\varkappa=\pm1$ (Thms. 5.1–5.2), but
    not for $\varkappa\in(-1,1)$: there it is a convex combination of the two extreme
    OCADs, whose weights lie inside the feasible set. (Remark 5.3, Fig. 4) **Proved** / **Shown**
14. The $\varkappa=0$ optima for $\mathbb P^N$ are:

    | $N$ | $\varpi_\star$ | Evidence |
    |---|---|---|
    | 2, 3 | $1/4$ | Thm. 5.7 |
    | 4, 5 | $2-\sqrt{14}/2\approx0.1292$ | Thm. 5.9 |
    | 6, 7 | $1-\sqrt{30}/6\approx0.08713$ | Thm. 5.10 |
    | 8, 9 | 0.057671737626023616 | App. C |
    | 10, 11 | 0.043783667884911184 | App. C |
    | 12, 13 | 0.032438153462135161 | App. C |
    | 14, 15 | 0.026235904303028568 | App. C |

    **Proved** ($N\le7$) / **Shown** ($N\ge8$)
15. For $\mathbb P^2$ and $\mathbb P^3$ and any $\varkappa$, the OCAD has
    $\varpi_\star=1/(4+2|\varkappa|)$ and a single internal node, with weight
    $(1+|\varkappa|)/(2+|\varkappa|)$. This agrees with the authors' earlier
    preprint. (Thm. 5.13, Remark 5.14) **Proved**
16. Closed-form OCADs exist for $\mathbb P^4,\mathbb P^5$ (two internal nodes, Thm. 5.15) and for
    $\mathbb P^6,\mathbb P^7$ (four internal nodes, Thm. 5.17). The weights are roots of cubics, in
    Viète form. (Apps. A–B) **Proved** (node positivity and location are checked graphically,
    Figs. 7–8)
17. For $N\ge8$ the OCAD comes from solving the nonlinear system (5.35) with `fsolve`, continued in
    $\varkappa$ from the analytic $\varkappa=-1$ solution. Residuals reach machine
    precision in about 6–10 iterations. (Alg. 5.20, Fig. 9) **Shown**
18. The OCADs need far fewer internal nodes than the classic CAD: 1 for $\mathbb P^{2,3}$, 2 for
    $\mathbb P^{4,5}$ and 4 for $\mathbb P^{6,7}$. This also reduces the cost of the limiter.
    (Remarks 5.11–5.18, Table 2, Remark 7.3) **Shown**
19. The quasi-optimal CAD (6.2) combines the $\varkappa=\pm1$ and $\varkappa=0$
    OCADs convexly, so it is feasible. Its weight is
    $\varpi^Q_\star=\varpi_{\star,0}\mathsf w^{\mathrm{GL}}_0/(\varpi_{\star,0}|\varkappa|+\mathsf w^{\mathrm{GL}}_0(1-|\varkappa|))$,
    and for $\mathbb P^2,\mathbb P^3$ it coincides with the OCAD. (Thm. 6.1) **Proved**
20. The classic CAD can fall to "57% ∼ 65%" of $\varpi_\star$. The quasi-optimal CAD stays above
    95% for $\mathbb P^4$–$\mathbb P^9$. (§6, Fig. 11) **Shown**
21. Bound-preserving theorem for $\mathbb P^N$-DG with any feasible symmetric CAD (Thm. 7.1,
    eq. 7.10). Suppose:
    - the Gauss-point interface values lie in $G$;
    - $\Pi_{ij}=\frac1{1-2\varpi}\sum_s\mathsf w_sp_{ij}(x^{(s)},y^{(s)})\in G$;
    - $\Delta t(a^{(1)}/\Delta x+a^{(2)}/\Delta y)\le\varpi c_0$.

    Then $\bar u^{n+1}_{ij}\in G$. The same holds under SSP time stepping. **Proved**
22. $\Pi_{ij}$ can be computed from the cell average and the interface values alone (7.16). The
    simplified limiter therefore needs no internal nodes, only $\varpi$. (Remark 7.4) **Derived**
23. Table 1 ($c_0=1$, $\varkappa=0$) compares three CFL numbers:

    | $N$ | linear stability $1/(2N+1)$ | classic $\mathsf w^{\mathrm{GL}}_0$ | optimal $\varpi_\star$ |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | 1 | 1/3 | 1/2 | 1/2 |
    | 2 | 1/5 | 1/6 | 1/4 |
    | 3 | 1/7 | 1/6 | 1/4 |
    | 4 | 1/9 | 1/12 | 0.1292 |
    | 5 | 1/11 | 1/12 | 0.1292 |
    | 6 | 1/13 | 1/20 | 0.08713 |
    | 7 | 1/15 | 1/20 | 0.08713 |
    | 8 | 1/17 | 1/30 | 0.05767 |
    | 9 | 1/19 | 1/30 | 0.05767 |

    **Derived**
24. With $c_0=1$, the optimal bound-preserving CFL number exceeds the linear-stability one,
    $1/(2N+1)$, for every $N\le9$ except $N=8$. So the linear-stability step alone guarantees
    bound preservation. With $c_0=1/2$ (HLL, HLLC), the bound-preserving condition dominates, but it
    is much milder than with the classic CAD. (Remark 7.7, Fig. 12) **Derived**
25. Accuracy (Ex. 1): the scheme keeps order $N+1$ for $N=2,4,6,8$, with errors close to those of
    the classic approach, and the limiter does not destroy accuracy. CPU time drops, for example:
    - $\mathbb P^2$, $N_x=640$: 2870.80 s against 3858.67 s;
    - $\mathbb P^8$, $N_x=14$: 44.91 s against 92.61 s.

    (Tables 3–6) **Shown**
26. CPU times, optimal / quasi-optimal / classic:
    - Burgers, $80\times80$, $\mathbb P^6$: 214.307 s optimal against 323.648 s classic;
    - shock–vortex, $\mathbb P^4$: 1299.55, 1327.45 and 1772.26 min;
    - relativistic Riemann problem, $\mathbb P^4$: 1623.67, 1597.97 and 2103.50 min;
    - relativistic jet, $\mathbb P^4$: 297.26, 334.59 and 414.19 h.

    The runs are in C++, double precision, on an Intel Xeon Platinum 8268. (Tables 7–10, §8)
    **Shown**
27. Without the bound-preserving limiter the DG code breaks down on Ex. 3 and Ex. 5. (§8.3, §8.5)
    **Stated**
28. The supporting lines of the critical polynomials touch the boundary of the feasible-weight set;
    the authors relate this to geometric quasilinearization. The boundary is non-smooth at
    $\varkappa=0$ for $\mathbb P^{4N+2},\mathbb P^{4N+3}$, where the OCAD and the critical
    polynomial are not unique. (§3.7, Fig. 4) **Shown**

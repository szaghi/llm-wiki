# Summary

Wang et al. (2023a) modify the two-point flux inside the fifth-order
characteristic-wise [alternative WENO](/methods/alternative-weno.md) (AWENO)
finite-difference scheme so that its numerical dissipation drops in smooth regions.
The interface states are interpolated with the Z-type
[affine-invariant weights](/methods/affine-invariant-weno.md) of
[Wang & Don (2022)](/sources/wang-2022-b.md) (the "Ai-AWENO-Z" scheme, called
AWENO in the paper). The new flux, LLF-M, keeps the form of the local
[Lax–Friedrichs flux](/methods/lax-friedrichs-flux.md) but replaces its coefficient
$a_{i+1/2}$ by $a^{\mathrm M}_{i+1/2}=a_{i+1/2}\max\{\mathsf k_i,\mathsf k_{i+1}\}$,
with $\mathsf k_i=\max\big(0.1,\,1-\exp(-10^5\varphi_{\mathrm F,i})\big)$
driven by a non-dimensional feature sensor $\varphi_{\mathrm F,i}$: the product of
the [Ducros](/definitions/ducros-shock-sensor.md) dilatation–vorticity ratio and
Jameson-type normalised second differences of pressure and density. The
[numerical dissipation](/definitions/numerical-dissipation.md) is thus cut to about
10% of the Lax–Friedrichs value where the sensor sees smooth flow and kept near it
at shocks. The idea parallels, for the Lax–Friedrichs flux, the same group's
[adaptive-diffusion central-upwind flux](/methods/adaptive-diffusion-central-upwind-flux.md),
whose paper the authors cite as a source of the WENO interpolation without drawing
the comparison.

There is no theorem. Fifth order is retained on the
[density-wave advection](/benchmarks/density-wave-advection.md) and the
[isentropic vortex](/benchmarks/isentropic-vortex.md), where LLF-M has smaller
errors than LLF. One-dimensional tests ([Lax, a Lax problem scaled by $10^{-7}$, and Sod](/benchmarks/sod-and-lax-shock-tubes.md),
[Woodward–Colella](/benchmarks/woodward-colella-blast-wave.md), an extended Mach-3
[shock–density-wave interaction](/benchmarks/shu-osher-problem.md)) show the same
shock capturing with sharper contacts and entropy waves; the scaled Lax problem also
shows the classical Z-weight AWENO oscillating where the affine-invariant one does
not. The paper calls the feature sensor non-dimensional and scale-invariant; its
pressure and density factors are normalised second differences, the building block
of the [scale sensors](/definitions/scale-sensor.md). Two-dimensional tests
([double Mach reflection](/benchmarks/double-mach-reflection.md),
[2D Riemann configurations 3 and 6](/benchmarks/two-dimensional-riemann-problems.md),
[forward-facing step](/benchmarks/forward-facing-step.md)) show more roll-up along
slip lines. The extra cost is 5–9% of CPU time.

# Key claims

Registry notation: conserved state $u$ (source $Q$), velocity $v$ (source $u$),
pressure $p$ (source $P$), candidate interpolants $u^{(k)}_{i+1/2}$ (source
$P^{(k)}$), weight exponent $q$ (source $p$), affine map $\sigma u+\mu$ (source
$\lambda f+c$), descaler $\mathsf m$ (source $\mu$), stencil mean $\langle u\rangle$
(source $\bar\xi$), Lax–Friedrichs coefficient $a_{i+1/2}$ (source $\alpha$), Ducros
ratio $\Phi_{\mathrm D}$ (source $\varphi_v$), Jameson pressure factor
$\varphi_{\mathrm J}[p]$ (source $\varphi_P$), vortex strength $\mathcal E$ (source
$\epsilon$). Page-local: $a^{\mathrm M}_{i+1/2}$ the LLF-M coefficient (source
$\bar\alpha$), $\mathsf k_i$ and $\mathsf k_{\min}$ the modifier and
its floor (source $\kappa_i$, $\kappa_{\min}$), $\delta_{\mathrm F}$ the sensor
sensitivity (source $\delta$), $\varphi_{\mathrm J}[\rho]$ the density analogue (source $\varphi_\rho$), $\varphi_{\mathrm F,i}$ the feature sensor.

1. LLF-M reduces the dissipation coefficient of the Lax–Friedrichs flux adaptively in space and time through a feature sensor, improving the resolution of small-scale structures while keeping the ENO property (Abstract, §1, §3.2, §5). **Shown**
2. AWENO with affine-invariant weights and either flux reaches fifth order on the advection of $(\rho,v,p)=(1+0.1\sin(\pi x),1,1)$ on $[0,2]$, $t=2$, $\Delta t\le\Delta x^{5/3}$: printed orders 5.30, 5.09, 5.02, 4.96 for both fluxes, $L^1(\rho)=6.18\times10^{-12}$ (LLF) and $6.17\times10^{-12}$ (LLF-M) at $N_x=320$ (§4.1, Table 1). **Shown**
3. Isentropic vortex ($t=1$): LLF-M has smaller errors than LLF on every grid, at $320\times320$ $L^1(\rho)=3.52\times10^{-7}$ (LLF, order 4.77) against $3.28\times10^{-8}$ (LLF-M, order 5.25); the LLF-M orders 5.07, 5.62, 5.51, 5.25 exceed five, and the text says both fluxes "achieve the optimal convergence order" (§4.5, Table 2). **Shown** *(Compiler check, not in the sources: the printed orders follow from the printed errors to within rounding; the LLF-M orders above five are pre-asymptotic values, not a demonstrated fifth order at $320^2$.)*
4. The LLF-M flux is $h^{\mathrm{LF\text{-}M}}=\tfrac12\big(f(u^-_{i+1/2})+f(u^+_{i+1/2})\big)-\tfrac12a^{\mathrm M}_{i+1/2}(u^+_{i+1/2}-u^-_{i+1/2})$ with $a^{\mathrm M}_{i+1/2}=a_{i+1/2}\max\{\mathsf k_i,\mathsf k_{i+1}\}$, $\mathsf k_i=\max\big(\mathsf k_{\min},1-\exp(-\delta_{\mathrm F}\varphi_{\mathrm F,i})\big)\in[\mathsf k_{\min},1)$, $a_{i+1/2}=\max\{\lvert v^\pm\rvert+c^\pm\}$ (Eqs. (17)–(20)). **Stated** (definition) *(Compiler check, not in the sources: $1-\exp(-\delta_{\mathrm F}\varphi_{\mathrm F,i})<1$, so $0.1\,a_{i+1/2}\le a^{\mathrm M}_{i+1/2}<a_{i+1/2}$ everywhere: the coefficient never reaches the local maximum wave speed, even at shocks, and the paper makes no monotonicity, entropy or positivity statement for it.)*
5. The feature sensor is $\varphi_{\mathrm F,i}=\Phi_{\mathrm D}\,\varphi_{\mathrm J}[p]\,\varphi_{\mathrm J}[\rho]$ with $\Phi_{\mathrm D}=(\nabla\cdot v)^2/\big((\nabla\cdot v)^2+\lvert\nabla\times v\rvert^2+10^{-40}\big)$, $\varphi_{\mathrm J}[q]=\lvert(q_{i+1}-2q_i+q_{i-1})/(q_{i+1}+2q_i+q_{i-1})\rvert$ applied to $q=p$ and $q=\rho$ (Eq. (21)); the product is said to stop the stand-alone Ducros sensor from firing where dilatation and vorticity are both small (§3.2). **Stated**
6. The sensor is $O(1)$ in high-divergence regions and tends to zero in vortex-dominated regions (§3.2). **Stated**
7. $\delta_{\mathrm F}$ is problem-dependent; larger values make the sensor more sensitive; $\delta_{\mathrm F}=10^5$ "works well" after extensive tests on shock tubes, and its optimal choice is left open (§3.2). **Stated**
8. $\mathsf k_{\min}=0.1$ keeps a small baseline dissipation where one or more primitive variables are constant or linear (§3.2, Fig. 1). **Stated**
9. In smooth density regions of the Sod and blast-wave problems $a^{\mathrm M}\approx\mathsf k_{\min}a$ (§4.3, Figs. 6, 8). **Shown**
10. In the shock–density-wave problem the LLF coefficient rises "to about $\alpha\ge4$ from $\alpha\approx1.4$" wherever the main shock passes, while the LLF-M coefficient exceeds 4 only around the main shock and the shocklets, and is "$\approx0.5$ or smaller" in the high-frequency smooth region (§4.4, Fig. 10). **Shown**
11. On the Lax problem scaled by $10^{-7}$ the classical AWENO with Z-weights ($\mathsf m=1$) oscillates near steep gradients and the affine-invariant AWENO does not; the same holds with LLF (figure omitted) (§4.2, Fig. 4). **Shown**
12. With $\mathsf m=1$ the affine-invariant AWENO-Z reduces to the classical AWENO-Z (§2.1). **Stated**
13. The descaler lets $\epsilon$ be set independently of the scaling and translation of the data (§2.1). **Stated**
14. Affine-invariant WENO keeps the ENO property for small scalings with non-zero translation, where WENO-JS and WENO-Z do not (§1, as recalled from Wang & Don 2022). **Stated**
15. WENO-JS reaches optimal order at critical points only with $\epsilon=\Delta x^2$ (§1, as recalled). **Stated**
16. Because AWENO interpolates conservative variables rather than fluxes, any monotone flux or approximate Riemann solver can be used (§2). **Stated**
17. The same conclusion holds for the seventh- and ninth-order AWENO schemes (§2). **Stated** (not shown)
18. The Lax–Friedrichs flux is relatively dissipative, and the alternatives are "complex to implement and time-consuming to compute" (§1). **Stated** (no comparison shown)
19. In 2D, LLF-M resolves more small-scale structure along the double-Mach slip line and jet tip, at the triple point and jet neck of Configuration 3, in the shear vortices of Configuration 6 and along the forward-facing-step slip line, with the same dissipation as LLF near shocks (§§4.6–4.8, Figs. 11–19). **Shown**
20. In Configuration 6 the three sensor factors detect dilatation against vorticity and high pressure and density gradients; their product keeps smooth vortices unflagged (§4.7, Fig. 17). **Shown**
21. CPU times LLF / LLF-M: double Mach reflection ($800\times200$) 954 / 1004 s, Configuration 3 ($400\times400$) 1386 / 1487 s, Configuration 6 ($400\times400$) 1246 / 1306 s, forward-facing step ($900\times300$) 12670 / 13760 s, "5%–9% more CPU times" (Fortran 90, ifort -O3, Intel i7-9700) (§4.9, Table 3). **Shown**
22. The idea extends to finite-volume WENO, DG and multi-material flows (future work) (§5). **Stated**

Setting (§2): fifth-order AWENO flux $\hat f_{i+1/2}=h(u^-,u^+)-\tfrac1{24}\Delta x^2f_{xx}\vert_{i+1/2}+\tfrac7{5760}\Delta x^4f_{xxxx}\vert_{i+1/2}$ with six-point central differences (Eqs. (3)–(4)); interpolation weights $d_k=(1/16,5/8,5/16)$; affine-invariant Z weights $\alpha_k=d_k\big(1+(\tau_5/(\beta_k+\epsilon\mathsf m^2))^q\big)$, $\mathsf m=10^{-40}+\tfrac15\sum_{S^5}\lvert u_l-\langle u\rangle\rvert$, $\epsilon=10^{-12}$, $q=2$ (Eqs. (7)–(8)); Roe-averaged characteristic projection; three-stage SSP Runge–Kutta, CFL 0.45 (Eq. (22)).

# Summary

The paper builds DGCCGF, a sharp-interface Runge–Kutta
[discontinuous Galerkin](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin.md) solver for
two-dimensional compressible two-medium flows (gas–gas and gas–water), each
fluid governed by the Euler equations with its own
[stiffened-gas equation of state](/definitions/stiffened-gas-equation-of-state.md)
in temperature form. It is a sharp-interface alternative to the
[diffuse-interface models](/definitions/five-equation-model.md) for
[multicomponent flows](/definitions/multicomponent-euler-equations.md). The
interface is carried by a [level-set](/methods/level-set-method.md) function
and represented by a straight cut line in each crossed Cartesian cell, a
[cut-cell](/methods/immersed-boundary-method.md) construction. The novelty is the
update of the two small cut cells of a crossed cell: instead of merging small
cells with neighbours, each fluid gets an intermediate mixed polynomial on the
whole regular cell, the $L^2$ projection of its real polynomial on its own part
and of a ghost-fluid polynomial on the other part (Eqs. 27–28). The ghost
states come from the original ghost fluid method (gas–gas) or its gas–water
variant, with the high-order coefficients of the ghost polynomial set equal to
those of the real fluid. Each fluid is then updated on the full cell, so small
cut cells do not restrict the time step and topological change is handled
without special treatment. The finite-volume "real–ghost mixing" of Bai & Deng
(2017) is the zeroth-order special case.

The other ingredients are an HLLC–HLL hybrid flux
([HLL family](/methods/hll-riemann-solvers.md)) switched by a pressure-jump
[shock indicator](/definitions/troubled-cell-indicator.md) derived from the
[Rankine–Hugoniot conditions](/definitions/rankine-hugoniot-condition.md), cited
as a remedy for the [carbuncle](/definitions/carbuncle-phenomenon.md); an exact
two-phase Riemann solver for interface velocities; HOUC3 level-set transport; a
topology-change identifier that triggers reinitialization; deletion of isolated
structures smaller than half a cell; quadtree
[AMR](/methods/adaptive-mesh-refinement.md); a TVB minmod limiter (a WENO
limiter for the Shu–Osher case); and three- or four-stage
[Runge–Kutta](/methods/ssp-runge-kutta.md) stepping. Evidence is numerical:
[gas–gas and gas–water shock tubes](/benchmarks/gas-liquid-riemann-problems.md),
slab shock–bubble problems, a two-phase [Shu–Osher](/benchmarks/shu-osher-problem.md)
problem, a drop in a compressible shear flow,
[Richtmyer–Meshkov](/benchmarks/richtmyer-meshkov-instability.md) instabilities,
Haas–Sturtevant [shock–bubble interactions](/benchmarks/shock-bubble-interaction.md),
a shock-induced bubble collapse and an
[underwater explosion](/benchmarks/underwater-explosion.md). Nothing is proved,
and conservation at the interface is not measured beyond the mass lost to
deletion.

# Key claims

Registry notation: the source's velocity $(u,v)$ is $(v_1,v_2)$, its specific
total energy $E$ is $E/\rho$ (the registry's $E$ is per unit volume), its
stiffening pressure $P_\infty$ is $p_{\infty,k}$, its wave speeds $S_L$, $S_R$
are $s^-$, $s^+$ and its DG degree $k$ is $N$.

1. The improved real–ghost mixing replaces geometric merging of small cells, so small cut cells do not restrict the time step and topological change of the interface is handled automatically; the algorithm is simpler than the DG cut-cell method of Deng & Li (§1, §2.5.2, §4). **Stated** (topological change **Shown**, §3.4, Fig. 25)
2. The finite-volume mixing of Bai & Deng is a zeroth-order version of the present one; transplanted to DG it is excessively dissipative, which the high-order mixing (Eqs. 27–28) avoids (§2.5.2, Fig. 3). **Shown** (qualitatively, Fig. 25)
3. For the air–helium shock–bubble interaction the original mixing method and the DG cut-cell method of Deng & Li give an interface evolution "obviously delayed at later stage", attributed to the latter's Lax–Friedrichs flux and low-order cut-cell scheme and to the former's zeroth-order mixing; the DG cut-cell method fails at 983 μs because it cannot handle the topological transition (§3.4, Fig. 25). **Shown**
4. The HLLC–HLL flux replaces the Lax–Friedrichs flux of Deng & Li "to reduce the numerical dissipation", and the HLL switch is used "to avoid the shock instability which may meet in the vicinity of strong shock" (§2.4). **Stated** (see Printed slips for the switch as printed)
5. The shock indicator (Eq. 18), with constant 0.3, fires when $\lvert p^--p^+\rvert>0.3\,\lvert v_n^--v_n^+\rvert\min(\rho^-c^-,\rho^+c^+)$, "similar to the Joukowsky equation except for an parameter", derived from the Rankine–Hugoniot conditions "with some empiric observations" (§2.4). **Stated**
6. Reinitialization every 100 steps suffices when the interface curvature is small; at large curvature or topological change it must be more frequent, which the identifier $I(t)$ (Eq. 26) detects from the vertex signs of the level set (§2.5.1). **Stated**
7. Isolated structures with volume below 0.5 of a cell are deleted; the mass loss is "weak" because deletion is rare (Fig. 6: helium and R22 shock–bubble, $N=2$, 180 and 360 cells per bubble diameter) (§2.5.3). **Shown**
8. The cut-cell treatment extends to 3D through a vertex-sign classification into (1−7), (2−6), (3−5) and (4−4) types (App. A, Fig. 37). **Stated** (no 3D test)
9. Air–helium shock tube: two-level grids with 50 and 100 elements per metre agree "very well" with the exact solution at $t=1.7817$ ms, with a sharp density jump at the interface (§3.1.1, Fig. 8). **Shown**
10. Water–air shock tube: good agreement with the exact solution except weak temperature oscillations, which weaken on the four-level grid (§3.1.2, Fig. 9). **Shown**
11. 1D water–gas shock–bubble: the solution converges with refinement (25, 50, 100 elements per metre) to the finite-volume cut-cell reference (§3.1.4, Fig. 11). **Shown**
12. At an equal number of elements (50 per metre) $N=1$ and $N=3$ beat $N=0$ but "seem the same", attributed to the piecewise-constant structure of the solution; at equal degrees of freedom the three agree up to slight differences (§3.1.4, Figs. 12–13). **Shown**
13. Two-phase Shu–Osher: at 200 elements the accuracy rises with the degree; at equal degrees of freedom (800 $N=0$, 400 $N=1$, 200 $N=3$) $N=1$ and $N=3$ agree except near the material interface and beat $N=0$ (§3.1.5, Fig. 14). **Shown**
14. Drop in a compressible shear flow ($N=2$, $64^2$ to $512^2$): observed shape-error orders 1.9973, 1.6802, 1.3306 and volume-error orders 2.5451, 2.7001, 3.2572; the errors "may be seriously effected by the re-initialization" (§3.2, Table 1). **Shown**
15. Air–SF$_6$ Richtmyer–Meshkov amplitude and growth rate agree with Holmes et al., Ullah et al., Bai et al. and the Zhang–Sohn model (§3.3.1, Fig. 17). **Shown**
16. Cu–gas Richtmyer–Meshkov (phase reversal): the rarefaction decompresses the metal from 8930 to 7570 kg/m³; the asymptotic bubble velocity is almost the unperturbed interface velocity, and the spike velocity decreases after its peak (§3.3.2, Figs. 18–19). **Shown**
17. Air–helium shock–bubble: space–time diagrams of the upstream, downstream and jet points converge over four AMR grids, and density contours agree with the Haas–Sturtevant shadowgraphs and published computations (§3.4, Figs. 22–24). **Shown**
18. Air–helium: the front-tracking results of Terashima & Tryggvason are delayed in the upstream and jet curves with respect to the present ones, as also found by others; the jet curve of the original mixing is delayed too (§3.4, Fig. 26). **Shown**
19. Air–R22: interface and shock positions almost coincide with Ullah et al.'s front tracking; the interface structure is richer "due to the higher grid resolution" (§3.4, Figs. 27–30). **Shown**
20. Shock-induced air-bubble collapse in water ($M=1.72$, 80 cells per diameter, four levels): water jet at about 2.86 μs, impact on the downstream wall at about 3.053 μs, water-hammer shock at about 3.28 μs; bubble height and width histories agree with Shukla (§3.5, Figs. 31–33). **Shown**
21. Underwater explosion ($160\times160$ background, three AMR levels): consistent with Shukla's $1200\times1200$ computation with a sharper liquid-film interface; the density along $x=2$ m at 0.2 ms agrees with Hu et al. and Deng et al. except at the bubble centre (§3.6, Figs. 34–36). **Shown**
22. For DG "the cost per cell is higher and the local conservation can not be easily guaranteed", whereas finite volumes "can easily achieve local conservation" (§1, p. 2). **Stated** (an introductory remark, not tested. With the test function $1$ the paper's own weak form, Eqs. (4)–(6), updates the cell mean by a difference of single-valued numerical fluxes, which is local conservation in the sense of the [flux-form result](/results/local-conservation-flux-form.md); the steps of this solver that do not conserve are the ghost-fluid mixing in cut cells and the deletion of isolated structures *(compiler check, not in the sources)*)

# Printed slips

- Eq. (11): $\rho^*_K=\beta_L/(S_K-S^*)$, $K=L,R$; the mass component of the jump relation (9) gives $\rho^*_K(S_K-S^*)=\rho_K(S_K-u_K)=\beta_K$, so the numerator is $\beta_K$.
- Eqs. (16), (18)–(19), HLLC–HLL switch: with $\hat{\mathbf U}^*=f\,\mathbf U^*_{\mathrm{HLLC}}+(1-f)\,\mathbf U^{\mathrm{HLL}}$, $f=1$ is pure HLLC. Eq. (19) is stated "when the indicator function is equal to 1": $f=f_p=1$ if both face-normal Mach numbers lie on the same side of 1, $f=0$ otherwise; $f$ where the indicator is 0 is not stated. A stationary shock on a face has one Mach number above and one below 1 and gets HLL, as the stated aim requires, but a detected moving shock with both Mach numbers on one side of 1 gets HLLC, the opposite of that aim; whether Kim et al.'s original switch differs cannot be decided from this paper *(compiler check, not in the sources: reading of the printed Eqs. 16, 18, 19; the same reading is on the [carbuncle](/definitions/carbuncle-phenomenon.md) page)*.
- Eq. (25): the third branch is written with $\varphi_0$ for $\phi_0$. Eq. (6): unbalanced closing parenthesis.
- Eq. (40) against Eq. (27): the 3D form drops the star and writes $\mathbf U_{cc1}$, $\mathbf U_{gc1}$ for the quantities Eq. (27) calls $\mathbf U_{mx1}$, $\mathbf U_{gmx1}$.
- §3.1.3: "a 1D shock-bubble interaction problem of Quirk and Karni [1]"; [1] is Abgrall (1996), Quirk & Karni is [63].
- Figs. 10–13 captions: "FVCCM100: results from FVCCM with 100 DG elements per meter"; FVCCM is a finite-volume method.
- Degrees of freedom: Fig. 15 counts 3 per cell for $N=2$ ($12288=64^2\times3$), Fig. 9 counts 6 ($15000=50^2\times6$); a complete $\mathbb P^2$ space in 2D has 6.
- Table 3: the Cu stiffening pressure is printed "4.62e10" in "bar"; in Pa it gives a sound speed $\sqrt{\gamma(p+p_\infty)/\rho}\approx3.94\times10^3$ m/s, in bar $\approx1.25\times10^6$ m/s, so Pa is meant. *(compiler check, not in the sources.)* The table also labels the gas density "$\rho_{air}$" for a gas with $\gamma=1.67$, and prints the amplitude as "a =" without a value.
- §3.6: "physical parameter is the same as those in section 3.5", but §3.5 prints no $C_p$ for the 2D water–air cases.
- Reference list: "Stony Broke" [33], "An conservative" [38], "Sethain" [55], [77], "Jaccobi" [55], "adaptived" [54], "imcompressible" [77]; implausible volumes in [4] ("9 (2018)") and [48] ("Numer. Math. 9 (2016)").

# Summary

Chen & Deng propose new nonlinear weights for fifth-order shock-capturing
interpolation. The weights keep the design order at smooth
[critical points](/properties/critical-point-accuracy.md) of any order and use
only data of the local stencil. They are the
[exponential nonlinear weights](/methods/exponential-nonlinear-weights.md):
the unnormalized weight is $\alpha_k=d_k\exp(-\beta_k^{\lambda_{\mathrm E}}/\lambda_{\mathrm E})$,
with the point-value [smoothness indicators](/definitions/smoothness-indicator.md)
$\beta_k$ and a stencil-local parameter
$\lambda_{\mathrm E}=\max(e^{-3\theta_{\mathrm E}},10^{-6})$,
$\theta_{\mathrm E}=\tau_5/(\beta_{\min}+\epsilon)^{0.8}$, where $\tau_5=\lvert\beta_0-\beta_2\rvert$
is the [WENO-Z](/methods/weno-z.md) global indicator. In smooth regions
$\tau_5$ is at least one order higher in $\Delta x$ than $\beta_{\min}$, so
$\lambda_{\mathrm E}\to1$ and $\alpha_k=d_k+O(\Delta x^2)$, which is the
fifth-order condition (Theorem 3.1, argued in a paragraph, not proved
formally). Near a discontinuity $\lambda_{\mathrm E}$ becomes small and the
non-smooth stencils are suppressed. A ratio form (Eqs. (35)–(36)) avoids
exponential underflow.

The weights are tested inside a fifth-order staggered
[weighted compact nonlinear scheme](/methods/weighted-compact-nonlinear-scheme.md)
(WCNS-EXP): point-value interpolation to flux points, the Roe flux,
interpolation in characteristic variables, and a sixth-order explicit
central difference that mixes flux-point and solution-point fluxes. The
comparators are WCNS-JS, WCNS-Z and a modified WCNS-MR, and, in the shock
tubes, finite-difference WENO-JS, -Z and -MR. In the
[approximate dispersion relation](/definitions/approximate-dispersion-relation.md)
analysis WCNS-EXP is slightly better than WCNS-JS and comparable to
WCNS-Z. In a quadruple-precision interpolation test only WCNS-EXP reaches
fifth order for every critical-point order. It costs about 1.2 to 1.34 times
as much as WCNS-JS, much less than WCNS-MR. The Euler tests are
[Sod and Lax](/benchmarks/sod-and-lax-shock-tubes.md),
[Shu–Osher](/benchmarks/shu-osher-problem.md) and Titarev–Toro,
[two 2D Riemann problems](/benchmarks/two-dimensional-riemann-problems.md),
[double Mach reflection](/benchmarks/double-mach-reflection.md),
[forward-facing step](/benchmarks/forward-facing-step.md) and
[Rayleigh–Taylor](/benchmarks/rayleigh-taylor-instability.md). The authors
report that WCNS-EXP breaks symmetry more, and oscillates more, in one
Riemann configuration. The authors claim that the weights use "no
dimensional scales". They are not invariant under $u\mapsto\sigma u$, which
bears on [scale invariance](/properties/scale-invariance.md) (compiler
check in Key claims 1).

# Key claims

Registry notation: $d_k$ the linear weights, $\beta_k$ the point-value
smoothness indicators, $\tau_5=\lvert\beta_0-\beta_2\rvert$, $\epsilon$ the
regularization, $q$ the exponent of WENO-Z weights. $\lambda_{\mathrm E}$,
$\theta_{\mathrm E}$, $\beta_{\min}=\min_k\beta_k$ and $\mu^{\mathrm E}_{kl}$ are
page-local (see the Notation map). Grid index $i$, flux points $x_{i+1/2}$.

1. The proposed weights reach the design (fifth) order whatever the order of the critical point. They use only local data and "no dimensional scales" (Abstract, §1, §5). **Stated** (the order is Derived and Shown; the absence of scales is asserted). The weights are not invariant under $u\mapsto\sigma u$. For $\lambda_{\mathrm E}=1$ they depend on the differences $\beta_k-\beta_l$, which scale as $\sigma^2$, and $\theta_{\mathrm E}$ scales as $\sigma^{0.4}$. On the smooth data $u_{i+j}=\sin(1+0.2j)$, $j=-2,\dots,2$, the weights are $(0.0625,0.6252,0.3123)$ for $\sigma=1$ and $(0.042,0.903,0.054)$ for $\sigma=100$. At the floor $\lambda_{\mathrm E}=10^{-6}$, $\beta^{\lambda}/\lambda=1/\lambda+\ln\beta+O(\lambda\ln^2\beta)$, so the weights tend to $\omega_k\propto d_k/\beta_k$, which is scale-invariant *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
2. Most earlier unconditionally optimal methods [14–16] rely on dimensional parameters or global data, which makes them hard to use on nonuniform grids. Baeza et al. [17] use only local data, but their framework excludes third order, which is treated separately in [18] (§1). **Stated**
3. WCNS keep free-stream preservation on curvilinear grids more easily than WENO when the metrics use the symmetric conservative metric method [20, 21], and they admit more choices of numerical flux [22] (§1). **Stated**
4. The sixth-order central difference (5) mixes flux-point and solution-point fluxes. Its truncation error is $\frac1{8960}\partial_x^7f\,\Delta x^6+O(\Delta x^8)$ (Eq. (6)). Because of its lower resolution it is more robust than the scheme using flux points only [25] (§2). **Derived**. A Taylor expansion of (5) reproduces the coefficient $1/8960$ *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
5. Fifth-order interpolation requires $\omega_k=d_k+O(\Delta x^2)$ (Eq. (25), citing [19]). Jiang–Shu weights fail this at critical points [8] (§3). **Stated**
6. With $\alpha_k=d_k\exp(-\beta_k^{\lambda_{\mathrm E}}/\lambda_{\mathrm E})$ (Eq. (27)) and $\lambda_{\mathrm E}=1$, $\alpha_k=d_k+O(\Delta x^2)$ (Eq. (31)), because $\beta_k=O(\Delta x^2)$ (Eqs. (28)–(30)). It suffices that $\lim_{\Delta x\to0}\lambda_{\mathrm E}=1$ (Eq. (32)) (§3). **Derived**
7. For smooth data with a critical point of any order, $\tau_5$ is at least one order higher in $\Delta x$ than $\beta_{\min}$ [8]. Hence $\theta_{\mathrm E}\to0$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm E}=e^{-3\theta_{\mathrm E}}\to1$ (§3, after Eq. (34)). **Stated** (cited to [8]). With the indicators (14)–(16), at a critical point where the first $m$ derivatives vanish ($m=1,\dots,4$), the leading orders are $\beta_k=O(\Delta x^{2m+2})$ and $\tau_5=O(\Delta x^{2m+3})$, so $\theta_{\mathrm E}=O(\Delta x^{0.4m+1.4})\to0$ for generic nonzero higher derivatives *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
8. **Theorem 3.1:** with the weights (26), (27) and (33), the interpolation (11) is unconditionally fifth order for smooth data (§3). **Derived** (the proof is the argument of claims 6–7, not a formal proof).
9. The exponent $0.8$ in (33) is empirical. It speeds up $\lambda_{\mathrm E}\to1$, because near high-order critical points $\tau_5\ll\beta_{\min}$ may fail at practical grid sizes and machine precision. The factor $3$ is chosen "to obtain a more stable scheme", and the floor $10^{-6}$ keeps the fraction in (27) finite (§3). **Stated**
10. Direct evaluation of (26)–(27) underflows for small $\lambda_{\mathrm E}$, so the ratio form (35)–(36) is used, with $\mu^{\mathrm E}_{kl}=\exp\big(-(\beta_k^{\lambda_{\mathrm E}}-\beta_l^{\lambda_{\mathrm E}})/\lambda_{\mathrm E}\big)$ (§3). **Stated**. With $e_k=\exp(-\beta_k^{\lambda_{\mathrm E}}/\lambda_{\mathrm E})$, $\mu^{\mathrm E}_{kl}=e_k/e_l$, so (35) equals (26)–(27) exactly *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
11. In the approximate dispersion relation analysis [28] (Fig. 1), WCNS-EXP is slightly better than WCNS-JS and comparable to WCNS-Z: EXP is better at high wavenumbers, Z at moderate ones. WCNS-MR has the largest dissipation of the four (§3). **Shown**
12. In the test $\mathsf g_k(x)=x^ke^{0.75(x-1)}$ on $[-1,1]$ in quadruple precision, WCNS-EXP reaches fifth order for all $k=1,\dots,6$ and is the closest to the linear scheme. For $k=1$, or for a critical-point order not below the scheme order, all schemes reach fifth order (§4.1, Fig. 2). **Shown**
13. For $k=2$ (first-order critical point) WCNS-MR drops to third order. WCNS-JS does too, but it recovers fifth order for small $\Delta x$ once $\beta_k<\epsilon=10^{-6}$, and the same mechanism explains the super-convergence seen for other schemes (§4.1). **Shown**
14. The order of WCNS-Z and WCNS-MR rises from second to fifth as $k$ goes from 3 to 6 (§4.1, Fig. 2). **Shown**
15. Sod and Lax shock tubes ($N_x=100$): WCNS-EXP captures the discontinuities slightly better than the other WCNS and than FD WENO-JS, -Z and -MR with global Lax–Friedrichs splitting (§4.2.1, Figs. 3–4). **Shown**
16. CPU cost ($N_x=1000$, Table 1, hardware not stated). With the same weights WCNS and WENO cost about the same. WCNS-EXP costs slightly more than WCNS-JS and -Z and much less than WCNS-MR. Sod totals: 110.06 s (JS), 111.13 s (Z), 273.04 s (MR) and 147.37 s (EXP), with 0.15, 0.15, 0.37 and 0.20 s per RK step. The FD WENO-JS, -Z and -MR totals are 130.09, 123.73 and 289.13 s. Lax totals: 176.46, 183.87, 476.27 and 210.68 s, and 181.46, 192.91 and 465.27 s for WENO (§4.2.1). **Shown**
17. Shu–Osher problem ($N_x=200$, $t=1.8$): WCNS-EXP is slightly better than the others (§4.2.2, Fig. 5). **Shown**
18. Titarev–Toro problem ($N_x=1000$): WCNS-EXP performs like WCNS-Z, and both are better than WCNS-JS and WCNS-MR (§4.2.3, Fig. 6). **Shown**
19. 2D Riemann problem (48), $800\times800$, $t=0.8$: WCNS-EXP resolves finer structures, but it keeps the symmetry less well and oscillates more than the others. A remedy along the lines of minimized dispersion and adaptive dissipation [34] is left out of scope (§4.3.1, Fig. 7). **Shown**
20. 2D Riemann problem (49), $800\times800$, $t=0.3$: WCNS-EXP "seems" to show slightly more structures near the discontinuities (§4.3.1, Fig. 8). **Shown**
21. Double Mach reflection ($800\times200$, $t=0.2$) needs a fallback to first-order interpolation when negative density or pressure appears after the fifth-order interpolation (as in [37]). WCNS-EXP is close to WCNS-Z and slightly better than JS and MR (§4.3.2, Figs. 9–10). **Shown**
22. Forward-facing step ($600\times200$, $t=4$) also needs the first-order fallback, and WCNS-EXP resolves more fine structures than the others (§4.3.3, Fig. 11). The staggered layout puts flux points, not solution points, at the domain ends, so the step corner needs no special treatment (§2). **Shown**
23. Rayleigh–Taylor instability ($100\times400$, $t=1.95$, $\gamma=5/3$): WCNS-EXP resolves the finest structures, "the best in this example" (§4.3.4, Fig. 12). **Shown**
24. The weights carry over directly to WENO schemes (reconstruction of the flux instead of interpolation of the variables); this is left to future work (Abstract, §5). **Stated**
25. Only fifth order is treated; the extension to other orders is claimed to be straightforward (§1). **Stated**

# Printed slips

- Fig. 6 caption: "Density plots of the Titarev-Toro problem at t = 1.8", while §4.2.3 says "The computational results at t = 5 are shown in Fig. 6".
- §4.1 refers to "the chosen small number ε = 10−6 in Eq. (12)"; $\epsilon$ appears in Eq. (13).
- Eqs. (28)–(30) expand the indicators with subscript $i$ ($u_i'$, …), while the stencils (14)–(16) are centred at $j$. The coefficients are right: a Taylor expansion of (14)–(16) reproduces them *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
- Shu–Osher data (43): the paper places the shock at $x=0.5$ on $[0,10]$ with the perturbation $1+0.2\sin5x$. Titarev–Toro data (44), on the same interval with the same shock position, has the shifted perturbation $1+0.1\sin(20\pi(x-5))$. The other $[0,10]$ placements on the [Shu–Osher page](/benchmarks/shu-osher-problem.md) put the shock at $x=1$ (Fu 2021, Ye et al. 2022 and others).
- Typos: "λ is a positive parameter than can be tuned" (§3), "may loss accuracy" (§1), "slightly more expansive" (§4.2.1), "it is interested to observe" (§4.1), "to replace the obtain values" (§4.3.2). The Table 1 caption labels the total CPU time "T tol".
- Ref. [11] prints its authors as "F. Acker, R.B. de, R. Borges, B. Costa", splitting one name into two.

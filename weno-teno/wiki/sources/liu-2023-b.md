# Summary

The authors extend their [RBF differential-quadrature finite-volume](/methods/rbf-differential-quadrature.md)
method (RBFDQ-FV), previously applied only to incompressible flow, to two-dimensional inviscid
and viscous compressible flow on unstructured triangular and quadrilateral grids, and claim it
is the first such use. Each cell carries a cubic Taylor polynomial about its centre with nine
derivative coefficients ("fourth-order approximation"). The derivatives are not fitted to cell
averages, as in k-exact [reconstruction](/methods/unstructured-gradient-reconstruction.md), but
computed by local meshless differential quadrature with a normalized multiquadric radial basis
function on the cell-centre values of a neighbourhood of range at most 3. Because the unknowns
are cell-centre point values, the cell integral of the polynomial couples the time derivatives
of neighbouring values through a non-diagonal operator $\mathsf M_i$: the method is implicit
(IRBFDQ-FV). Steady problems use LU-SGS with $\mathsf M_i$ replaced by its diagonal (CFL 2000);
unsteady problems use four-stage ESDIRK with dual time stepping.

Interface fluxes are a (shock-stable) Roe flux for inviscid flow and, for viscous flow, the
discrete [gas-kinetic](/methods/gas-kinetic-scheme.md) flux solver DGKFS (D2Q4 model), which
evaluates convective and viscous fluxes together from particle states interpolated by the cubic
polynomial. Shocks are handled by a new point-extrema-based extended-bounds (PEEB) limiter that
scales the whole high-order part of the polynomial by one factor $\theta_i\in[0,1]$, computed
from extrema of the primitive variables at Gauss points, vertices and centre, with bounds of the
vertex neighbours relaxed by a tolerance $O(h^{1.5})$.

The tests show third-order gradients and second-order Laplacians, more accurate than
least-squares finite differences (LSFD) and k-exact; fourth order on the
[isentropic vortex](/benchmarks/isentropic-vortex.md), most accurate and cheapest of the three
fourth-order methods; then the [Shu–Osher problem](/benchmarks/shu-osher-problem.md), the
[Mach 3 forward-facing step](/benchmarks/forward-facing-step.md), a viscous NACA0012 at
$M=0.8$, $\mathrm{Re}=500$, the Daru–Tenaud [viscous shock tube](/benchmarks/viscous-shock-tube.md)
at $\mathrm{Re}=200$, a Blasius boundary layer on cells of aspect ratio up to 443.6, and a
hypersonic [flow past a cylinder](/benchmarks/flow-past-circular-cylinder.md) at $M=8.03$
without carbuncle. Nothing is proved; all claims are numerical.

# Key claims

Registry notation: conservative variables $u=(\rho,\rho v,E)$, here **point values** $u_i$ at
the cell centre $x_i$ of cell $\Omega_i$ (not averages), primitive variables $(\rho,v,p)$; page-local
$\varphi_{\mathrm{MQ}}$ (multiquadric), $c_{\mathrm{MQ}}$ (shape parameter), $\mathsf c^{(p)}_{ij}$
(quadrature weights), $\mathsf M_i$ (implicit mass operator), $\theta_i$ (PEEB factor),
$\kappa_{\mathrm{PEEB}}(h)$ (tolerance).

1. IRBFDQ-FV is, per the authors, the first application of the high-order RBFDQ-FV method to compressible flow on unstructured grids (§1, p. 2). **Stated**
2. The cell solution is the cubic Taylor polynomial $u(x,y)=u_i+\nabla^{(9)}u_i\cdot C_i(x,y)+O(\Delta x^4,\Delta y^4)$ with the nine derivatives $\partial_x,\partial_y,\partial_{xx},\partial_{yy},\partial_{xy},\partial_{xxx},\partial_{yyy},\partial_{xxy},\partial_{xyy}$ and the matching monomials $C_i$ (§3.1.1, Eqs. (12)–(13)). **Stated** (construction)
3. The nine derivatives are computed by local differential quadrature from the cell-centre values of the $N$ supporting cells, $\partial^ku_i=\sum_{j=1}^N\mathsf c^{(k)}_{ij}u_j$; the $9\times N$ weight matrix depends only on the point positions and is computed once on stationary meshes (§3.1.1, Eq. (16)). **Stated**
4. Substituting the polynomial into the cell integral gives $\Omega_i\,\partial_tu_i+\sum_{k=1}^9\overline{\overline C}{}^k_i\sum_j\mathsf c^{(k)}_{ij}\partial_tu_j=\mathsf M_i\,\partial_t u=-\mathcal R_i$, and "the presence of $\mathbf M_i$ suggests the implicitness of the proposed method" (§3.1.1, Eq. (17)). **Derived**
5. For positive definite radial basis functions the collocation matrix is non-singular (citing Micchelli); the multiquadric $\varphi_{\mathrm{MQ}}=\sqrt{(x-x_j)^2+(y-y_j)^2+c_{\mathrm{MQ}}^2}$ is used (§2.1–2.2, Eqs. (3), (5)). **Stated**
6. Default normalized shape parameter $c_{\mathrm{MQ}}=30d_{\min}/\sqrt N$, "based on numerous experiments" (§2.2, after Eq. (6)). **Stated** (empirical)
7. The supporting cells form the von Neumann neighbourhood of range $l\le3$ (§2.2). **Stated**
8. For $f=\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)$ on five triangular meshes ($h=1/10$ to $1/60$) the $L_2$ relative errors of the gradient and the Laplacian converge at least at the theoretical orders 3 and 2; the legend slopes are 3.667 and 3.668 (gradient) and 2.848 and 3.069 (Laplacian) for $c_{\mathrm{MQ}}=2,3$, against 3.201 and 2.303 for LSFD and 3.172 and 2.207 for k-exact, and the quadrature is the most accurate of the three (§5.1.1, Fig. 2). **Shown**
9. Isentropic vortex on four uniform triangular meshes (800 to 12800 points, $t=2$): IRBFDQ-FV, LSFD-FV and k-exact FV all reach fourth order in $L_\infty$ and $L_1$ of density, IRBFDQ-FV being the most accurate (§5.1.2, Fig. 3). **Shown**
10. For equal error IRBFDQ-FV needs less CPU time than LSFD-FV and k-exact FV (§5.1.2, Fig. 4; no numbers in the text). **Shown**
11. With the PEEB limiter switched on in the smooth vortex the $L_\infty$ and $L_1$ errors vary "only slightly" (§5.1.2, Fig. 5). **Shown**
12. Shu–Osher on triangles, $h=1/40$: IRBFDQ-FV and LSFD-FV with PEEB ($\kappa_{\mathrm{PEEB}}=100h^{1.5}$) capture the smooth extrema; the second-order scheme is more diffusive (§5.2, Fig. 6). **Shown**
13. Mach 3 forward-facing step, 108,543 triangles ($h\approx1/160$), $t=4$: the high-order method captures the Kelvin–Helmholtz roll-up along the contact discontinuity, which the second-order method does not, and separates the shock from the wall-reflected wave; the pattern agrees with k-exact results (§5.3, Fig. 8). **Shown**
14. NACA0012, $M=0.8$, $\mathrm{Re}=500$, angle of attack $10^\circ$, 5788 triangles: $C_d=0.28926$, $C_l=0.47809$; the second-order scheme gives 0.30955/0.50802 on 5788 cells and 0.29658/0.47861 on 12287 cells; the references are Yang et al. 0.28587/0.47449, Jawahar & Kamath 0.27726/0.50231 and the GAMM range 0.243–0.2868 / 0.4145–0.517 (§5.4, Table 1). **Shown**
15. On the NACA0012 the method converges faster and needs less CPU time than the second-order scheme for comparable results (§5.4, Fig. 11; no numbers in the text). **Shown**
16. Viscous shock tube, $\mathrm{Re}=200$, $\mathrm{Pr}=0.73$, $t=1$: primary-vortex height 0.166 (present, $h=1/500$), 0.166 (LSFD-FV), 0.161 (second order), against 0.168 (M-AUSMPW+), 0.166 (compact high-order GKS) and 0.165 (WENO-GKS) (§5.5, Table 2). **Shown**
17. In the viscous shock tube both high-order solvers on $h=1/250$ outperform the second-order one on $h=1/500$ in the bottom-wall density, compared with a high-order GKS reference on $1500\times750$; IRBFDQ-FV is more accurate than LSFD-FV at $h=1/500$ (§5.5, Fig. 13). **Shown**
18. Laminar boundary layer, $M=0.15$, $\mathrm{Re}=10^5$, $110\times40$ quadrilaterals of aspect ratio up to 443.6: the velocity profiles at $x/L=0.0247$, $0.0820$, $0.2625$ agree with Blasius, and at $x/L=0.0247$ the high-order scheme is more accurate than second order (§5.6, Fig. 15). **Shown**
19. In the boundary layer the implicit scheme at CFL 2000 converges faster and costs less CPU time than explicit four-stage Runge–Kutta at CFL 2 (§5.6, Fig. 16; no numbers in the text). **Shown**
20. Hypersonic cylinder, $M=8.03$, $T_\infty=124.94$ K, wall at 294.44 K, $\mathrm{Re}=1.835\times10^5$ on the radius, $60\times160$ quadrilaterals with first spacing 1/2000: the bow shock is captured "without the carbuncle phenomenon", and the wall pressure and heat flux agree with Wieting's experiment and a gas-kinetic BGK solution, the heat flux better than at second order (§5.7, Figs. 17–18). **Shown**
21. DGKFS evaluates inviscid and viscous fluxes together, avoiding the separate viscous-flux treatment of DG and spectral-volume methods, and is simpler and cheaper than Maxwellian-based gas-kinetic schemes (§1, p. 3; §3.1.2). **Stated**
22. DGKFS is locally second order, $O(\delta_t^2)$ in its streaming step, but since $\delta_t$ is much smaller than the mesh spacing "the overall accuracy would not be affected by such local errors" (§3.1.2, after Eq. (19)). **Stated** (no viscous convergence test is reported; the fourth-order test is inviscid with the Roe flux)
23. The Roe scheme is chosen "due to its simplicity and good positivity property for problems with strong shock wave and expansion wave" (§1, p. 3); the cited form is the shock-stable Roe scheme of Kim et al. **Stated**
24. PEEB is called a "positivity preserved limiting procedure" (§1, p. 3); no proof or positivity test is given. **Stated**
25. The limiter $u(x,y)=u_i+\theta_i\big(\nabla^{(9)}u_i\cdot C_i\big)$ (Eq. (41)) is applied to the conservative variables "for mass conservation at the discrete level", the bounds being computed on primitive variables (§4). **Stated** *(compiler check, not in the sources: by Eq. (14) the cell integral of the polynomial is $\Omega_iu_i+\nabla^{(9)}u_i\cdot\overline{\overline C}_i$, so scaling by $\theta_i<1$ changes it by $(\theta_i-1)\nabla^{(9)}u_i\cdot\overline{\overline C}_i$ unless the centre value is redefined; the flux-form update itself stays conservative.)*
26. The tolerance $\kappa_{\mathrm{PEEB}}(h)=O(h^{1.5})$ is chosen "based on extensive numerical tests" to balance oscillation suppression and accuracy in smooth regions (§4, after Eq. (37)). **Stated** (empirical)
27. The method "outperforms the conventional second-order FV solver" and shows "superior accuracy and efficiency" to the k-exact and LSFD finite-volume methods of the same order (§6). **Shown** (by the comparisons of §5)

# Printed slips

- §5, introduction: "four classic benchmark cases" are announced, while §§5.2–5.7 present six, and §1 says "Six more steady and unsteady numerical experiments".
- The PEEB tolerance is $\kappa(h)$ in §4 and "$\alpha=100h^{1.5}$" in §5.2.
- Eq. (7) divides the normalized weights by $D_i$ for every derivative order $p$. *(compiler check, not in the sources: with the coordinates normalized by $D_i$ as in Eq. (6), $\partial_x=D_i^{-1}\partial_{\bar x}$, so a $p$-th derivative scales as $D_i^{-p}$.)* Either a slip or an unstated convention.
- Eq. (17) closes the parenthesis after $\sum_jw^k_{ij}$, leaving $\partial\mathbf W_{ij}/\partial t$ outside the $j$-sum with a free index.
- Eq. (19) uses $e_d$ in $e_{p,d}=(2-\gamma)e_d$ without defining it; §3.1.1 calls $e=P/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$ the "potential energy of the mean flow", which is the specific internal energy.
- §5.6: a maximum aspect ratio of 443.6 is stated with "minimum cell sizes $\Delta x_{\min}=0.08$ and $\Delta y_{\min}=0.05$", whose ratio is 1.6; the values or their units are misprinted, and the plate length is not given.
- §2.2 gives the default $c=30d_{\min}/\sqrt N$, §5.1.1 tests "$c=2$ and $c=3$" without saying whether these are normalized values.
- Fig. 2: the slopes printed in the legends (3.667, 3.668 for gradients; 2.848, 3.069 for Laplacians) exceed the theoretical orders 3 and 2 that the text says they "agree well with".
- "gird points" for "grid points" (§4); ref. [61] prints the first author as "M. Pandol" for Pandolfi.

# Summary

The paper introduces **multirate exponential Rosenbrock (MERB)** methods
([multirate exponential methods](/methods/multirate-exponential-methods.md)),
a class of [multirate infinitesimal methods](/methods/multirate-infinitesimal-methods.md)
for $y'=f(t,y)$ in which the fast/slow splitting is not fixed. At each macro
step $H$ the right-hand side is linearized about the current solution,
$f=J_ny+V_nt+N_n(t,y)$. The linear part $J_ny$ is the fast operator and the
rest is slow. The starting point is an explicit exponential Rosenbrock
(ExpRB) method ([exponential integrators](/methods/exponential-integrators.md))
whose weights are linear combinations of $\phi_k$ functions. Lemma 2.2 shows
that each ExpRB stage and the update are exact solutions of linear ODEs
$v'=J_nv+p(\theta)$ whose forcing is a polynomial in the local time
$\theta$, built from slow differences $D_{nj}$. Solving these fast ODEs
with any inner solver at micro step $h=H/m$ gives MERB. No matrix functions
are needed, at the price of a new linearization every macro step. This
extends the authors' MERK methods (2020), which require a fixed linear
autonomous fast operator $Ly$.

Theorem 2.12 gives $\lVert\hat y_n-y(t_n)\rVert\le CH^p+CHh^{r_{\mathrm s}}+Ch^{r_{\mathrm f}}$,
so order $p$ needs stage-ODE solvers of order $r_{\mathrm s}\ge p-1$ and a
final-ODE solver of order $r_{\mathrm f}\ge p$; the paper states that
MRI-GARK needs both $\ge p$. ExpRB methods have few
[stiff order conditions](/results/exponential-runge-kutta-stiff-order-conditions.md):
7 for order 6, against 36 for Runge–Kutta or exponential Runge–Kutta
methods. The paper generalizes exprb32, exprb42, pexprb54s4 and pexprb65s7
to non-autonomous form with free nodes and derives MERB2–MERB6, which need
1, 2, 2, 3 and 3 modified fast ODEs per slow step. MERB6 is claimed as the
first sixth-order infinitesimal multirate method.

The experiments are a 1D reaction–diffusion front and a new
nonautonomous bidirectional coupling problem. They compare MERB3–6 with
MERK3–5 and MRI-GARK-ERK33a/45a ([IMEX-MRI-GARK](/methods/imex-mri-gark.md) is
cited only), each competitor run with dynamic and fixed splittings. All
methods reach their nominal orders. MERB is the most efficient in slow
function calls and in MATLAB runtime. MRI-GARK is the most efficient in
total function calls, because its fast traversal per step is $1.0H$.

# Key claims

Registry notation: $y$ the solution (source $u$), $f$ the right-hand side
(source $F$), $H$, $h=H/m$, $m$ the macro step, micro step and multirate
ratio (agree), $Y_i$ the ExpRB stages (source $U_{ni}$), $\phi_k$ (source
$\varphi_k$, same normalisation), $L$ MERK's fixed linear fast operator.
Page-local: $V_n=\partial_tf(t_n,y_n)$, $N_n=f-J_ny-V_nt$,
$D_{ni}=N_n(t_n+c_iH,Y_i)-N_n(t_n,y_n)$, $\theta$ the local fast time
(source $\tau$; as on [multirate exponential methods](/methods/multirate-exponential-methods.md)), $r_{\mathrm s}$ and $r_{\mathrm f}$ the orders of the stage-ODE
and final-ODE solvers (source $q$, $r$). Hats denote MERB quantities.

1. MERB methods are infinitesimal multirate methods built by reading
   explicit ExpRB stages as exact solutions of modified linear fast ODEs,
   with no fixed linear fast operator. The split is redone at each step by
   dynamic linearization (§1, §2.2, Eqs. 2.1, 2.14–2.17). **Derived**
2. The linearization residual satisfies
   $\partial_yN_n(t_n,y_n)=0$ and $\partial_tN_n(t_n,y_n)=0$ (Eq. 2.7). Hence
   $D_{ni}=O(H^2)$ and every ExpRB method has order at least 2
   (Remark 2.1). **Derived**
3. An ExpRB method of order 6 needs only 7 stiff order conditions, "much
   less than the 36 conditions needed for explicit Runge–Kutta or
   exponential Runge–Kutta methods of the same order" (Remark 2.1,
   Table 1, from Luan & Ostermann 2013). **Stated**
4. Lemma 2.2: if $a_{ij}(HJ_n)=\sum_k\alpha^{(k)}_{ij}\phi_k(c_iHJ_n)$ and
   $b_i(HJ_n)=\sum_k\beta^{(k)}_i\phi_k(HJ_n)$, then $Y_i=v_{ni}(c_iH)$ and
   $y_{n+1}=v_{n+1}(H)$ for the linear ODEs (2.9) with the polynomial
   forcings (2.10) (§2.2). **Proved** (sketched by reference to the MERK
   paper, Thm 3.1)
5. Theorem 2.4 writes the MERB global error exactly as Error1 + Error2 +
   Error3 + Error4 (Eq. 2.30): linearization drift acting on $y_0$,
   propagated ExpRB local errors, propagated inner-solver errors of the
   final ODE (2.17), and the difference of the stage-coupling terms.
   **Proved**
6. Theorem 2.12 (Assumptions 1–2: $J$ generates a strongly continuous
   semigroup with $\lVert e^{tJ}\rVert\le Ce^{\omega t}$; smooth solution and
   $f$): a MERB method on an ExpRB method of global order $p$, with stage
   ODEs solved at order $r_{\mathrm s}$ and the final ODE at order
   $r_{\mathrm f}$ with micro step $h=H/m$, satisfies
   $\lVert\hat y_n-y(t_n)\rVert\le CH^p+CHh^{r_{\mathrm s}}+Ch^{r_{\mathrm f}}$
   (Eq. 2.55). **Proved**. The proof bounds the inner-solver errors through
   $\lVert\hat J_k\rVert\le L_J$ (page-local; source $L$) and $\phi_1(L_JH)$
   (Eq. 2.57), a bounded-Jacobian assumption on the fast operator, so the
   constants are not uniform in the fast stiffness *(compiler check, not in
   the sources: the constant $L_J$ enters the bound)*.
7. For fixed $m$ the bound is
   $CH^p+\frac C{m^{r_{\mathrm s}}}H^{r_{\mathrm s}+1}+\frac C{m^{r_{\mathrm f}}}H^{r_{\mathrm f}}$,
   so order $p$ needs $r_{\mathrm s}\ge p-1$ and $r_{\mathrm f}\ge p$, "an
   improvement compared to MRI-GARK methods, that in fact require both" to be
   $\ge p$ (Remark 2.13). **Derived** (MERB part); **Stated** (MRI-GARK
   requirement)
8. The MERB bound is "similar to the one obtained with MERK methods", and
   the analysis is "much more elegant" because it splits the error into
   MERB-vs-ExpRB and ExpRB-vs-exact parts (Remark 2.13, §4). **Stated**
9. MERB2, from the exponential Rosenbrock–Euler scheme, needs 1 modified ODE
   per slow step (§2.4.1, Eq. 2.59). **Derived**
10. MERB3 is a one-parameter family in $c_2$ from a generalized exprb32. It
    needs 2 modified ODEs per step, against 3 for third-order MERK and
    MRI-GARK. With $c_2=1/2$ the total fast traversal is $1.5H$ (§2.4.2,
    Eqs. 2.60–2.61). **Derived**
11. MERB4 comes from exprb42 (Luan 2017), made non-autonomous, with
    $c_2=3/4$. It needs 2 modified ODEs per step, against 5 for fourth-order
    MRI-GARK and 4 for MERK4; its fast traversal is $1.75H$ (§2.4.3,
    Eqs. 2.62–2.63). **Derived**
12. MERB5 is a family from pexprb54s4 with free $c_2,c_3$ and
    $c_4=\frac{3(5c_3-4)}{5(4c_3-3)}$. Stages $Y_3$, $Y_4$ are independent and
    share one forcing polynomial. It needs 3 modified ODEs per step; "the only
    existing fifth-order multirate method, MERK5, requires 5". The tests use
    $c_2=c_4=1/4<c_3=33/40$, with traversal $\tfrac{83}{40}H=2.075H$
    (§2.4.4, Eqs. 2.64–2.65). **Derived**
13. MERB6 is a 7-stage family from pexprb65s7, extended to non-autonomous
    form with free distinct nodes. It is "the first-ever sixth-order
    infinitesimal multirate method" and needs 3 modified ODEs per step. The
    tests use $c_3=c_5=1/10<c_2=c_6=1/9<c_7=1/8<c_4=1/7$, with traversal
    $\tfrac{79}{63}H\approx1.253H$ (§2.4.5, Eq. 2.66). **Derived**
    (construction); **Stated** (priority). The MERB6 coefficients $a_{i2},a_{i3}$ make conditions 4, 6 and 7 hold
    and the printed forcings follow from (2.10) *(compiler check by hand,
    not in the sources)*.
14. Before this work no infinitesimal method rooted in partitioned
    Runge–Kutta theory (MIS, RMIS, extended MIS, MRI-GARK, IMEX-MRI-GARK) had
    order above four, because the number of order conditions grows
    exponentially (§1). **Stated**
15. Stages with the same forcing polynomial come from one fast solve over
    the longest interval, read off at intermediate times ($c_4<c_3$ in
    MERB5; $c_3<c_2$ and $c_5,c_6,c_7<c_4$ in MERB6) (§2.4.4–2.4.5).
    **Derived**
16. Supplying $\hat N_n$ and $\hat D_{ni}$ analytically avoids cancellation
    at tight tolerances. Products $Jw$ and $V\theta$ can be formed by finite
    differences of $f$, but at high accuracy this causes "excessive
    floating-point cancellation error" (§2.5, Algorithm 2.1). **Stated**
17. Table 2, (slow stages, modified ODEs, fast traversal in units of $H$):
    MERB3 (2, 2, 1.5), MERK3 (3, 3, 2.166), MRI-GARK33a (3, 3, 1), MERB4 (2,
    2, 1.75), MERK4 (6, 4, 2.833), MRI-GARK45a (5, 5, 1), MERB5 (4, 3,
    2.075), MERK5 (10, 5, 3.2), MERB6 (7, 3, 1.253). **Stated**
18. Optimal $m$ (Table 2; reaction–diffusion dynamic/fixed, bidirectional
    dynamic/fixed): MERB3 10/–, 80/–; MERK3 20/10, 80/10; MRI-GARK33a 20/5,
    80/10; MERB4 10/–, 40/–; MERK4 20/10, 40/10; MRI-GARK45a 10/1, 40/1;
    MERB5 5/–, 10/–; MERK5 5/5, 10/10; MERB6 5/–, 5/–. Dynamic linearization
    "almost exclusively" gives a larger optimal $m$ than fixed linearization
    (§3.3). **Shown**
19. Reaction–diffusion, measured rates (Figs. 1–3; * = fixed splitting):
    MERB3 3.07, MERK3 2.79, MERK3* 3.16, MRI-GARK33a 2.71, MRI-GARK33a* 3.22;
    MERB4 3.66, MERK4 3.71, MERK4* 5.25, MRI-GARK45a 3.83, MRI-GARK45a* 4.64;
    MERB5 4.7, MERK5 4.47, MERK5* 5.15, MERB6 6.15. **Shown**
20. Bidirectional coupling, measured rates (Figs. 4–6): MERB3 3.44, MERK3
    3.13, MERK3* 3.1, MRI-GARK33a 3.14, MRI-GARK33a* 3.19; MERB4 3.74, MERK4
    4.0, MERK4* 4.01, MRI-GARK45a 3.84, MRI-GARK45a* 4.11; MERB5 5.11, MERK5
    4.91, MERK5* 5.02, MERB6 5.81. **Shown**
21. Dynamic linearization gives lower errors than fixed linearization on
    both problems; on the bidirectional problem the gap at equal $H$ is "up
    to $10^3$" for third-order methods (§3.5–3.6). **Shown**
22. MERB methods are "uniformly the most efficient when considering slow
    function calls" and have the best MATLAB runtime, with same-order MERK
    under dynamic splitting sometimes competitive. MRI-GARK always wins in
    total function calls (§3.5–3.6, §4). **Shown** (MATLAB on unstated
    hardware; the authors call runtime "a poor proxy for runtimes on HPC
    applications")
23. On reaction–diffusion MERB5 is the most efficient of the fifth- and
    sixth-order methods in all three measures, and MERB6 starts with larger
    errors. On the bidirectional problem MERB6 overtakes MERB5 in runtime at
    small errors and is the most efficient in total calls (§3.5–3.6,
    Figs. 3, 6). **Shown**
24. MERK4* (fixed linearization) converges at fifth order on the
    reaction–diffusion problem (rate 5.25, §3.5). **Shown**
25. Future work: embedded methods for error estimation, multirate step
    adaptivity, implicit or IMEX slow treatment (§4). **Stated**

# Printed slips

- Eq. (2.62), exprb42 weight: "$H\frac{16}9\varphi_3(HJ_n)D_{n2}$" with
  $c_2=\frac34$. Condition 1 of Table 1 requires $b_2c_2^2=2\phi_3$, i.e.
  $\frac{32}9\phi_3$. The MERB4 forcing (2.63) is consistent with $\frac{32}9$,
  so (2.62) misprints it.
- Eq. (2.64), MERB5 base: $b_3=\frac1{c_3^2(c_4-c_3)}(c_4\varphi_3-6\varphi_4)$.
  Conditions 1–2 require $2c_4\phi_3$, as in the printed $b_4$ and in the
  forcing (2.65).
- §3.6, fixed splitting: the slow part prints $(w-\beta t)$ in both squared
  brackets where (3.3c) has $(w+\beta t)$. The exact solution confirms
  (3.3c).
- Eqs. (2.23a), (2.27), (2.38a), (2.38b): $\varphi_2(HJ_n)$ or
  $\varphi_2(c_iHJ_n)$ without the hat or tilde that the neighbouring terms
  carry.
- Eqs. (2.23a)–(2.23b) write the ExpRB step with values of $\tilde N_n$
  rather than the differences $D_{ni}$ of (2.5); the two forms are not
  reconciled in the text.
- Proof of Corollary 2.11 invokes "the stability bound (2.19)" where the
  discrete bound (2.20) is needed.
- §1 attributes Lie–Trotter splitting to ref. [20], McLachlan & Quispel's
  2002 review.
- Remark 2.1: "36 conditions" for explicit Runge–Kutta methods of order 6;
  the rooted trees with at most 6 vertices number 37 *(compiler count, not in
  the sources)*.
- Ref. [7] prints the first author as "D. E. K. et al." (Keyes et al.,
  IJHPCA 2013).
- §3.5: "between the dynamic fixed linearization approaches" lacks "and";
  abstract: "the highest order ever constructed infinitesimal multirate
  methods".

# Summary

The paper studies the convergence factor of [Parareal](/methods/parareal.md)
for linear parabolic problems $y'=Ly+g(t)$ with $-L$ self-adjoint positive
definite. With backward Euler as coarse propagator and any strongly stable
high-order one-step fine propagator, Yang, Yuan & Zhou proved a mesh-robust
contraction with factor about 0.3. Friedhoff & Southworth report about 0.26
for SDIRK-22 and 0.15 for SDIRK-33 on both levels. The authors ask whether a
better coarse propagator beats this barrier.

In the eigenbasis of $L$ the error recursion has the componentwise factor
$\kappa(s)=\bigl(R_{\mathrm f}(-s/J)^J-R_{\mathcal G}(-s)\bigr)/\bigl(1-|R_{\mathcal G}(-s)|\bigr)$, $s=-\Delta T\lambda$, where
$R_{\mathrm f}$ and $R_{\mathcal G}$ are the fine and coarse
[stability functions](/definitions/stability-function.md). Theorem 3.2 proves
$\max_j\lVert U^{(k)}_j-y_{jJ}\rVert\le c\,\kappa_c^k$ with
$\kappa_c=\sup_s|\kappa(s)|$, also for nonsmooth initial data. The paper then
**optimizes** a rational $R_{\mathcal G}$ of degree $(2,2)$ to minimize $\kappa_c$ for a
given fine propagator. The constraints are consistency of order $p$
(linear in the coefficients) and $|R_{\mathcal G}|<1$ on the negative real axis
including infinity, which is strong stability in the sense of
[A- and L-stability](/properties/a-stability.md), enforced at the critical
points of $R_{\mathcal G}$ by a barrier method. A bound shows the factor varies by at
most 0.055 for step ratios between 16 and 128 (2-stage Lobatto IIIC fine
propagator).

The optimized coarse propagators reach $\kappa_c\approx0.014$, against 0.298 for
backward Euler and 0.262 for SDIRK-22. This holds for 2-, 3- and 4-stage
Lobatto IIIC and 3-stage Radau IIA fine propagators
([fully implicit Runge–Kutta](/methods/fully-implicit-runge-kutta.md)), nearly
uniformly in the step ratio. Tests on the 1D [heat equation](/benchmarks/heat-equation.md)
with smooth and nonsmooth data, the [Allen–Cahn equation](/benchmarks/allen-cahn-equation.md)
and the viscous [Burgers equation](/benchmarks/burgers-equation.md) observe
contraction near 0.04 against 0.3, and simulated Parareal efficiencies 2 to
3.5 times higher. The fine stages of the nonlinear tests are solved by
Newton ([implicit stage solvers](/methods/implicit-stage-solvers.md)). The
gain shrinks for stiff nonlinearities with large step ratios. The paper
belongs to [parallel-in-time integration](/methods/parallel-in-time-integration.md).
No parallel run is made.

# Key claims

Registry notation: $L=-A$ (source $u'+Au=f$, $A$ positive definite), step
$h$ (source $\Delta t$), slice length $\Delta T=Jh$, number of slices $N$
(source $N_c$), slices $T_j$, iterate $U^{(k)}_j$ (source $U^n_k$), fine
solution $y_{jJ}$ at $T_j$ (source $u^{nJ}$), propagators $\mathcal F$,
$\mathcal G$, order $p$ (source $q$). Page-local: step ratio $J=\Delta T/h$ (kept from the source, as on
[Parareal](/methods/parareal.md); not the registry Jacobian), fine and coarse stability functions $R_{\mathrm f}$, $R_{\mathcal G}$ with the source's
$r(s)=R_{\mathrm f}(-s)$ and $R(s)=R_{\mathcal G}(-s)$, scaled eigenvalue $s=-\Delta T\lambda\ge0$
for an eigenvalue $\lambda<0$ of $L$, componentwise factor $\kappa(s)$ and
convergence factor $\kappa_c$.

1. For a one-step fine propagator satisfying (P1)–(P3), the Parareal error
   in each eigencomponent obeys
   $e^{(k+1)}_{j+1}=R_{\mathcal G}(-s)e^{(k+1)}_j+\bigl(R_{\mathrm f}(-s/J)^J-R_{\mathcal G}(-s)\bigr)e^{(k)}_j$,
   hence $|e^{(k+1)}_{j+1}|\le\sup_s|\kappa(s)|\max_j|e^{(k)}_j|$ (§3.1, Eq. (3.1)).
   **Derived**
2. Theorem 3.2: under (P1)–(P3) and the data regularity of Lemma 2.1,
   $\max_{1\le j\le N}\lVert U^{(k)}_j-y_{jJ}\rVert\le c\,\kappa_c^k$; Parareal converges
   if $\kappa_c<1$ (§3.1). **Proved**
3. With backward Euler as coarse propagator, Yang, Yuan & Zhou proved a
   threshold ratio, independent of $\Delta T$, $h$ and the spectrum, above
   which the contraction factor is about 0.3, observed sharp (§2.2, Eq.
   (2.4)). **Stated**
4. Friedhoff & Southworth report mesh-independent factors of about 0.26
   for SDIRK-22 and 0.15 for SDIRK-33 on both levels, $J\in\{4,\dots,64\}$
   (§2.2). **Stated**
5. A fine propagator with $|R_{\mathrm f}(-\infty)|=1$ (Crank–Nicolson, Gauss)
   needs a bounded spectrum and a large enough $J$ for convergence, so such
   propagators are "seldom used" in Parareal (§2.1, Remark 3.1).
   **Stated**
6. Consistency of order $p$ of the parametric coarse function
   $R_{\mathcal G}(-\lambda)=\sum_{i=0}^na_i\lambda^i/\bigl(1+\sum_{i=1}^me^{b_i}\lambda^i\bigr)$ is equivalent
   to linear relations that eliminate $a_0,\dots,a_p$ (§3.2, Eq. (3.5)).
   **Derived**
7. $|R_{\mathcal G}|<1$ on the whole negative real axis is enforced exactly by
   checking only the positive critical points of $R_{\mathcal G}(-\lambda)$ (closed form
   for $m=n=2$) and the point at infinity, through a log-barrier (§3.2,
   Eq. (3.7)). **Derived**
8. Proposition 3.3: every optimal parameter gives a coarse propagator that
   is consistent of order $p$ and strongly stable, $|R_{\mathcal G}(-\infty)|<1$ (§3.2).
   **Proved**
9. Remark 3.4: the $(2,2)$ form loses accuracy to round-off because of the
   squared operator in the numerator; a rewritten form reaches nearly
   machine precision (§3.2). **Stated** (no numbers shown)
10. Theorem 3.5: if the fine propagator satisfies (P1)–(P3),
    $J\mapsto R_{\mathrm f}(-s/J)^J$ is strictly convex and $R_{\mathrm f}\ge0$ on the
    negative real axis, then for $16\le N_1\le N_2\le128$ the factor
    $\sup_s|\kappa|$ at $N_2$ exceeds that at $N_1$ by at most
    $112\sup_s|h_\kappa(s)|$, with $h_\kappa$ the sensitivity (3.9) (§3.3).
    **Proved**
11. The convexity assumption holds for 2- and 4-stage Lobatto IIIC and
    3-stage Radau IIA; the 3-stage Lobatto IIIC stability function changes
    sign and violates $R_{\mathrm f}\ge0$ (§3.3, Remark 3.7). **Stated** (proved for
    2-stage Lobatto IIIC only)
12. Corollary 3.6: for the 2-stage Lobatto IIIC fine propagator,
    $R_{\mathrm f}(-s)=2/(s^2+2s+2)$, and its optimized coarse propagator,
    $|h_\kappa|\le4.91\times10^{-4}$, so the factor varies by at most 0.055 for
    $J\in[16,128]$ (§3.3). **Proved** (with numerically evaluated constants)
13. Numerically, $112\sup|h_\kappa|$ is $1.10\times10^{-2}$, $2.20\times10^{-8}$ and
    $5.20\times10^{-7}$ for 2-stage Lobatto IIIC, 4-stage Lobatto IIIC and
    3-stage Radau IIA, and $\sup_{J\in[16,128]}\sup_s|\kappa|$ is about
    $1.35$–$1.40\times10^{-2}$ for all four fine propagators (Fig. 3.1).
    **Shown**
14. Optimized factor $\max_s\kappa$ (Table 4.1): backward Euler 0.264–0.299,
    SDIRK-22 0.261–0.269, optimized coarse propagators 0.013–0.020 over
    $J\in\{2,4,16,64,128\}$ and all four fine propagators; e.g. 2-stage
    Lobatto IIIC, $J=16$: 0.298, 0.262 and 0.013. **Shown**
15. §3 states a factor of about 0.02 for $J\in[16,128]$; Table 4.1 gives
    0.013–0.014 there, and 0.020 only at $J=2$ (§3, Table 4.1). **Shown**
16. For an A-stable θ-scheme fine propagator ($\theta=0.52$) an optimized
    $(3,3)$ coarse propagator gives about 0.10 against 0.298 for backward
    Euler (§4.1, App. B). **Shown**
17. The factor curves of the optimized propagators have sharp, narrow kinks
    near zero (super-fast convergence at isolated frequencies) that are not
    robust (§4.1, Fig. 4.1). **Shown**
18. Heat equation, smooth data, $J=20,100$, $h=1/500$: optimized
    propagators converge much faster than backward Euler and robustly in
    $J$; backward Euler shows its predicted rate 0.3 at $J=100$; with
    $N=5$ slices the error vanishes after 5 iterations (finite
    termination) (§4.2, Fig. 4.2). **Shown**
19. Heat equation, discontinuous $y_0$: rates agree with Theorem 3.2; to
    reach $10^{-8}$ the optimized propagator needs 2 fewer iterations than
    backward Euler at $J=20$ and 9 fewer at $J=100$ (§4.2, Fig. 4.3).
    **Shown**
20. Heat equation, $T=100$, $J=100$, target $10^{-7}$ (Table 4.2):
    iterations 10/4, 11/4 and 10/4 (backward Euler / optimized) for 3-stage
    Lobatto IIIC, 3-stage Radau IIA and 4-stage Lobatto IIIC fine
    propagators; speedup including coarse cost 11.48 against 25.52, 4.81
    against 11.03, 3.93 against 10.35; efficiency 7.65% against 17.02%,
    10.68% against 24.51%, 13.09% against 34.51%. **Shown**
21. Allen–Cahn, $\varepsilon^2=1$, $J=20,100$, $h=1/2000$: observed rate about
    0.04 with optimized propagators against 0.3 with backward Euler (§4.2,
    Fig. 4.4). **Shown**
22. Allen–Cahn, $\varepsilon^2=0.02$, $h=10^{-4}$: rate about 0.04 for $J=10,20$,
    but "for larger $J$, the difference between the BE scheme and OCPs is
    small", which calls for nonlinear modifications (§4.2, Fig. 4.5).
    **Shown**
23. Allen–Cahn, $\varepsilon^2=1$, $T=10$, $J=20$, target $10^{-6}$, coarse
    substeps $\Delta T/5$ (Table 4.3): iterations 6/3, 11/5, 11/3; speedup
    10.93/18.78, 3.65/7.90, 3.43/11.63; efficiency 7.29%/12.52%,
    7.27%/15.80%, 8.59%/29.08%. **Shown**
24. Viscous Burgers: viscosity 1, $J=20,100$, $h=1/2000$: optimized rate
    about 0.04. Viscosity 0.02, $h=10^{-4}$: at $J=10$ about 0.06 against
    0.21 for backward Euler; at $J=100$ both are slow and the optimized
    propagator is "still competitive" (§4.2, Figs. 4.6–4.7). **Shown**
25. The optimization uses no training data, unlike neural-network coarse
    propagators (§1). **Stated**
26. There is no parallel implementation and communication is ignored: the
    cost per iteration is the sequential coarse time plus the maximum fine
    time over slices (§4). No hardware is given. **Stated**

*(Compiler checks, not in the sources.)* The four $(2,2)$ optimized propagators of App. B are
nearly identical,
$R_{\mathcal G}(-\lambda)\approx(1-0.21\lambda+0.005\lambda^2)/(1+0.79\lambda+0.38\lambda^2)$, so they are in
practice independent of the fine propagator, as expected since
$R_{\mathrm f}(-s/J)^J\approx e^{-s}$ for any accurate fine method at $J\ge16$.
All reported optimized propagators have order $p=1$: the gain is in the
damping profile, not in accuracy.

# Summary

The paper studies [explicit–implicit–null (EIN) time marching](/methods/explicit-implicit-null-method.md) for PDEs whose highest spatial derivative has order $r=2,3,4$: nonlinear diffusion, KdV-type dispersion and biharmonic-type dissipation. An explicit method needs $h=O(\Delta x^r)$. An implicit or [IMEX](/methods/imex-runge-kutta.md) method that treats a nonlinear highest-derivative term implicitly needs a Newton solve at every stage. EIN adds and subtracts a linear, constant-coefficient term $a_1\partial_x^rU$ and treats only the added copy implicitly. Everything else, the subtracted copy and all nonlinear terms included, is explicit. Each implicit stage is then a linear constant-coefficient solve, and the parabolic (or dispersive) step restriction disappears. The coefficient is $a_1=a_0\max(\text{coefficient of the highest derivative})$, and the question is the smallest admissible ratio $a_0$. The approach is an alternative to the [linearly implicit IMEX methods](/methods/semi-implicit-imex-runge-kutta.md), which also make the implicit stages linear.

The time integrator is one five-stage, third-order IMEX pair, taken from Wang, Zhang, Wang & Shu (2020). Entry by entry it is ARS(4,4,3), though the paper does not name it so. Space is discretized by fourth-order central finite differences (composites of a fourth-order first-difference operator) and by $P^2$ LDG. A von Neumann analysis of the linear models $U_t-aU_{xx}=0$, $U_t+aU_{xxx}=0$ and $U_t+aU_{xxxx}=0$ (periodic) evaluates the amplification factor or matrix numerically for $h/\Delta x^r$ from $10^{-10}$ to $10^{10}$. The schemes are unconditionally stable iff $a_0\ge0.54$, with the same threshold for all three equations and both spatial discretizations. The threshold depends on the IMEX pair, not visibly on the space discretization.

Experiments in 1D and 2D with $h=\Delta x$ cover linear and nonlinear diffusion, convection–diffusion of [Burgers](/benchmarks/burgers-equation.md) type, the [Korteweg–de Vries equation](/benchmarks/korteweg-de-vries-equation.md) and its generalizations, Zakharov–Kuznetsov, nonlinear biharmonic and Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equations. They show third order at $a_0=0.54$, blow-up on fine meshes at $a_0=0.53$, and much larger errors at $a_0=10$. An appendix proves $L^2$ stability of a modified semi-discrete LDG scheme for $U_t+(a(U)U_{xx})_{xx}=0$.

# Key claims

Notation: $h$ is the step and $\Delta x$ the mesh width (source $\tau$ and $h$). The added implicit term gives $f^{\mathrm I}$ and the rest $f^{\mathrm E}$ (source $\mathcal L$ and $\mathcal N$), with tableaux $(A^{\mathrm I},b^{\mathrm I})$ and $(A^{\mathrm E},b^{\mathrm E})$. $a_1$ is the coefficient of the added term and $a_0$ its ratio to the largest highest-derivative coefficient (source notation kept).

1. For a $k$-th order PDE ($k\ge2$), explicit time marching suffers $h=O(\Delta x^k)$; implicit and IMEX methods with an implicitly treated nonlinear highest-derivative term need a nonlinear algebraic solve each step. (§1, abstract) **Stated**
2. The EIN method adds and subtracts a sufficiently large linear highest-derivative term with constant coefficient and applies an IMEX method to the equivalent equation, with the added term implicit and the rest explicit. It needs no nonlinear iterative solver and removes the explicit step restriction. (§1, Eq. (1.4)) **Stated**
3. The name EIN is due to Duchemin & Eggers [16]; the added term must "have the same scaling in wave number as the most stiff term". The method was first used by Douglas & Dupont [15], later for level-set mean-curvature motion [30], molecular-beam-epitaxy growth [33], Boltzmann equations with stiff sources [18] and Cahn–Hilliard [29], all at low order. (§1) **Stated**
4. At low order EIN can be viewed as the "stabilization approach" [25], but not at high order, where the stabilizing term EIN introduces is "much more complex" than that of the stabilization method [11, 20, 24]. (§1) **Stated**
5. Wang, Zhang, Wang & Shu [32] applied EIN pairs up to order three with LDG for diffusion but analysed stability only for the first- and second-order schemes. (§1) **Stated**
6. For dispersive equations the sign of the added term $\pm a_1U_{xxx}$ must follow the wind direction. If the wind direction changes inside a cell, $a_1$ may need to vary in space and a central flux replace the upwind flux for the auxiliary terms. The analysis covers a fixed wind direction only. (§2.2) **Stated**
7. The time integrator is the five-stage IMEX scheme (2.16)–(2.17) of [32], third order. (§2.4) **Stated** (order asserted, not verified in the paper)
8. Von Neumann criterion: $\lvert G\rvert\le1$ for all $\xi\in[-\pi,\pi]$ implies stability of the EIN finite-difference scheme (Lemma 3.1); for LDG, a uniformly diagonalizable amplification matrix $G$ with spectral radius $\le1$ (Lemma 3.2). (§3) **Stated**
9. A larger $a_0$ gives a larger error, because the two equal added terms are treated differently (one explicitly, one implicitly); too small an $a_0$ loses stability; the goal is the smallest $a_0$. (§3, after Lemma 3.1) **Stated** (heuristic, confirmed numerically in §4)
10. Numerical search: $\lvert G\rvert\le1+10^{-10}$ is tested on a discrete set of $\xi\in[-\pi,\pi]$ for $h/\Delta x^r=10^{-10},10^{-9},\dots,10^{10}$; the smallest admissible $a_0$ is $0.54$, which "verifies that the scheme is unconditionally stable if $a_0\ge0.54$". The code is in Matlab. (§3) **Shown** (numerical evaluation of the exact amplification factor, not a proof)
11. Proposition 3.1: with the EIN time discretization (2.17), the finite-difference schemes (2.2) for $U_t-aU_{xx}=0$, (2.13) for $U_t+aU_{xxx}=0$ and (2.15) for $U_t+aU_{xxxx}=0$ all have the same lower bound $a_0=0.54$ for unconditional stability ("Somewhat surprisingly"). (§3) **Shown**
12. Proposition 3.2: the same bound $0.54$ holds for the $P^2$ LDG schemes (2.10), (A.5), (A.9) for the same three equations. (§3) **Shown**
13. Remark 3.1: the result also holds for finite-difference and LDG schemes of other spatial orders with the same IMEX method; details not included. **Stated**
14. Remark 3.3: the same threshold holds in 2D and 3D by a similar von Neumann analysis; details omitted. **Stated**
15. Remark 3.4: the threshold depends on the IMEX time discretization but seems insensitive to the spatial discretization; $a_0=0.54$ may not hold for another IMEX method. **Stated**
16. The authors analysed other IMEX methods by Fourier analysis with "similar stability conclusions", not reported. (§1, §2.4) **Stated** (not shown)
17. 1D linear diffusion (4.1), $h=\Delta x$, $T=1$: the finite-difference scheme with $a_0=0.54$ reaches $L^1$ error $3.16\times10^{-10}$ at $N=1280$ with order $2.96$; $a_0=0.53$ blows up at $N=1280$ ($1.18\times10^{-6}$, order $-8.91$); $a_0=10$ gives $2.47\times10^{-6}$ (Table 4.1). LDG with $a_0=0.53$ diverges from $N=320$ ($5.49\times10^{2}$ at $N=1280$); $a_0=0.54$ gives order $3.00$ and $3.38\times10^{-10}$ (Table 4.2). **Shown**
18. 1D convection–diffusion (4.3) with $a(U)=U^2+2$ and $a_1=a_0\max_{u^n}\{(u^n)^2+2\}$: both schemes are third order at $a_0=0.54$; LDG at $a_0=0.53$ deteriorates ($L^\infty$ order $-3.55$ at $N=640$); finite differences at $a_0=0.53$ stay stable up to $N=1280$; $a_0=10$ gives errors $10^3$–$10^4$ times larger and orders still $2.72$ at $N=1280$ (Tables 4.3–4.4). **Shown**
19. 2D heat equation (4.5): up to $60^2$ the $a_0=0.53$ finite-difference runs show no instability; on $100^2$–$180^2$ LDG at $a_0=0.53$ loses convergence (orders $-0.14$ to $-0.88$) while finite differences stay third order; $a_0=10$ converges at orders $2.01$–$2.25$ (Tables 4.5–4.8). **Shown**
20. 1D linear dispersive equation (4.8): $a_0=0.53$ explodes for both schemes ($L^1$ error $1.01\times10^{1}$ finite differences, $7.33\times10^{2}$ LDG at $N=1280$); $a_0=0.54$ is third order ($8.87\times10^{-10}$ and $1.05\times10^{-9}$) (Tables 4.11–4.12). **Shown**
21. Generalized KdV (4.9), $\alpha=0.1$, $T=\pi$, $a_1=a_0\max\{2(u^n)^2\}$: $a_0=0.53$ diverges on fine meshes, $a_0\ge0.54$ is third order; the penalty of $a_0=10$ is mild here (Tables 4.13–4.14). **Shown**
22. 2D Zakharov–Kuznetsov (4.10), $T=\pi$: $a_0=0.54$ is third order; LDG at $a_0=0.53$ loses order; $a_0=10$ gives errors $\approx0.15$ with orders $\approx0.87$ on $60^2$ (Tables 4.15–4.16). **Shown**
23. 1D linear biharmonic (4.12): $a_0=0.53$ explodes ($L^1$ error $1.30\times10^{4}$ finite differences, $1.36\times10^{7}$ LDG at $N=1280$); $a_0=0.54$ is third order (Tables 4.19–4.20). **Shown**
24. Nonlinear biharmonic (4.13) with the modified LDG of App. A.3 and $a_1=a_0\max\{(u^n)^2+2\}$: $a_0\ge0.54$ gives order $\approx3.1$, $a_0=0.53$ diverges (Tables 4.21–4.22). **Shown**
25. 2D Kuramoto–Sivashinsky (4.14), $\sigma=2$, and 2D nonlinear biharmonic (4.15): stable at $a_0=0.54$; $a_0=10$ converges at order $\approx1.3$–$1.7$ on the meshes used; $a_0=0.53$ results, "not showing signs of instability yet" on the coarse meshes, are omitted (Tables 4.23–4.26). **Shown**
26. The criterion $a_0\ge0.54$, derived for linear highest-derivative models, also holds numerically for nonlinear equations and for equations with lower-order (convection) terms, in 1D and 2D. (Remark 3.2, §5) **Shown** (numerical only)
27. Theorem (App. A.3): the semi-discrete LDG scheme (A.10)–(A.14) with fluxes (A.15) for $U_t+(a(U)U_{xx})_{xx}=0$, $a\ge0$, periodic, satisfies $\frac12\frac{d}{dt}\int_\Omega u^2\,dx+\int_\Omega a(u)v^2\,dx=0$, hence is $L^2$ stable. **Proved** (semi-discrete, spatial)
28. Future work: spatially varying linear implicit terms, non-periodic boundaries and non-uniform meshes, by energy analysis. (§5) **Stated**

*(Compiler checks, not in the sources.)* The tableau (2.17) has $c=(0,\tfrac12,\tfrac23,\tfrac12,1)$ for both parts and coincides with ARS(4,4,3) on the [IMEX page](/methods/imex-runge-kutta.md). $b^{\mathrm I}$ and $b^{\mathrm E}$ equal the last rows of $A^{\mathrm I}$ and $A^{\mathrm E}$, so $y_{n+1}=Y_5$ ([stiffly accurate](/properties/stiff-accuracy.md)), and the implicit diagonal is $\tfrac12$ on stages 2–5, so with constant $a_1$ every stage solves the same linear system. Because $G$ depends on $a$, $a_0$ and $h/\Delta x^r$ only through $h$ times the spatial symbol, and the threshold is the same for real-negative and imaginary symbols, the result is a property of the IMEX pair on the split test equation $y'=(1-a_0)\lambda y+a_0\lambda y$. The claim that the implicit matrix "is only needed to be solved once" (§1) holds for fixed $a_1$; every nonlinear test recomputes $a_1$ from $u^n$ each step.

# Printed slips

- Eq. (4.10): the domain is printed "$(x,y)\in(\pi,\pi)^2$"; the minus sign is missing (the other 2D problems use $(-\pi,\pi)^2$).
- §4.1.2, p. 11: "a more extensive mesh refinement study from $120^2$ to $180^2$ grid points", while Tables 4.7–4.8 list $N_x,N_y=100,120,\dots,180$.
- §3: Lemma 3.1 is introduced as "the necessary and sufficient stability condition" but stated only as a sufficient condition; Lemma 3.2 likewise.
- Ref. [37] (J. Sci. Comput. 17, 2002) prints pages "1–4", implausibly short for a research article.
- Ref. [22] cites KdV as "Philos. Mag. 91 (2011) 1007–1028", the 2011 reprint, not the 1895 original.
- Tables 4.23–4.24 print orders $7.34$ and $7.32$ at the finest mesh ($100^2$), pre-asymptotic; the text concedes the refinement was chosen "to obtain nicer-looking numerical orders".

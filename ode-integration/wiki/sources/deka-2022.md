# Summary

The paper proposes a matrix-free time integrator for the method-of-lines
semi-discretization of the 2.5-D resistive MHD equations in the code
`cppmhd`. It uses exponential Rosenbrock schemes
([exponential integrators](/methods/exponential-integrators.md)) whose
φ-function actions are computed by polynomial interpolation at Leja points
([evaluating φ-functions](/methods/phi-function-evaluation.md)). Steps are
chosen by a cost-minimizing controller (Einkemmer 2018, extended to
exponential integrators by Deka & Einkemmer 2021;
[step-size control](/methods/step-size-control.md)). Two ingredients make
the method matrix-free. Jacobian–vector products are forward finite
differences of the right-hand side. The largest eigenvalue of the Jacobian,
which places the Leja points, is estimated by power iteration and
recomputed only every 50 steps. The authors claim this is the first
matrix-free Leja-based exponential integrator. Exponential Runge–Kutta-type
schemes preserve linear invariants, so $\nabla\cdot B=0$ holds without
divergence cleaning (Einkemmer & Ostermann 2015, cited).

The evaluation is numerical, on a [Kelvin–Helmholtz instability](/benchmarks/kelvin-helmholtz-instability.md)
(cases I–IV, two diffusion regimes, grids $128^2$ to $800^2$) and a magnetic
reconnection problem (cases V–VI). With the fourth-order EXPRB43, Leja beats
Krylov (EPIC library) by factors up to 2.5–3 in the diffusion-dominated KHI
cases, 6–8 against tolerance in the low-diffusion ones, and 6.4 in case VI.
EXPRB43 with Leja beats the explicit embedded pairs RK43 and DOPRI54
([embedded pairs](/methods/embedded-runge-kutta-pair.md)) by up to 6.3–8.4
against error, and [CVODE](/software/cvode.md)'s variable-order BDF by up to
3. The cost-based controller is cheaper than the traditional one with Leja
in most configurations, and it lets Krylov runs finish at lenient
tolerances. For the "Krylov-friendly" EPIRK5P1, Krylov beats Leja. The
fifth-order EXPRB54s4 with Leja is worse than EXPRB43. Estimating the
spectrum every 50 steps costs about 1.7% of the runtime, against about 39%
when done every step.

# Key claims

Registry notation: $y$ the semi-discrete state (source $u$), $h$ the step
(source $\Delta t$), $J_n$ the Jacobian at $y_n$ (source $\mathcal J(u^n)$),
$N_n(y)=f(y)-J_ny$ the page-local remainder (source $\mathcal F$), $L$ and
$N$ the linear part and remainder of $y'=Ly+N(y)$ (source $\mathcal A$, $g$),
$\phi_k$ (source $\varphi_l$, same normalisation), $Y_2,Y_3,Y_4$ the stages
(source $a^n,b^n,c^n$), $\Lambda$ the estimated largest eigenvalue of $J_n$
(source $\alpha$, §2.3; page-local, as on [evaluating φ-functions](/methods/phi-function-evaluation.md)), $\alpha_{\mathrm c},\beta_{\mathrm c},\lambda_{\mathrm c},\delta_{\mathrm c}$
the controller parameters (source $\alpha,\beta,\lambda,\delta$, §2.4).

1. The matrix-free Leja-based exponential Rosenbrock integrator is, "to the
   best of our knowledge", the first matrix-free Leja-based exponential
   integrator; earlier Leja work used test problems whose linearization is
   given analytically (§1). **Stated**
2. Exponential integrators preserve all linear invariants and equilibria
   (Einkemmer & Ostermann 2015, Thm 2). With a finite-difference Jacobian the
   discrete solenoidal condition (Eq. 11) holds for $J_n$ and for $N_n$, so
   $\nabla\cdot B=0$ is preserved without cleaning (§1; §3.1). **Stated**
   (cited theorem); **Shown** in Fig. 12: $\nabla\cdot B$ stays between about
   $10^{-10}$ and $10^{-9}$ up to $T=100$ (case VI; EXPRB43 and EPIRK5P1;
   tol $10^{-3},10^{-5},10^{-7}$).
3. Exponential Euler is first order in general; exponential Rosenbrock–Euler
   $y_{n+1}=y_n+h\phi_1(hJ_n)f(y_n)$ (Eq. 3) is second order because it uses
   the Jacobian (§2, §2.1). **Stated**
4. EXPRB43 (Hochbruck et al. 2009) is fourth order with an embedded
   third-order solution and "presents an excellent trade-off between accuracy
   and the computational cost" (§2.1, Eqs. 4–5). **Stated** (order);
   **Shown** (trade-off)
5. EXPRB54s4 (Luan & Ostermann 2014) is a 4-stage fifth-order scheme with an
   embedded fourth-order solution. Per step it needs ten matrix-function
   evaluations, EPIRK5P1 eight (error estimator included) and EXPRB43 five
   (§2.1, Eqs. 6–7; §5.3). **Stated**
6. EPIRK5P1 (Tokman et al. 2012) is fifth order; its embedded fourth-order
   solution sets $g_{32}=0.5$, $g_{33}=1.0$ in Eq. (8) (Loffeld & Tokman 2013)
   (§2.2, Table 1). **Stated**
7. Leja points allow incremental interpolation, one function evaluation per
   added node, whereas Chebyshev points require re-evaluation at all nodes
   (§2.3). **Stated**
8. $\Lambda$ is estimated by power iteration with a safety factor. The matrix
   is shifted by $\Lambda/2$ and scaled by $\Lambda/4$, so the Leja points lie in
   $[-2,2]$. This spectral estimate is "the primary difference" from Deka &
   Einkemmer (2021) (§2.3). **Stated**
9. Leja interpolation converges only if the norm of the interpolated
   function and $h$ are not too large; otherwise the step is rejected and
   restarted with the smaller step of the traditional controller. For
   EPIRK5P1 on reconnection the polynomial fails to converge within the
   maximum of 500 Leja points for large $h$ (§2.3; §5.2). **Stated** /
   **Shown**
10. Traditional controllers assume that the cost per step does not depend
    on $h$. This holds for explicit methods and for implicit methods with
    direct solvers, but not for iterative (implicit or exponential)
    integrators, whose cost depends nonlinearly on $h$ (§1, §2.4). **Stated**
11. The cost-based controller (Einkemmer 2018) follows from gradient descent
    on $\ln$(cost per unit time) with respect to $\ln h$:
    $h_{n+1}=h_n\lambda_{\mathrm c}$ if $1\le\sigma_n<\lambda_{\mathrm c}$,
    $h_n\delta_{\mathrm c}$ if $\delta_{\mathrm c}\le\sigma_n<1$, and $h_n\sigma_n$
    otherwise, with $\sigma_n=\exp(-\alpha_{\mathrm c}\tanh(\beta_{\mathrm c}\Delta_n))$
    and $\Delta_n$ the log–log slope of the cost (page-local, as on
    [step-size control](/methods/step-size-control.md)). The parameters are
    $\alpha_{\mathrm c}=0.65241444$, $\beta_{\mathrm c}=0.26862269$,
    $\lambda_{\mathrm c}=1.37412002$, $\delta_{\mathrm c}=0.64446017$. The step
    taken is the minimum of this proposal and the traditional step (§2.4).
    **Derived** (formal, assuming the cost function varies slowly in $n$)
12. Estimating $\Lambda$ every 50 steps instead of every step (KHI case III,
    EXPRB43, proposed controller), total/power-iteration seconds (every 50
    steps vs every step): tol $10^{-3}$ 110/2 vs 165/63; $10^{-4}$ 115/2 vs
    196/77; $10^{-5}$ 98/3 vs 211/108; $10^{-6}$ 151/4 vs 320/167; $10^{-7}$
    426/19 vs 1294/887 (§4.2.1, Fig. 6, Table 3). **Shown**
13. KHI, EXPRB43: the proposed controller beats the traditional one at all
    tolerances for Leja and Krylov in cases I and II, and only for Leja in
    cases III and IV. With the traditional controller Krylov converges only
    at tol $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-6}$ in case I, because large steps give large
    Krylov bases (cost $O(m_{\mathrm K}^2)$ for basis size $m_{\mathrm K}$)
    (§4.2, Figs. 2–5). **Shown**
14. KHI, Leja vs Krylov (EXPRB43): case I factor 2.5 (tolerance) and 1.65
    (error); case II factor 3; cases III and IV Leja superior at lenient and
    intermediate tolerances, factors about 1.8 and 2.3 (error) and 6 and 8
    (tolerance) (§4.2). **Shown**
15. Leja shows a systematic decrease of error with runtime in all KHI cases,
    while Krylov's error and cost are "somewhat unpredictable" in cases I and
    II (§4.2). **Shown**
16. EXPRB43 (Leja) vs explicit embedded pairs (traditional controller): in
    case II at tol $10^{-6}$ it is 1.6× faster than RK43 and 2.2× faster than
    DOPRI54. Factors are 3.6 and 2.4 (error) and 3.7 and 4.8 (tolerance) in
    case I; 6.3 and 8.4 (error) and 9.8 and 13 (tolerance) in case II. RK43
    fails to converge at tol $10^{-2}$, DOPRI54 at $10^{-2}$ and $10^{-3}$
    (§4.3, Fig. 7). **Shown**
17. The cost-based controller is not designed for explicit schemes, whose
    cost per step does not depend on $h$ (§4.3). **Stated**
18. EXPRB43 (Leja and Krylov) vs CVODE variable-order BDF (cases III, IV):
    EXPRB43 is "more stable in terms of the error incurred as a function of
    the computational cost"; Leja improves on CVODE by up to 3 (case III) and
    2.3 (case IV) (§4.3, Fig. 8). **Shown**
19. Reconnection, EPIRK5P1: both controllers work equally well for Krylov;
    for Leja the proposed controller improves some configurations and
    worsens others; Krylov beats Leja (§5.2, Fig. 9). **Shown**
20. Reconnection, EXPRB43: Leja beats Krylov by up to 87% (tolerance) and
    50% (error) in case V, and by factors up to 6.4 (tolerance) and 4.3
    (error) in case VI. With Leja the proposed controller is cheaper than the
    traditional one in all configurations, and the two cost about the same
    for tol $\le10^{-7}$; with Krylov they perform virtually the same (§5.2,
    Fig. 10). **Shown**
21. With Leja the proposed controller gives a roughly linear relation between
    cost and tolerance (or error) in many configurations and flattens the
    "zigzag" curves of the traditional controller; not so with Krylov (§5.2;
    §6). **Shown**
22. EPIRK schemes are "Krylov-friendly": they reuse one Krylov basis and put
    higher-order $\phi_k$ in later stages (Tokman & Loffeld 2010). These
    conditions are redundant for Leja. EPIRK5P1 uses different coefficients
    for internal stages, solution and error estimate, so no $\phi_k$
    evaluation can be reused. This explains Krylov's advantage on EPIRK5P1
    (§5.3). **Stated** (Fig. 10 as supporting evidence)
23. EXPRB54s4 (Leja) is worse than EXPRB43 in both reconnection cases and
    marginally better than EPIRK5P1 (Leja) in case VI, despite two more
    matrix-function evaluations (§5.3, Fig. 13). **Shown**
24. A fifth-order exponential Rosenbrock scheme with fewer stages and
    matrix-function evaluations could improve on EXPRB43 "and maybe even
    EPIRK5P1 (with Krylov)"; augmented-matrix evaluation of $\phi_k$
    combinations (Al-Mohy & Higham 2011) is future work (§5.3). **Stated**
25. Leja interpolation needs only matrix–vector products and no inner
    products, which favours massively parallel architectures such as GPUs
    (§1, §6). **Stated**
26. The introduction states gains "up to a factor of three" for Leja over
    Krylov (§1), while the body reports larger factors (6, 8, 6.4). **Stated**

# Printed slips

- §2.1: "$\mathcal F(u)=f(u)-\mathcal J(u)$" for the remainder
  $f(u)-\mathcal J(u^n)u$.
- §2, p. 3: the linear exact solution is printed "$u^{n+1}=u^n\exp(\mathcal A\Delta t)$",
  with the operator order reversed.
- §2, p. 3: the recursion "$\varphi_{l+1}(z)=\frac1z(\varphi_l(z)-\frac1{l!})$,
  $l\ge1$" with $\varphi_0=e^z$ does not define $\varphi_1$; the range should
  be $l\ge0$.
- Eq. (10): "$(P/\rho)^\gamma=$ constant" for $P/\rho^\gamma=$ constant.
- Eq. (11): the discrete divergence is written with the full state
  $U^n_{i\pm1,j}$, $U^n_{i,j\pm1}$ instead of the components $B_x$, $B_y$.
- §2.3 and §2.4 both use $\alpha$, for the eigenvalue estimate and for a
  controller parameter.
- Fig. 1 caption: snapshots at $T=2.0$ and $2.75$ "for the KHI (case IV)",
  but case IV ends at $T_f=1.0$; no case reaches $2.75$.
- §1 "up to a factor of three" against the factors 6, 8 and 6.4 of §4.2 and
  §5.2.
- §5.2: "the Jacobian function diverges after just a few iterations",
  apparently for the Leja interpolation.
- Fig. 3 caption reports that Leja fails to converge for tol $<10^{-5}$ in
  case II; the text of §4.2 does not mention this.
- Reference list: Einkemmer, Tokman & Loffeld 2017 is printed as "J. Sci.
  Comput., 330, 550" with the J. Comput. Phys. DOI 10.1016/j.jcp.2016.11.027.
  The Hindmarsh & Serban URL contains a space ("cv guide.pdf"), and a
  Bergamaschi DOI prints "11758549 93" where the underscore was lost.

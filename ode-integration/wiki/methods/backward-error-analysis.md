# Backward error analysis, modified equations and modifying integrators

**Backward error analysis** (BEA) asks which differential equation a numerical
method solves exactly. For a one-step map $\psi_h$ applied to $y'=f(y)$, the
**modified equation** is $\tilde y'=\tilde f(\tilde y)$ with

$$\tilde f=f+h\tilde f_2+h^2\tilde f_3+\cdots$$

(page-local coefficients $\tilde f_j$), chosen so that its exact time-$h$ flow
reproduces $\psi_h$ order by order in $h$: $y_n=\tilde y(t_n)$ formally
([Blanes et al. (2024)](/sources/blanes-2024.md), §4.4, after Griffiths &
Sanz-Serna, Hairer, Reich; [Ketcheson & Ranocha (2023)](/sources/ketcheson-2023.md),
§2.4). A method of order $p$ has $\tilde f_j=0$ for $2\le j\le p$. The series
generally diverges and is truncated.

## Computing the modified equation

**From B-series.** If $\psi_h=B(a,\cdot)$, write $h\tilde f=B(b,\cdot)$ with
$b(\varnothing)=0$. The modified flow is the exact-flow series $e$ applied to
$h\tilde f$, so $b$ solves $b\star e=a$ with the
[substitution law](/definitions/b-series.md); since $(b\star e)(\tau)=b(\tau)$
plus terms in smaller trees, the solution is computed tree by tree (Ketcheson
& Ranocha (2023), Eq. (26)). Automating this to high order was the original
motivation for [BSeries.jl](/software/bseries-jl.md), because by hand it is
tedious enough that BEA "is less well-known" for nonlinear problems (§2.4).

**From the BCH formula.** For a [splitting method](/methods/operator-splitting.md)
$\psi_h=\varphi^{[1]}_{a_{s+1}h}\circ\varphi^{[2]}_{b_sh}\circ\dots\circ\varphi^{[1]}_{a_1h}$,
the logarithm of its Lie transformation is $h$ times the sum of the Lie
derivatives of $f^{[1]}$ and $f^{[2]}$ plus higher terms that are iterated
commutators of them, so the modified vector field lies in the Lie algebra
generated by $f^{[1]}$ and $f^{[2]}$ (Blanes et al. (2024), Eq. (4.14)). If
both parts are Hamiltonian, so is every commutator, and the method has a
**modified Hamiltonian** $\tilde H$ (§4.4).

## Long-time consequences

Blanes et al. (2024, §4.4, recalling Hairer–Lubich–Wanner, Moan, Calvo &
Hairer) state:

* the numerical solution is exponentially close to the flow of the optimally
  truncated modified equation, $\lVert\psi_h(y_0)-\tilde\varphi_h(y_0)\rVert\le C h\,e^{-h_0/h}$
  (page-local constants $C$, $h_0$; Eq. (4.16));
* for an order-$p$ splitting of a Hamiltonian system on a compact set,
  $H(y_n)=H(y_0)+O(h^p)$ for $nh\le e^{h_0/(2h)}$;
* for integrable systems the global error of a symplectic method grows at most
  linearly, $\le Cth^p$ for $t\le h^{-p}$, whereas non-symplectic methods give
  $H(y_n)-H(y_0)=O(th^p)$ and quadratic error growth;
* a variable step size destroys these properties, because the modified
  equation depends on $h$ (§4.4, §9.10).

Symmetric methods have modified equations in even powers of $h$: a map is
time-symmetric iff its BCH exponent is odd in $h$ (§2.1.1). Ketcheson &
Ranocha (2023, §3.1.2) confirm by computation that the modified equation of
Störmer–Verlet, treated as an additive RK method on the Kepler problem, has no
odd powers of $h$ through $h^7$. For linear symmetric splittings of the
harmonic oscillator the modified Hamiltonian is a rescaled quadratic form whose
frequency is fixed by the half-trace of the one-step matrix (Blanes et al.
(2024), §4.3, after Blanes–Casas–Sanz-Serna 2014). See
[symplecticity](/properties/symplecticity.md).

## Worked examples

* **Two-stage explicit second-order family.** With $b=(1-\alpha_\star,\alpha_\star)$,
  $c_2=a_{21}=1/(2\alpha_\star)$ (page-local parameter $\alpha_\star$), the
  modified field has $h^2$ coefficient $-\tfrac16$ on the tall tree $f'f'f$
  and $(-\tfrac16+\tfrac1{8\alpha_\star})$ on $f''(f,f)$; no $h$ term appears
  because the method has order 2 (Ketcheson & Ranocha (2023), Eq. (27)).
* **Explicit Euler on Lotka–Volterra.** The $O(h)$ modified equation agrees
  with Hairer–Lubich–Wanner; modified equations up to $O(h^5)$ are needed to
  follow the Euler solution for $h=0.1$–$0.12$ to $T=10$, or for $h=0.1$ to
  $T=66.4$ (§2.4.2, Fig. 1).
* **AVF method.** Its modified equation to $h^4$ reproduces Celledoni et al.
  (2010) and shows energy preservation of the truncated field to that order
  (§3.2).
* **Explicit midpoint on a nonlinear oscillator.** For
  $y_1'=-y_2/\lVert y\rVert^2$, $y_2'=y_1/\lVert y\rVert^2$, explicit midpoint
  conserves $\lVert y\rVert^2$ exactly at every step size, with closed-form
  solution, and is symmetric on this problem although not in general. Its
  modified field is $\tilde f(y)=f(y)\,\varrho(h/\lVert y\rVert^2)$ with
  (page-local) $\varrho(z)=1-\tfrac{z^2}{12}+\tfrac{z^4}{80}-\tfrac{z^6}{448}+\tfrac{z^8}{2304}+O(z^{10})$
  (§2.6, Eqs. (35)–(37)). The argument: the problem's Hamiltonian structure
  makes even-order derivative terms parallel to $f$ and odd ones parallel to
  $y$, and every truncation of the modified equation of an energy-conserving
  method must conserve energy (Hairer–Lubich–Wanner Cor. IX.5.3, as recalled).
  At order $h^5$, 16 of the 20 B-series coefficients are nonzero yet their
  elementary differentials sum to zero on this problem (§2.6.1).

## Imposing structure on the modified equation

The modified equation can also be used to *design* methods. [de León et al. (2025)](/sources/deleon-2025.md)
(§2) require the
modified field of an explicit Runge–Kutta method to be energy preserving to
order $q$ on canonical Hamiltonian systems: the energy-preserving B-series
characterization of Celledoni et al. (Theorem 1, as quoted) gives linear
conditions on the modified-field coefficients, one per conjugacy class of
trees (§2.2), and the substitution law, solved with BSeries.jl and SymPy,
translates them into conditions on the elementary weights and the tableau
(Eqs. (9)–(16)). The result is the family of
[pseudo-energy-preserving methods](/methods/pseudo-energy-preserving-runge-kutta.md),
whose energy error per step is $O(h^{q+1})$ with $q$ above the classical order.
Their argument for long-time behaviour is the backward-error one: when the
dominant error terms of the modified equation do not change the energy, the
global error is expected to grow linearly for moderately long times, which
they observe but do not prove (§4). Two constants of their printed map–flow
relations (Eq. (9)) are inconsistent with the exact flow
($\tfrac7{12}$ and $\tfrac16$ where $\tfrac1{12}$ and $\tfrac18$ are required),
while the conditions they derive from it are correct *(compiler check, not in the
source: with the coefficients of all trees of two or more vertices set to zero the
relation must return $1/\gamma(\tau)$, i.e. $\tfrac1{12}$ and $\tfrac18$ for these two trees)*.

## Modifying integrators

The inverse problem: find a field $\bar f$ (page-local) such that the method
applied to $\bar f$ reproduces the **exact** flow of $f$; with
$h\bar f=B(\bar b,\cdot)$ this is $\bar b\star a=e$ (Ketcheson & Ranocha
(2023), §2.5). Truncating $\bar f$ at $h^{k}$ and applying the method gives a
method of order $p+k$. Results:

* explicit Euler on Lotka–Volterra with $h=0.35$: the order-1 modifying
  integrator still drifts (much less than Euler), the order-2 one is
  dissipative, the order-3 one is indistinguishable from the exact, nearly
  periodic solution (Fig. 2);
* explicit midpoint on the oscillator: $\bar f=f\,(1+\tfrac{z^2}{12}+\tfrac{z^4}{20})$
  to $h^4$, $z=h/\lVert y\rVert^2$; from $h^5$ a term parallel to $y$ appears
  and the truncated field is no longer Hamiltonian (Eq. (40)). The order-8
  modifying integrator is far more accurate initially, but because it does not
  conserve energy its error grows faster: at $h=0.6$ the plain midpoint method
  is better at large times, at $h=0.3$ the modifying integrator stays better
  until both errors reach about 100% (§2.6.2, Fig. 3);
* Störmer–Verlet on Kepler: the order-4 modifying integrator has no third-order
  terms but puts $q$ in the right-hand side of $\dot q$, so the scheme is no
  longer explicit; the authors judge the approach unappealing there (§3.1.2,
  Eq. (47)).

The trade-off in the second case — local accuracy against a structural
invariant — is the same one that motivates
[processing](/methods/processing-and-effective-order.md), which corrects only
at output times and leaves the long-time behaviour of the kernel intact
(Blanes et al. (2024), §4.5).

## Highly oscillatory problems

For $y'=Ly+f^{[2]}(y)$ with $e^{tL}$ periodic of frequency $\omega$, Blanes et
al. (2024, §5) build an expansion valid uniformly in $\omega$: Strang
splitting has a modified Hamiltonian under the non-resonance conditions
$k\omega h/2\pi\notin\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}$ (Eq. (5.31)), and an explicit
processor of rotations and kicks makes the processed method's modified
Hamiltonian $H+O(h)$ (Eq. (5.47)); near-resonant step sizes produce energy
spikes (§5.7, Figs. 5.1–5.2).

## Qualifications

* Ketcheson & Ranocha (2023, Eq. (38)) print the closed form of the oscillator
  factor as $\frac{d}{dz}\arccos\frac{1-z^2/4}{1+z^2/4}=2\arctan(z/2)$, which
  is inconsistent with their own series (37) ($\varrho(0)=1$). The function
  matching (37) term by term is $\varrho(z)=\frac{2}{z}\arctan\frac z2$: the
  modified flow over time $h$ must rotate by the angle
  $2\arctan(z/2)$ per step, since $\cos(2\arctan(z/2))=\frac{1-z^2/4}{1+z^2/4}$
  *(compiler check by series expansion, not in the source)*.
* Their claim that this exact energy conservation is "unique among explicit
  discretizations of initial value ODEs" is stated "as far as we are aware"
  and is not proved.
* They print the Kepler force as $q/\lvert q\rvert^3$ (repulsive); the usual
  problem has $-q/\lvert q\rvert^3$, and their Eq. (47) is sign-sensitive.
* Their Eq. (29) prints explicit Euler as $y_{n+1}=y_n+f(y)$, without $h$.

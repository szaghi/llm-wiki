# Summary

The third edition of Toro's textbook keeps the Godunov-method core of the
first edition ([Toro (1997)](/sources/toro-1997.md)) and adds three
chapters that carry it to arbitrary order. **This is a scoped read:**
the prefaces, the table of contents, Chapter 10 and the new
Chapters 18, 19 and 20, with the bibliography entries those chapters
cite; Chapters 1–9, 11–17 and 21 were not read.

* **Ch. 10** (HLL and HLLC, "a substantially revised version"): the HLLC
  middle speed is derived inside the solver from equal star pressures;
  two further HLLC variants and a multicomponent extension are given;
  PVRS-based pressure wave speeds become the recommended default; a
  seven-test suite (now with a non-isolated stationary contact and
  Rusanov results) replaces the first edition's six.
* **Ch. 18** generalizes the centred FORCE flux, the mean of the
  Lax–Friedrichs and two-step Lax–Wendroff fluxes, to Cartesian and
  unstructured meshes in 2D and 3D through edge-based staggered volumes,
  with monotonicity and linear-stability limits and a one-parameter 1D
  family.
* **Ch. 19** states the generalized Riemann problem GRP$_K$ (piecewise
  polynomial data of degree $K$, source terms) and solves it at the
  interface by a time Taylor series: a classical Riemann problem for the
  leading term, Cauchy–Kowalewski plus $K$ linearized Riemann problems
  for the derivatives (the Toro–Titarev solver), with alternatives.
* **Ch. 20** builds the ADER finite-volume scheme on it, works the
  second-order case for linear advection with a linear reaction source,
  and shows long-time advection and an ADER-DG convergence table to tenth
  order.

# Key claims

**Chapter 10: HLL and HLLC (revised).**

1. **HLL** (§§10.2–10.3, eqs. 10.13, 10.17–10.21): integral average and
   flux as in the first edition; "Harten, Lax and van Leer [244] showed
   that the Godunov scheme … if convergent, converges to the weak solution
   … In fact they proved that the converged solution is also the physical,
   entropy satisfying, solution" (p. 321), unchanged from the first
   edition ([HLL](/methods/hll-riemann-solver.md)).
2. **Complete and incomplete solvers** (§10.1, p. 316; §18.1, p. 598),
   new in this edition: HLLC "is a complete Riemann solver, for the Euler
   equations"; for systems with more than three distinct characteristic
   fields (MHD, multiphase) it "becomes incomplete, tending to behave like
   HLL". Rusanov is a one-wave and HLL a two-wave incomplete solver;
   flux-vector splitting behaves like an incomplete solver.
3. **HLLC conditions** (§10.4, eqs. 10.24–10.32): consistency with the
   HLL average, Rankine–Hugoniot across the three waves, equal pressure
   and normal velocity in both star states, tangential velocity unchanged
   across the outer waves, and $S_M = v_{n*}$.
4. **Middle speed inside HLLC** (eqs. 10.36–10.37, read from the page).
   The side star pressures are $p^\mp + \rho^\mp(S_{L,R} - v_n^\mp)(S_M - v_n^\mp)$,
   and equating them gives
   $$S_M = \frac{p^+ - p^- + \rho^- v_n^-(S_L - v_n^-) - \rho^+ v_n^+(S_R - v_n^+)}{\rho^-(S_L - v_n^-) - \rho^+(S_R - v_n^+)} .$$
   "Thus, we only need to provide estimates for $S_L$ and $S_R$, just as
   for the simpler HLL solver" (p. 325). In the first edition the same
   formula was Batten et al.'s middle-wave estimate (1st ed.
   eqs. 10.57–10.58); here Batten et al. are credited only with "new ways
   of estimating the wave speeds" (p. 316).
5. **Star states and flux** (eqs. 10.26, 10.38–10.39): identical to the
   first edition ([HLLC](/methods/hllc-riemann-solver.md)).
6. **Two HLLC variants** (eqs. 10.40–10.44, 10.74–10.76), new. Variant 1
   writes the star flux with the side star pressures, "relax[ing]" the
   equal-pressure condition, "being more consistent with the pressure
   approximations (10.36)" (p. 325). Variant 2 uses one mean star
   pressure, the mean of the two side expressions. "For the tests
   considered, all three versions of HLLC give identical results" (§10.8,
   p. 334).
7. **Any equation of state** (p. 326): "All manipulations so far … are
   valid for any equation of state. The equation of state only enters in
   prescribing estimates for $S_L$ and $S_R$."
8. **Multicomponent** (§10.4.3, eqs. 10.45–10.46): species mass
   fractions are carried by the star states with the same factor
   ("nothing special needs to be done"); tangential velocities are special
   passive scalars.
9. **Wave-speed estimates** (§10.5, eqs. 10.47–10.66). Davis direct and
   min/max, "not recommended for practical computations"; Roe-average
   eigenvalues credited to "Davis [150] and Einfeldt [181]"; Einfeldt's
   $\bar c_{\mathrm E}$ form (10.52–10.54; the mean velocity still
   undefined on the page); Einfeldt et al. "introduce the concept of
   positively conservative Riemann solvers … density and internal energy
   remain positive" (p. 329); Rusanov with Davis's bound, or the simpler
   and "likely … more robust" $S = \max(\vert v_n^-\vert + c^-, \vert v_n^+\vert + c^+)$
   (10.56, new); Lax–Friedrichs for $S = h/\Delta t$; pressure-based
   $S_L = v_n^- - c^- q^{\mathrm w}_L$, $S_R = v_n^+ + c^+ q^{\mathrm w}_R$
   with $p_*$ from PVRS, TRRS or TSRS (10.59–10.66).
10. **Revised recommendation** (pp. 331–332). "In computational practice
    we *could* use the hybrid scheme" (1st ed. p. 305: "we use"); "For
    ideal gases we find that the simplified PVRS scheme, with
    $p_* = \max(0, p_{pvrs})$ is very simple and also is found to be
    sufficiently robust". The HLLC summary (§10.6, eqs. 10.67–10.73):
    $p_{\mathrm{PV}}$, then pressure-based $S_L$, $S_R$, then $S_M$ from
    claim 4, then the flux. The §10.8 runs still use the adaptive
    noniterative scheme of §9.5.2.
11. **Results** (§10.8, p. 335). HLL and HLLC "broadly similar" to exact
    Godunov; the sonic rarefaction of Test 1 is "better resolved by the HLL
    and HLLC … than by the exact Riemann solver"; on the stationary
    non-isolated contact of Test 5 HLLC is comparable to exact, while HLL
    diffuses it like Steger–Warming and van Leer splitting; "The HLLC
    Riemann solver preserves the excellent entropy–satisfaction property of
    the HLL Riemann solver"; Rusanov ≈ HLL on slow contacts but "clearly
    inferior to the HLL scheme" at the sonic rarefaction.
12. **Open issues** (§10.9, p. 336): "the positivity/negativity of the
    momentum flux Safranov [420]" and, after V. A. Titarev (personal
    communication), robustness of the wave speeds for "very high speed flow
    inpinging [sic] on solid stationary walls; some of the well known wave
    speed estimates may fail". Extensions with more fields: Gurski (MHD),
    Miyoshi & Kusano (HLLD), Bouchut et al.

**Chapter 18: FORCE fluxes in multiple space dimensions (new).**

13. **1D FORCE** (§18.2.1, eqs. 18.6–18.8): the mean of the
    Lax–Friedrichs flux and the two-step Lax–Wendroff flux, the physical
    flux at $\tfrac12(\bar u_i + \bar u_{i+1}) - \tfrac12\frac{\Delta t}{h}[f(\bar u_{i+1}) - f(\bar u_i)]$
    (18.7). It derives from a deterministic reading of staggered Glimm;
    Chen & Toro proved convergence for isentropic gas dynamics and shallow
    water with bed slope.
14. **Monotone weighted family** (§18.2.2, eqs. 18.11–18.19, Fig. 18.1).
    For linear advection, the convex combination with weight $\omega$ on
    Lax–Wendroff gives Lax–Friedrichs, FORCE and Lax–Wendroff at
    $\omega = 0, \tfrac12, 1$; the weight $1/(1 + \vert\nu\vert)$ reproduces
    Godunov's upwind scheme and separates monotone (below) from
    non-monotone (above). FORCE is "the optimal scheme" among
    constant-weight monotone members and "reduces the viscosity of the
    Lax–Friedrichs scheme by 50%"; Godunov reduces it "by a further 19%
    ($\ln 2 - 1/2$), relative to the FORCE scheme; this is the maximum
    possible reduction within the class of monotone methods" (p. 604), its
    numerical viscosity being $\tfrac12(1 - \vert\nu\vert)$ times the speed
    times $h$ (18.19). GFORCE uses the Godunov weight with a chosen Courant
    number; for nonlinear systems "the success is only partial".
15. **Multi-D limitation** (p. 605): plain FORCE (and Lax–Friedrichs,
    Lax–Wendroff "as stated here") has a linear-stability problem in 2D and
    3D; dimensional splitting would exclude unstructured meshes and order
    above two.
16. **Multi-D Cartesian FORCE** (§18.3, eqs. 18.21–18.35): edge-based
    staggered volumes, a half step on them and a half step back, recast in
    conservative form. In $\alpha$ space dimensions the only change from 1D
    is $\Delta t \to \alpha\,\Delta t$ in the Lax–Wendroff state and the
    Lax–Friedrichs dissipation $h/(\alpha\,\Delta t)$ (18.34–18.35).
17. **1D FORCE$\alpha$ family** (§18.4.1, eqs. 18.39–18.41), with $\alpha \ge 1$
    now a free parameter: "monotone and
    linearly stable under" $\nu \le \sqrt{2\alpha - 1}/\alpha$, decreasing
    in $\alpha$. The Lax–Wendroff member with $\alpha = 2$ is the Godunov
    centred scheme, linearly stable for $\vert\nu\vert \le \tfrac12\sqrt2$
    but monotone only for $\tfrac12 \le \vert\nu\vert \le \tfrac12\sqrt2$,
    "useless in practice"; the Lax–Friedrichs member with $\alpha = 2$
    "appears to be new", monotone over its full range
    $\vert\nu\vert \le \tfrac12$.
18. **Multi-D linear analysis** (§18.4.3, eqs. 18.42–18.47,
    Figs. 18.6–18.7). Monotone iff the sum of the squared directional
    Courant numbers is at most $\tfrac12$ in 2D and $\tfrac13$ in 3D
    (18.47); the empirically determined linear-stability region coincides.
19. **Unstructured meshes** (§18.5, eqs. 18.48–18.60). A staggered volume
    per face, from the two adjacent cells; FORCE is the mean of a
    volume-weighted Lax–Wendroff-type flux and a Lax–Friedrichs-type flux
    (18.57–18.60), "These two component fluxes appear to be new". No
    eigenstructure and no Riemann solver are needed (§18.7). Schemes "of up
    to sixth order of accuracy in space and time" with ADER FV and DG are
    reported in the Toro, Hidalgo & Dumbser preprint.
20. **Upwind against centred** (§18.4.2, p. 612; p. 598). On the stationary
    contact at $t = 5$, Rusanov and HLL "have more numerical viscosity for
    this test than any of the centred FORCE$\alpha$ schemes"; van Leer
    flux-vector splitting beats FORCE1 but not FORCE2 or FORCE3; "unless one
    has a complete Riemann solver available, it is more convenient to use a
    centred–type method".

**Chapter 19: the generalized Riemann problem (new).**

21. **Definition** (§19.2, eq. 19.5). GRP$_K$ is the Cauchy problem for a
    balance law with smooth data on each side of the interface whose
    derivatives above order $K$ vanish. GRP$_0$ is the classical Riemann
    problem, GRP$_1$ the piecewise-linear Ben-Artzi–Falcovitz GRP; also
    called "Derivative Riemann Problem" and "High–Order Riemann Problem".
    Its interface solution gives one-step FV and DG schemes of order $K+1$
    in space and time.
22. **Toro–Titarev solver** (§19.4, eqs. 19.22–19.41). The interface state
    is a time Taylor series. Leading term: the Godunov state of the
    homogeneous classical Riemann problem between the boundary-extrapolated
    values, source neglected; it may come from "a classical Riemann
    solver, exact or approximate" (pp. 636, 642). Higher terms:
    Cauchy–Kowalewski expresses time derivatives through space derivatives,
    sources included; the space derivatives at the interface come from
    evolution equations for the derivatives, with their source terms
    neglected and linearized about the leading state, so that $K$ linear
    classical Riemann problems with one eigenstructure give them. GRP$_K$
    costs "1 (generally non–linear) Riemann problem … and $K$ linearized
    Riemann problems" (p. 641).
23. **Justification** (§19.4.5, p. 642): "For linear systems with constant
    coefficients the method … is justified. For the non–linear case there
    appears to be no theoretical justification available so far, although
    the empirical evidence available is supportive".
24. **Scalar examples** (§19.5, eqs. 19.55–19.70). Linear advection
    reproduces the exact Taylor expansion; for linear advection–reaction
    the source enters only through the coefficients, "At no point did we
    need to solve directly inhomogeneous problems" (p. 646); a nonlinear
    scalar case with source is worked to second order (19.70, read from the
    page).
25. **Short-time validity** (§19.5.4, Table 19.1): the series "remains valid
    only for small times" (p. 650).
26. **Other GRP$_K$ solvers** (§19.6, pp. 651–653). Harten,
    Osher, Engquist & Chakravarthy (evolve extrapolated values by Taylor
    and Cauchy–Kowalewski, then classical Riemann problems); Castro & Toro
    (Riemann problems for evolved time derivatives): "for homogeneous
    linear hyperbolic systems with constant coefficients, all the three
    solves [sic] are algebraically equivalent. Otherwise, they are all
    different but are equivalent in terms of order of accuracy" (p. 652).
    Dumbser, Enaux & Toro: local space–time DG evolution, no
    Cauchy–Kowalewski, stiff sources. The obstacle of Cauchy–Kowalewski is
    "sheer complexity" more than cost: "the use of symbolic manipulators is
    a must" (p. 652).
27. **Terminology** (§19.3.2, p. 633): "In a numerical context,
    Cauchy–Kowalewski procedure is also known as the Lax–Wendroff
    procedure" ([Lax–Wendroff (Taylor) time discretization](/methods/taylor-time-discretization.md)).

**Chapter 20: the ADER approach (new).**

28. **Framework** (§20.2, eqs. 20.2–20.11). The exact finite-volume balance
    with the interface flux the time average of $f$ at the GRP$_K$
    solution between the reconstructed polynomials $\mathcal P_i$ and
    $\mathcal P_{i+1}$ of degree $K$, and the source the space–time average
    of $\mathbf s$ along the Cauchy–Kowalewski evolution inside the cell.
    Order $K+1$ in space and time with one nonlinear reconstruction per step
    (§20.4.3).
29. **History** (§20.1, pp. 656–657). ADER was put forward by Toro,
    Millington & Nejad (2001) for linear problems on Cartesian meshes, with
    fixed-stencil schemes up to tenth order in 1D and 2D; "for linear
    problems with constant coefficients the ADER schemes are identical to
    the schemes of [242]" (Harten et al., ENO III), different but of equal
    order for nonlinear problems. ADER extends the second-order GRP method
    of Ben-Artzi & Falcovitz, Harten et al.'s scheme extends MUSCL–Hancock.
    Nonlinear ADER beyond second order first in Titarev & Toro (2002);
    multi-D Cartesian, non-Cartesian (Käser) and unstructured FV and DG
    (Dumbser et al.) followed.
30. **Second-order ADER with a source** (§20.3, eqs. 20.23–20.41). For
    linear advection with a linear reaction source the flux "includes a
    contribution due to the source term … the flux knows of the source
    term"; with zero slopes and no source in the flux it reduces to Godunov
    with an upwind source. Without source, the left, right or mean slope
    reproduce Warming–Beam, Lax–Wendroff and Fromm (20.41); with ENO slopes
    it is "effectively a blend of the Warming–Beam and the Lax–Wendroff
    schemes" (p. 666).
31. **Arbitrary order, linear case** (§20.4, eqs. 20.43–20.50): an explicit
    flux formula for linear advection (20.48). Stiff sources need the
    Dumbser–Enaux–Toro variant (p. 669).
32. **Reconstruction** (§20.2.4, p. 660): by Godunov's theorem, fixed-stencil
    schemes of order above one are oscillatory, so non-oscillation needs
    adaptive stencils (TVD, ENO, WENO)
    ([Godunov's order barrier](/results/godunov-order-barrier.md)).
33. **Order verification** (§20.5.2, Table 20.1, from Dumbser & Munz):
    ADER-DG on "very irregular" triangles for 2D linearized Euler reaches
    its design order up to ten (see Tests). "For the range of smaller
    errors it is distinctly better to use a high–order method" (p. 673).

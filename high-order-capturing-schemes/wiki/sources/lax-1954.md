# Summary

Lax defines weak solutions of systems of conservation laws by an
integral identity against compactly supported $C^1$ test functions, and
observes that the class of weak solutions depends on which conservation
form is chosen, that weak solutions are not unique, and that a further
principle (entropy, vanishing viscosity or stability) must select the
physical one (§1). He proposes a difference scheme for discontinuous
flows built on the **conservation form**: a centred difference of the
flux and a forward time difference taken from the **average of the two
neighbours**, on a staggered lattice. This is the scheme now called
[Lax–Friedrichs](/definitions/monotone-flux.md). He conjectures that it
converges under the CFL condition and shows that a strong (bounded,
a.e.) limit is a weak solution. Computations of Burgers' equation, a
cubic flux, Eulerian and Lagrangian gas dynamics and a wall reflection
support convergence with a shock width that stays constant and a contact
width that grows like the square root of the cycle count. A
non-conservative scheme converges to a discontinuity of the wrong speed
(0.388 against 0.333). For a single genuinely nonlinear equation he proves
continuous dependence in a weak norm and $L^1$ compactness of the solution
set (§5). The paper is the source the bundle's pages name for the
Lax–Friedrichs scheme; it does **not** contain the "Lax shock tube" data
(see Tests).

# Key claims

1. **Weak solution** (§1, eq. 3, p. 160). A bounded measurable $u$ is a
   weak solution of $\partial_t u + \partial_x f(u) + B = 0$ with initial
   data if the space–time integral of (time derivative of the test
   function times $u$, plus its space derivative times $f(u)$, minus the
   test function times $B$), plus the integral of the test function at
   $t = 0$ times the initial data, vanishes for every test function with
   continuous first derivatives that vanishes outside a bounded set.
   Genuine solutions are weak; a weak solution with continuous first
   derivatives is genuine. Two genuine solutions separated by a smooth
   curve form a weak solution iff the jump of $u$ times the curve's speed
   equals the jump of $f(u)$ (p. 160), the Rankine–Hugoniot condition for
   mass, momentum and energy.
2. **Three facts about weak solutions** (§1, pp. 161–162). (a) The class
   depends on the conservation form: after a genuinely nonlinear change of
   unknowns giving another conservation law, the transformed weak solution
   is in general not a weak solution of the new law; for gas dynamics a
   weak solution of mass, momentum and energy is in general not one of the
   entropy equation, and "in physical problems one is looking for weak
   solutions of the first three". (b) Weak solutions cannot be obtained as
   limits of genuine solutions. (c) The initial values do not in general
   determine a unique weak solution (footnote 1: two weak solutions of
   Burgers' equation from the same step data). Selection principles named:
   entropy (Lax doubts that it alone ensures uniqueness), limits of viscous
   flows, stability.
3. **Vanishing viscosity** (§1, eqs. 5–6, pp. 162–163). For
   $\partial_t u + \partial_x f(u) + B = \mu\,\partial_{xx} u$, if the
   solutions converge boundedly a.e. as $\mu \to 0$, the limit is a weak
   solution; proved only for Burgers' equation (Hopf). In italics (p. 163):
   "A weak limit of weak solutions is not a weak solution unless it is also
   a strong limit."
4. **The difference scheme, as printed** (§1, p. 163). The flux derivative
   is replaced by the symmetric quotient over $x \pm h$, and the time
   derivative by the new value minus the **average** of $u$ at $x \pm h$,
   over $\Delta t$; the lattice is staggered ($t = n\Delta t$, $x = mh$,
   $m + (-1)^n$ even). In registry form,
   $$u^{n+1}_i = \tfrac12\left(u^n_{i+1} + u^n_{i-1}\right) - \frac{\Delta t}{2h}\left(f(u^n_{i+1}) - f(u^n_{i-1})\right).$$
   Written out for Burgers' equation with $\Delta t/h = 1$ (§3, p. 183):
   $u^{n+1}_i = \tfrac12(u^n_{i+1} + u^n_{i-1}) + \tfrac14\left[(u^n_{i-1})^2 - (u^n_{i+1})^2\right]$.
   Replacing the average by $u^n_i$ (forward time, centred space) gives a
   scheme that is "unconditionally unstable" (footnote 5). Lax names the
   novelty as "the use of the conservation form of the hydrodynamic
   equations and, to a lesser extent, the particular way of differencing"
   (p. 159): no artificial-viscosity term and no shock tracking.
   *Reader's algebra, not printed by Lax:* the scheme is the conservative
   update with $\hat f_{i+1/2} = \tfrac12\left(f(u_i) + f(u_{i+1})\right) - \tfrac12\,\frac{h}{\Delta t}\,(u_{i+1} - u_i)$,
   the global Lax–Friedrichs flux with $\alpha = h/\Delta t$.
5. **Equivalent viscosity** (p. 165). The forward-time, centred-space
   scheme for the viscous equation of claim 3, with $h$, $\Delta t$,
   $\mu \to 0$ together at fixed $\Delta t/h$ and $\mu = h^2/(2\Delta t)$,
   is exactly the proposed scheme.
6. **Convergence: a conjecture and a conditional theorem** (pp. 163–164).
   Conjecture: as $\Delta t, h \to 0$ under the CFL criterion (the domain
   of dependence of the differential equation inside that of the difference
   equation), the solutions stay uniformly bounded and converge a.e. for a
   wide class of data. *If* so, the limit is a weak solution (summation by
   parts in place of integration by parts; a strong limit is required).
   Convergence is claimed proved, with the proof deferred to a separate
   note, only for $\partial_t u - \partial_x[\log(a + b e^{-u})] = 0$,
   $a, b > 0$, with arbitrary bounded measurable data (pp. 159, 164). Lax
   expects the conjecture to hold only for hyperbolic systems with some
   further property (citing Weyl).
7. **Explicit-formula conjecture** (p. 164). For a single law with a
   concave flux, the viscosity method and the scheme both converge to the
   solution given by a maximization formula (the later Lax–Oleinik
   formula), stated here as a conjecture.
8. **Stability conjecture** (pp. 164–165). Among all maps assigning weak
   solutions to data, exactly one is continuous "in some reasonable
   topology"; offered as the reason why different viscosity and difference
   limits select the same solution. Lax notes the vagueness.
9. **Shock and contact behaviour** (pp. 177–180). Shock width depends on
   $\Delta t/h$ and is narrowest when the ratio is as large as possible;
   velocity and pressure converge faster than density and specific volume.
   Calkin's observation: if $h/\Delta t$ equals the shock speed exactly,
   the shock is reproduced with no transition; for gas dynamics this mesh
   ratio is necessarily unstable. Contact discontinuities spread: width
   $O(\sqrt n)$ after $n$ cycles, while the shock width stays constant. With
   $\Delta t/h = 1$ on $\partial_t u + \partial_x u = 0$ the data are
   translated exactly. Lax argues that contact spreading in Eulerian
   coordinates cannot be prevented without moving meshes.
10. **Rigid wall** (§2, pp. 180–181). Reflect the velocity with a sign
    change at the first lattice point beyond the wall and copy the other
    variables. The mixed problem is well posed when the number of boundary
    relations equals the number of characteristics entering the domain.
11. **Discrete travelling waves** (§3, eqs. 7–8, pp. 183–186). Conjecture:
    the steady difference equation of the Burgers scheme has a continuous
    monotone profile joining 1 to 0, unique up to a shift, and iterates
    converge to it; none exists with the end states switched: "the finite
    difference method furnishes compression shocks but no rarefaction
    shocks" (unproved). The viscous analogue is proved: profiles exist iff
    the right state is below the left one.
12. **A non-conservative scheme gives the wrong shock** (§3, pp. 186–187,
    Figs. 5–6). For $\partial_t u + u^2\partial_x u = 0$ the scheme
    $u^{n+1}_i = u^n_i - \tfrac12\left((u^n_i)^2 + (u^n_{i-1})^2\right)(u^n_i - u^n_{i-1})\,\Delta t/h$
    with step data and $\Delta t/h = 1$ converges to a steady profile moving
    at about 0.388, "appreciably" different from the weak-solution speed
    0.333; the same with perturbed data.
13. **Irreversibility** (§4, pp. 187–188). Weak solutions are
    time-reversible as a class, the physically relevant ones are not; any
    procedure giving the physical solution "must be unsymmetric in time".
14. **Proved for one equation: weak-norm stability and compactness** (§5,
    pp. 188–191). Weak norm: the largest absolute integral of a function
    over an interval. For $\partial_t u + \partial_x f(u) = \mu\,\partial_{xx} u$
    the weak-norm distance of two solutions at $t > 0$ is at most twice its
    value at $t = 0$, uniformly in $\mu$ (maximum principle for the
    integrated difference, eq. 10). Difference analogue (eqs. 11–13): with
    the discrete primitive (partial sums of $u^n$), the difference of two
    solutions obeys a recursion with **positive coefficients summing to
    one** when $\vert f'\vert\,\Delta t/h$ "should not exceed one", hence a
    maximum principle. Lemma (genuine nonlinearity, $f''$ bounded below by
    a positive constant): weak convergence of $u_n$ and of $f(u_n)$ to $u$
    and $f(u)$ holds iff $u_n \to u$ in $L^1$. Consequences: viscosity (and
    difference) solutions from bounded data of fixed support form an
    $L^1$-compact set; the solution operator is "completely continuous", a
    form of irreversibility.
15. **Several space variables** (§6, pp. 191–192). The same staggered
    scheme with the average over the $2d$ neighbours; convergence under a
    mesh-ratio restriction was proved for linear symmetric hyperbolic
    systems by K. O. Friedrichs. 2D Euler is written in conservation form
    in $\rho$, $\rho v_x$, $\rho v_y$, $E$.

# Tests

The scheme of claim 4 unless stated; computations on IBM calculators,
the Los Alamos MANIAC and the UNIVAC (p. 192). Mesh ratios are quoted as
$\Delta t/h$, not as Courant numbers.

1. **Burgers shock** (Ex. 1, p. 165, Table I). Data 1 then 0,
   $\Delta t/h = 1$, exact shock speed 1/2. After 44 and 48 cycles the
   transition sits at lattice index about 22 and 24, about five points
   from 0.99548 to 0.00210; the profile changes by one unit in the fifth
   decimal between the two cycles.
2. **Burgers rarefaction** (Exs. 2–3, pp. 167–168, Tables II–III, Figs.
   1–2). Data 0 then 1, $\Delta t/h = 1$ for 48 cycles and $1/2$ for 63;
   the smaller ratio rounds the corners.
3. **Cubic flux** (Ex. 4, p. 168, Table IV). $\partial_t u + \partial_x(u^3/3) = 0$,
   step 1 to 0, $\Delta t/h = 1$, run with the **one-sided** conservative
   scheme $u^{n+1}_i = u^n_i - \tfrac13\left[(u^n_i)^3 - (u^n_{i-1})^3\right]\Delta t/h$,
   not the averaged one; exact shock speed 1/3; transition at index $n/3$,
   two to three points wide.
4. **Euler, Eulerian, single shock** (Ex. 5, pp. 169–172, Tables V–VI).
   Left $v_x = 2$, $p = 50$, $\rho = 50$; right $v_x = 0$, $p = 0$,
   $\rho = 10$; $\gamma = 1.5$; $\Delta t/h = 0.25$. Shock speed 2.5;
   measured $124/49 = 2.48$ (49 cycles) and $248/99 = 2.50$ (99 cycles).
5. **Euler, rarefaction, contact and shock** (Ex. 6, pp. 172–175, Tables
   VII–VIII). Left velocity 1, otherwise as Ex. 5. Exact: rarefaction over
   $-0.225 < x/t < 1.47$, star state $v_x = 1.47$, $p = 27.1$, shock at
   speed 1.84; measured shock speed $88/49 = 1.79$ and $184/99 = 1.86$;
   behind the shock $v_x = 1.47 \pm 0.01$, $p \approx 27 \pm 0.3$, while
   the density "differs considerably" (smeared contact).
6. **Euler, $\gamma = 2$** (Ex. 7, p. 175, Tables IX–X). Left $v_x = 2$,
   $p = 100$, $\rho = 50$; right as Ex. 5; $\Delta t/h = 0.25$. Exact:
   rarefaction over $0 < x/t < 2.26$, $v_x = 2.26$, $p = 76.5$,
   $\rho = 30$, shock at 3.40; computed $v_x = 2.26$, $p = 76.5$. The
   ratio 0.25 exceeds the CFL limit in the star state, and "the indicated
   instability is indeed beginning to show around the shock front".
7. **Lagrangian gas dynamics** (Ex. 8, pp. 175–179, Tables XI–XII).
   Specific volume, velocity and energy in a mass coordinate; left
   $V = 1$, $v_x = 4$, $p = 8$; right $V = 3$, $v_x = 0$, $p = 0$;
   $\gamma = 2$; $\Delta t/h = 0.25$; shock speed 2, measured "exactly". A
   small bump in $V$ and energy persists near the initial discontinuity
   (reader's observation; Lax does not comment).
8. **Wall reflection** (§2, pp. 180–183, Table XIII, Fig. 3). Ex. 8 data
   against a wall; $\Delta t/h = 0.25$ for 102 cycles, then 0.07 for 650
   more; the reflected shock lies between indices 91 and 96 against the
   predicted 91. Running at 0.07 throughout gave substantially the same
   result.
9. **Steady discrete profiles** (§3, pp. 183–185, Tables XIV–XV, Fig. 4).
   Burgers with perturbed data: the profiles are translates (shift about
   0.2), supporting claim 11.
10. **Non-conservative scheme** (§3, pp. 186–187, Figs. 5–6): claim 12.

**No "Lax shock tube" here.** The data
$(\rho, v_x, p) = (0.445, 0.698, 3.528)$ | $(0.5, 0, 0.571)$ with
$\gamma = 1.4$, which the bundle's
[Lax shock tube](/benchmarks/lax-shock-tube.md) page attributes to
"Lax 1954", appear nowhere in pp. 159–193 (reader's check of the text
layer and of every table on the page images). The paper's Euler
Riemann problems are Exs. 5–8 above, with $\gamma = 1.5$ or 2.

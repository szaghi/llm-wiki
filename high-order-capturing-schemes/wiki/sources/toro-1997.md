# Summary

A graduate textbook on Godunov-type shock-capturing methods, presented
through the 1D (and $x$-split 3D) Euler equations. Its core, and the part
read for this bundle, is:

* a complete exact Riemann solver for ideal and covolume gases: one
  nonlinear pressure equation solved by Newton–Raphson, explicit wave
  relations, a sampling procedure and closed-form vacuum solutions
  (Ch. 4);
* Godunov's first-order method built on it (Ch. 6);
* approximate Riemann solvers, each run through first-order Godunov on
  the same 1D tests: approximate-state solvers (PVRS, TRRS, TSRS and
  adaptive hybrids, Ch. 9), HLL and the author's HLLC with pressure-based
  wave speeds (Ch. 10), Roe's solver with the Harten–Hyman entropy fix
  (Ch. 11), and Osher's solver with P- and O-ordering (Ch. 12).

The common test set (Tables 4.1 and 6.2: 100 cells, Courant number 0.9,
transmissive boundaries) is the origin of the "Toro tests" later papers
reuse: the modified Sod problem, the 123 problem, the two halves of the
Woodward–Colella blast wave and their shock collision. §§17.1–17.2 give
the 2D/3D explosion test and two single-Mach-reflection wedge runs
against Takayama's photographs. Later chapters (not read) cover WAF,
MUSCL–Hancock and centred TVD schemes, source-term splitting and
multi-D methods; the 1st edition has no MUSTA or ADER chapter.

# Key claims

1. **Pressure equation** (§4.2, Prop. 4.2.1, eqs. 4.5–4.9). The star
   pressure is the root of the sum of two one-sided functions (shock
   branch above the side pressure, rarefaction branch below) plus the
   velocity jump; the star velocity follows explicitly (4.9). The
   function is monotone and concave down (§4.3.1, eqs. 4.37–4.38), so a
   positive root is unique when it exists
   ([exact Riemann solver](/methods/exact-riemann-solver.md)).
2. **Vacuum** (§4.3.1, eq. 4.40; §4.6, eqs. 4.68–4.82). A positive star
   pressure requires $\frac{2c^-}{\gamma-1} + \frac{2c^+}{\gamma-1} > v_n^+ - v_n^-$;
   otherwise "vacuum is created by the non-linear waves" and a
   closed-form solution applies (4.81–4.82). A shock cannot be adjacent
   to vacuum (Prop. 4.6.1). An approximate solver's vacuum condition
   "will in general be different" (p. 143).
3. **Newton–Raphson and initial guesses** (§4.3.2, eqs. 4.44–4.49,
   Table 4.2). Two-rarefaction, linearized (PV), two-shock and
   arithmetic-mean guesses; tolerance $10^{-6}$ on the relative change.
   "By excluding Test 2, $p_{\mathrm{TS}}$ is the best guess overall"
   (p. 130); the program uses a hybrid, and "the overwhelming majority of
   Riemann problems … can be accurately approximated by the simple value
   $p_{\mathrm{PV}}$" (p. 133).
4. **Wave relations and sampling** (§§4.4–4.5, eqs. 4.50–4.67,
   Fig. 4.14), and the covolume-gas version (§4.7, eqs. 4.84–4.104),
   which needs an extra iteration only inside rarefactions.
5. **Godunov's method** (§§6.2–6.3, eqs. 6.11–6.27). The flux is the
   physical flux of the exact solution on the $t$-axis, and the scheme is
   stable for Courant number up to 1, more generous than the
   no-interaction limit $\tfrac12$ (6.9 vs 6.13), because only the
   $t$-axis value is needed. Ten cases are sampled for $x/t = 0$
   (Fig. 6.5, Table 6.1). The common estimate of the largest speed from
   $\max_i(\vert v_i\vert + c_i)$ "can lead to an underestimate"
   (eq. 6.20), so the runs reduce the Courant number by a factor 0.2 for
   the first 5 steps (Remark 6.4.1). A reflective wall is a mirror state;
   its boundary Riemann problem is two shocks or two rarefactions with
   zero star velocity, solvable in closed form (§6.3.3).
6. **Entropy glitch** (§6.4.1, p. 216). On the modified Sod test, exact
   Godunov shows "the small discontinuous jump within the rarefaction …
   sometimes referred to as the entropy glitch", which "arises only in
   the presence of sonic rarefaction waves"; since Godunov is
   "theoretically entropy satisfying", its size should vanish as
   $h \to 0$.
7. **Slowly moving shocks** (§6.4.1, p. 217; §12.4, p. 373). On scheme
   Test 4 the slow left shock is "sharply resolved (two cells) but is not
   monotone", with low-frequency oscillations, "so far … an unresolved
   difficulty" (citing Roberts, Billett & Toro, Arora & Roe). Osher's
   scheme with O-ordering does not show them, "a distinguishing property
   of the Osher approach" ([slowly moving shock anomaly](/definitions/slowly-moving-shock-anomaly.md)).
8. **Approximate-state solvers** (Ch. 9, eqs. 9.19–9.49). PVRS (a
   linearization of the primitive-variable equations, exact for an
   isolated contact or shear wave), TRRS (exact for two rarefactions,
   detectable a priori) and TSRS (two-shock, non-iterative). Direct
   quadratic two-shock approximations are rejected because non-unique and
   complex roots "can occur very often" (p. 283). An adaptive scheme uses
   PVRS when the pressure ratio is below $Q_{\mathrm{user}} = 2$ and the
   PVRS pressure lies between the data pressures; "over 90% of all
   Riemann problems are handled by the cheap linearised Riemann solver"
   (p. 285), and the non-iterative PVRS–TRRS–TSRS scheme "is recommended
   for practical applications" (p. 286).
9. **HLL** (§§10.2–10.3, eqs. 10.13, 10.18–10.21, 10.62). The two-wave
   model is exact only for $2\times2$ systems. For Euler, the smearing of
   stationary contacts, shear waves and material interfaces "is
   unacceptable" (p. 293). For a passive scalar HLL is more diffusive than
   Godunov whenever the flow is subsonic (§10.7).
10. **HLLC** (§10.4, eqs. 10.26–10.36). Star states and flux as printed,
    with $S_* = u_*$ (10.30) and passive scalars carried by the star
    states (10.36); infinite resolution of a stationary contact (§10.7,
    Fig. 10.9) ([HLLC Riemann solver](/methods/hllc-riemann-solver.md)).
11. **Wave-speed estimates** (§10.5, eqs. 10.37–10.58). Davis's direct
    and min/max estimates; Roe-average eigenvalues credited to "Davis [94]
    and Einfeldt [105]"; Rusanov and Lax–Friedrichs as special cases
    (10.42–10.45); Einfeldt's $\bar u \mp \bar d$ form (10.46–10.48);
    pressure-based estimates from PVRS, TRRS, TSRS or the adaptive
    scheme, used in practice (10.49–10.56, p. 305); Batten et al.'s
    middle-wave speed (10.57–10.58).
12. **Roe** (§§11.1–11.5). Three equivalent flux forms (11.27–11.29); the
    Roe–Pike wave strengths and averages (11.113, 11.118), "identical to
    the Roe averages obtained by the original Roe method" (p. 333); the
    Harten–Hyman entropy fix (11.119–11.134) with star speeds from the
    Roe states, PVRS, TRRS, TSRS or the adaptive scheme ("even better",
    p. 339). Without the fix Roe computes an entropy-violating jump in the
    sonic rarefaction of Test 1 (Fig. 11.4); with it the result is
    "almost indistinguishable" from exact Godunov (Fig. 11.5). "The Roe
    method, as described, does actually fail" on Test 2 (p. 340);
    Einfeldt et al. "showed that in fact this anomaly afflicts all
    linearised Riemann solvers" (p. 313).
13. **Osher** (§12.3, eqs. 12.94–12.96). O-ordering (Osher's original)
    gives unrealistically large intersection pressures in strong
    rarefactions and fails on Test 2; it is undefined, and the scheme
    fails, when $c^- + c^+ + \tfrac{\gamma-1}{2}(v_n^+ - v_n^-) \le 0$
    (12.96), which strong shocks reach (Ch. 12 Test 5). P-ordering works
    on both but shows "spurious oscillations in Test 5 near the shocks"
    (p. 373).
14. **HLLC near sonic points** (§10.8, p. 308; §11.5.2, p. 340). On
    Test 1 HLLC is "more accurate than the corresponding results using the
    exact Riemann solver" near the sonic point: "correct handling of
    sonic flow is a distinctive feature of HLL-type Riemann solvers". HLL
    and HLLC are "virtually indistinguishable" on Tests 1–4.
15. **Multi-D tests** (§§17.1–17.2), read beyond the main scope: the 2D
    and 3D explosion
    ([multidimensional explosion](/benchmarks/multidimensional-explosion.md))
    and two single-Mach-reflection wedge runs
    ([double Mach reflection](/benchmarks/double-mach-reflection.md);
    [regular and Mach reflection](/definitions/mach-reflection.md)).

**Findings** (first order, 100 cells):
* *Exact Godunov* (§6.4.1, Figs. 6.8–6.11): Test 1 shock about 4 cells,
  contact about 20, monotone, glitch at the sonic point; Test 2 good in
  $\rho$, $v$, $p$, poor in the specific internal energy
  $p/((\gamma-1)\rho)$; Test 3 contact worst, post-shock density not
  attained, velocity overshoot at the rarefaction tail; Test 4 right
  shock over 5 cells, slow left shock over 2 cells but non-monotone,
  contact heavily smeared.
* *Lax–Friedrichs* (§6.4.2): odd–even pairing of cell values, shock
  about 8 cells, very poor rarefaction and contact, inaccurate internal
  energy on Test 2, post-shock density not attained on Test 3. The
  second-order Richtmyer scheme oscillates on Test 1 and fails on
  Tests 2–4.
* *PVRS* (Figs. 9.5–9.8): comparable to exact, with slight oscillations
  behind the right shock of Test 4. *Adaptive PVRS–TRRS–TSRS*
  (Figs. 9.9–9.12): comparable to exact; TSRS and TRRS "almost
  indistinguishable" (not shown).
* *HLLC* (Figs. 10.5–10.8): best near the sonic point of Test 1; less
  accurate in velocity and internal energy on Test 2 (Fig. 10.6), but no
  failure; Tests 3–4 like exact. *HLL vs HLLC* on Ch. 10 Tests 5–6
  (Fig. 10.9): HLL smears the stationary contact over the whole domain
  by $t = 2$; HLLC resolves it exactly and the moving one sharply.
* *Roe* (Figs. 11.4–11.7): rarefaction shock in Test 1 without the fix,
  close to exact with the Harten–Hyman fix; fails on Test 2; Tests 3–4
  close to exact.
* *Osher, O-ordering* (Figs. 12.5–12.7): Test 1 close to exact including
  the sonic point; Tests 3–4 good, the slow shock without spurious
  oscillations; fails on Tests 2 and 5. *P-ordering* (Figs. 12.8–12.12):
  runs all five, with oscillations near the shocks of Test 5.

**Multi-D** (Ch. 17, read beyond the scope of Chs. 4–12):
* *Explosion* (§17.1.1): $[0, 2]^2$, a circle of radius 0.4 centred at
  $(1, 1)$, $(\rho, v_x, v_y, p) = (1, 0, 0, 1)$ inside and
  $(0.125, 0, 0, 0.1)$ outside, $\gamma = 1.4$, $t = 0.25$; WAF with
  dimensional splitting, HLLC and the VANLEER limiter, Courant number
  0.9, $101\times101$ cells, area-weighted initial data in the cells cut
  by the circle; reference from the 1D radial equations with geometric
  source (eqs. 17.1–17.2) by the random choice method, 1000 cells,
  Courant number 0.4. Shock resolved with two mesh points, contact with
  two to three (Fig. 17.4). The 3D version (§17.1.3): cube
  $[0, 2]^3$, sphere of radius 0.4 at $(1, 1, 1)$, same data,
  $t = 0.25$.
* *Shock reflection from wedges* (§17.2): domain $25.0\times16.5$,
  wedge apex at $x = 4.69$, initial shock at $x = 4.0$, ambient air at
  rest with $\rho = 1.225$ kg/m³, $p = 1.01325\times10^5$ Pa, post-shock
  state from the Rankine–Hugoniot relations. Start-up errors are avoided
  by letting the shock run 90 % of the way to the apex and then resetting
  the state behind it. WAF, dimensional splitting, HLLC, VANLEER, Courant
  number 0.9. $M_s = 1.7$ on a 25° wedge: a regular curvilinear
  $800\times528$ mesh and a CHIMERA–AMR–Cartesian run (base
  $50\times33$, two levels, factor 4), single Mach reflection,
  "qualitative agreement … is excellent" with a photograph by
  K. Takayama (Fig. 17.9). $M_s = 1.2$ on a 30° wedge: $420\times240$
  curvilinear mesh, single Mach reflection, "satisfactory". On this case
  Colella's PLM with HLLC is close to WAF–HLLC, while PLM with HLL smears
  the slip surface "very significantly" (Figs. 17.10–17.12; PLM runs
  courtesy of T. Saito). Only photographs are compared; no triple-point
  angles or positions are printed.

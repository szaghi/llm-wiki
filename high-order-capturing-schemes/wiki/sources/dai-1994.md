# Summary

Dai and Woodward build a nonlinear approximate Riemann solver for the
full seven-variable 1D ideal-MHD system in a Lagrangian mass coordinate,
to supply interface fluxes to a PPM-type Godunov scheme. Every wave of
the fan is a discontinuity: two fast shocks, two rotational
discontinuities, two slow shocks and a contact, bounding eight regions.
Rarefactions are replaced by "rarefaction shocks" that obey the jump
conditions with an entropy decrease, so the solver is exact when no
rarefaction is present and is meant for weak rarefactions. Closed
formulas give the shock speed as a function of the post-shock transverse
field, and a cubic gives the jump as a function of the shock speed; a
Newton iteration on three transverse-field values and one field
orientation closes the solution at the contact. Its best-known result is
a regular solution of the Brio–Wu states, with no compound wave, from
which the authors conclude that the ideal-MHD Riemann problem is not
unique. Merged into the [Brio–Wu shock tube](/benchmarks/brio-wu-shock-tube.md),
[Ryu–Jones and Dai–Woodward shock tubes](/benchmarks/ryu-jones-shock-tubes.md),
[approximate Riemann solvers](/methods/approximate-riemann-solvers.md) and
[nonconvexity of ideal MHD](/results/mhd-nonconvexity.md).

# Key claims

1. **Lagrangian system** (§2, eqs. 2.1–2.7): seven variables (specific
   volume, pressure, three velocity components, $B_y$, $B_z$) in the mass
   coordinate $dm = \rho\,dx$, $B_x$ constant, Gaussian units. Alfvén waves
   need the full three-component system: they "do not occur generally if
   we demand that $u_z = 0$ and $B_z = 0$" (paper $u_z$ = $v_z$).
2. **Wave speeds** (§3). The Lagrangian (mass-flux) speeds are $\rho$
   times the Eulerian $c$, $c_A$, $c_f$, $c_s$. The entropy invariant is
   $p/\rho^\gamma$; the other six Riemann invariants exist only as inexact
   differentials.
3. **Linear solver** (§3, Fig. 4). The central state is found by holding
   each Riemann invariant constant along straight characteristics from
   the two sides. It is exact only for weak jumps and serves as an initial
   guess; special invariants are given for $B_\perp = 0$ and $B_x = 0$.
4. **Jump conditions** (§4, eqs. 3.1–3.7), written with the Lagrangian
   discontinuity speed for mass, momentum, transverse flux and energy.
5. **Rarefaction shocks** (§4, p. 359): "we will approximate rarefactions
   by rarefaction shocks obeying the jump conditions given above (and
   involving decreases in the entropy). This approximation limits the
   applicability of this work to weak rarefactions." Hence (§5, p. 366):
   "For those MHD Riemann problems which involve no rarefaction waves, our
   Riemann solver is exact."
6. **Discontinuity catalogue** (§4): contact, tangential (only for
   $B_x = 0$), fast, slow and rotational discontinuities, with switch-on
   and switch-off shocks as limits. Across fast and slow shocks the
   transverse field keeps its orientation, $B_z/B_y$ unchanged (eq. 4b);
   only a rotational discontinuity changes the sign of the transverse
   components (p. 368). **Intermediate shocks are excluded** from the
   solver (p. 360).
7. **Shock relations** (§4, eqs. 6–10). For a given pre-shock state the
   jump in $B_y$ solves a cubic whose coefficients depend on the shock
   speed (eq. 6; root chosen by the entropy condition), and the squared
   fast or slow shock speed is a closed formula in the jump (eq. 9; eq. 10
   for $B_x = 0$). As the shock speed tends to infinity the jump in $B_y$
   tends to $\frac{2}{\gamma-1}$ times its pre-shock value, and the
   compression to the hydrodynamic limit $\rho_1/\rho_0 = (\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$
   (eqs. 7a–b).
8. **Algorithm** (§5, pp. 363–366). Guess the transverse field behind the
   two fast shocks and the left slow shock, and a common field
   orientation between the rotations; fast shocks, rotations at the
   Alfvén speed and slow shocks then fix all regions; Newton's method
   enforces continuity of the three velocity components and of the
   pressure at the contact (eqs. 11a–d), with derivatives by the chain
   rule. For $B_x = 0$ the iteration is on the common total pressure,
   "following the method used in PPM for the hydrodynamic case"
   ([PPM](/methods/ppm.md)). The initial guess blends the linear solver
   with a single-strong-wave guess, weighted by the largest normalized
   jump in the invariants; for several strong waves the Newton update is
   relaxed by a factor $1 - f^N$ ($0 < f < 1$, $N$ the iteration count).
   When the solver fails, the code uses the average of the left and right
   states for the flux (p. 371).
9. **Planar variant** (§5, p. 371): with $B_z = v_z = 0$ there is no
   Alfvén wave; the procedure iterates on three transverse-field values.
10. **The Brio–Wu states have a regular solution** (p. 368, Tables
    IVa–b). At $\gamma = 2$ and $B_x = 2.6587$ (Gaussian, $= 0.75\sqrt{4\pi}$)
    the solver returns two fast rarefactions (computed as rarefaction
    shocks), two slow shocks (Mach 2.2 to the right, 1.05 to the left),
    one rotational discontinuity with "−180° rotation" (Table IVb:
    $1.7992\times10^2$ degrees) and a contact. From the post-fast-wave
    states the problem has no rarefaction, so the solver is exact; a 1D
    simulation from those states still forms the compound wave of Brio &
    Wu and of Zachary & Colella. "Apparently, this Riemann problem does
    not have a unique solution." §6: the compound wave "disappears in our
    solutions from the MHD Riemann solver, but it will be generated
    through the conservation law … no matter what approximation we use";
    planar initial data can still produce rotational discontinuities, so
    the three-component model is necessary.
11. **Cost** (§6, p. 372): one Newton iteration is "enough to obtain
    sufficiently accurate fluxes in an actual code even for very strong
    MHD shocks"; unoptimized, with one iteration, the solver does
    "348,143 Riemann problems per second on a Cray-C90". The linear solver
    is suggested away from strong waves.
12. **Degenerate speeds** (p. 359, citing Brio & Wu): the solution can
    depend on the neglected dissipation; the paper leaves that choice to
    the programmer.

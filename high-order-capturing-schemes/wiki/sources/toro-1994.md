# Summary

The paper adds the missing contact wave to the two-wave HLL solver and
names the result **HLLC** (§§1, 4). The middle wave speed $S_M$ is set
equal to the star normal velocity; the left and right star states follow
from the integral conservation law across $S_L$ and across $S_R$
separately, with that normal velocity (eqs. 18–28), and the supersonic
cases get their own star fluxes (eqs. 29–30). It proposes three families
of wave-speed estimates, isentropic (two-rarefaction), linearized
(primitive-variable) and "hybrid" pressure-based ones that use the exact
shock and rarefaction speed relations for an estimated star pressure (§3,
eqs. 10–17), plus an iterative revision through the HLL average state
(§3.4). HLLC is used only inside the second-order TVD WAF method (§5,
eqs. 31–36) and tested on Sod, a sonic Sod, the Woodward–Colella blast
waves, a covolume-gas shock tube and a Mach 1.7, 25° wedge reflection
against a Takayama interferogram (§6). The stated motivation is efficient
Godunov-type methods for non-ideal gases (§1, eq. 3). Merged into
[HLLC](/methods/hllc-riemann-solver.md) and [HLL](/methods/hll-riemann-solver.md).

# Key claims

1. **The HLL defect** (§1). HLL with Davis or Einfeldt speeds is "very
   efficient, robust and simple" and handles sonic flow "remarkably well",
   but neglects the intermediate wave and so ruins contacts, and in 2D/3D
   vortex sheets and shear waves; contacts matter in combustion.
2. **HLL recalled** (§2.4, eqs. 7–9). The star state
   $u^{\mathrm{HLL}}_*$ and the three-branch HLL flux as on the
   [HLL page](/methods/hll-riemann-solver.md). Davis's observation:
   $S_R = h/\Delta t$, $S_L = -S_R$ gives the Lax–Friedrichs flux (p. 27).
3. **Isentropic estimates** (§3.1, eqs. 10–11). Two-rarefaction star
   velocity and sound speed, then $S_L$ is the smaller of $v_n^- - c^-$ and
   $v_{n*}$ minus the star sound speed, and $S_R$ the larger of
   $v_n^+ + c^+$ and $v_{n*}$ plus it. The printed star velocity
   carries $\tfrac12(u_L - u_R)$ where the two-rarefaction solution has
   $\tfrac12(u_L + u_R)$ (checked at 300 dpi; recorded as printed).
4. **Linearized estimates** (§3.2, eqs. 12–14), from Toro (1991): star
   pressure, velocity and densities of the primitive-variable linearization
   with arithmetic-mean density and sound speed; then the star sound speeds
   and the min/max speeds as in claim 3.
5. **Hybrid (pressure-based) estimates** (§3.3, eqs. 15–16).
   $S_L = v_n^- - c^-q^{\mathrm w}_L$, $S_M = v_{n*}$,
   $S_R = v_n^+ + c^+q^{\mathrm w}_R$, with $q^{\mathrm w} = 1$ if
   $p_* \le p^\mp$ and
   $\left[1 + \frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}(p_*/p^\mp - 1)\right]^{1/2}$ otherwise.
   Tested with $p_*$, $v_{n*}$ from the linearized estimate, "very
   satisfactory". "The choice of $p^*$ is more critical than that of
   $u^*$"; an estimate above the exact star pressure is desirable, so that
   shock speeds are overestimated.
6. **Two-rarefaction pressure, ideal and covolume gas** (§3.3, eq. 17),
   with the **conjecture** that experience suggests $p_{\mathrm{TR}}$ is
   at least the exact $p_*$, "a conjecture that would be useful to prove".
7. **Iterative revision** (§3.4). Isentropic and linearized choices are
   "not sufficiently robust for very severe flow regimes", hybrid ones
   "both accurate and very robust". A revision recomputes density,
   velocity, pressure and sound speed from $u^{\mathrm{HLL}}_*$ and then
   the speeds: "very robust estimates". The tests said to be "reported in
   Sect. 5" are not in the paper.
8. **HLLC star states** (§4, eqs. 18–28, Fig. 4). Across each outer wave,
   the star flux is $f(u^\mp) + S_{L,R}(u^{\mathrm{HLLC}}_{*L,R} - u^\mp)$
   (18–19); with the star normal velocity equal to $S_M$, the star density,
   pressure and energy follow on each side (22–24, 26–28). The left and
   right star pressures, $p^\mp + \rho^\mp(S_{L,R} - v_n^\mp)(S_M - v_n^\mp)$
   (eqs. 23, 27, compiler's expansion), are **computed separately and are
   generally unequal** for an independently estimated $S_M$. Equalizing
   them by their mean "seems appropriate", but "numerical experiments
   suggest that such condition might not be necessary"; the estimated $p_*$
   could be used in the energies instead (p. 29).
9. **Supersonic cases** (eqs. 29–30, Figs. 5–6). For $S_M \le S_R \le 0$
   and for $0 \le S_L \le S_M$ the star flux of the far side is given in
   closed form, because WAF uses the fluxes of all wave regions. **The
   four-branch first-order HLLC flux is not printed.**
10. **WAF** (§5.1, eqs. 31–36). The interface flux is a weighted average
    of the fluxes of the wave regions, weights from the wave Courant
    numbers, with a TVD flux limiter on each wave jump. With exact solvers
    the state nearest the $t$-axis is replaced by the sonic solution, but
    "when using the HLLC-Riemann solver no special measures to deal with
    sonic flow are required" (p. 30).
11. **Conclusions** (§7). Results "comparable to those obtained using the
    exact Riemann solver and far superior" to HLL; savings against the
    exact solver "modest (about 50 %)" for the simple equations of state
    used, expected larger for complex ones; advantages: simplicity and
    correct handling of sonic flow.

# Tests

All with WAF (second order, TVD), 1D on the unit interval unless stated.

1. **Sod** (§6.1, Figs. 8–9). $\gamma = 1.4$, covolume 0;
   $(1, 0, 1) \mid (0.125, 0, 0.1)$ at the midpoint; 100 cells, Courant
   number 0.8, $t = 0.25$, Superbee. HLL (hybrid speeds) smears the
   contact over "about 20 points", HLLC over "about 4". Specific internal
   energy is "the most useful variable for assessing computed results"
   ([Sod shock tube](/benchmarks/sod-shock-tube.md)).
2. **Sonic Sod** (§6.1, Fig. 10). Left velocity $3/4$, discontinuity at
   $x = 0.4$, 100 cells, $t = 0.25$; the other data are not restated. HLLC
   "retains the good property of HLLC [read HLL] in dealing with sonic
   points"; no quantitative comparison.
3. **Woodward–Colella blast waves** (§6.2, Figs. 11–13). $\gamma = 1.4$,
   diaphragms at 0.1 and 0.9, $\rho = 1$, $v_x = 0$, $p = 1000, 0.01, 100$;
   boundary conditions not stated. 3000 cells, $t = 0.028$: HLLC and exact
   show "no difference"; on the strongest contact HLL spreads "about 450
   points", HLLC "less than 10". 500 cells, $t = 0.038$: "obvious
   superiority" of HLLC
   ([interacting blast waves](/benchmarks/interacting-blast-waves.md)).
4. **Covolume shock tube** (§6.3, Fig. 14). "First suggested by Einfeldt
   (1988)" (his Aachen proceedings paper, `einfeldt-1988-b`, not
   [Einfeldt (1988)](/sources/einfeldt-1988.md)); Sod-like with covolume
   0.8, $t = 0.20$; initial data not restated. Contact resolution "very
   satisfactory".
5. **Mach reflection** (§6.4, Figs. 15–17). $M_s = 1.7$ on a 25° wedge,
   $350\times200$ cells, 100 density contours; HLLC against the exact
   solver and a Takayama holographic interferogram; the slip line from the
   triple point is "virtually identical" to the exact solver's.
   Qualitative only ([double Mach reflection](/benchmarks/double-mach-reflection.md)).

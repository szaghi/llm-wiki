# Summary

Shu asks for time discretizations of method-of-lines TVD, TVB and ENO
schemes that keep the TVD property of forward Euler. Linear stability
analysis of ODE solvers, he argues, says nothing about convergence when the
solution has discontinuities (§1). For steady states (§2) he gives an
$s$-stage Runge–Kutta-type scheme, first order in time, whose nonlinear
incremental coefficients are frozen at the start of each cycle. One cycle
is then the $s$-th power of a forward-Euler-type operator and is TVD under
CFL number $s$ (Proposition 2.1). For time-dependent problems (§3) he
defines multilevel (multistep) schemes and proves them TVD by writing each
step as a convex combination of forward-Euler steps, using a downwind-type
operator where a coefficient is negative (Proposition 3.1). He shows that
TVD multistep schemes of every order exist (Corollary 3.1) and tabulates
twelve of them up to fifth order (Table 1). The construction extends to
source terms (TVB) and to $d$ dimensions with a maximum-norm bound. The
tests (§4) are scalar steady-state problems for the §2 scheme only.

This is **not** the paper that introduces the second- and third-order TVD
Runge–Kutta methods: those are in
[Shu & Osher (1988)](/sources/shu-1988.md), which this paper cites as its
[16] (Remark 3.3). No coefficient of those methods is printed here.

# Key claims

1. **Motivation** (§1, p. 1074). For ODE solvers in method-of-lines form
   "it seems that only linear stability analysis is available in the
   literature"; "linear stability is not enough for convergence in the
   presence of discontinuities, and a stronger nonlinear stability such as
   TVD is needed."
2. **Building block** (§2, eqs. 2.2–2.3). The TVD schemes considered can
   be written in incremental form, the new value being the old one plus a
   nonnegative coefficient times $\Delta^1 u_i$ minus a nonnegative
   coefficient times $\Delta^1 u_{i-1}$, with $\Delta t/h$ times the sum of
   the two coefficients at an interface at most 1 (2.3a–c). The ENO
   schemes of Shu & Osher fit this form. An example of a severe
   forward-Euler restriction (eq. 2.1): mesh ratio $2/5$ for the
   third-order $\beta$-scheme of Osher & Chakravarthy.
3. **Steady-state multistage scheme** (§2, eqs. 2.4–2.6). Each stage
   applies the incremental operator, with its coefficients frozen at the
   start of the cycle, to the previous stage, scaled by the stage
   coefficient $i/(s(s-i+1))$, $i = 1,\dots,s$; the last coefficient is
   1, so the scheme is first order in time. It has the form of Jameson's
   and Turkel's multistage schemes "except the way of freezing nonlinear
   parts"; Jameson's four-stage scheme uses $1/(5-i)$, and those schemes
   were analysed only for linear stability, under a CFL restriction less
   than $s$ (2.8 for four stages) (p. 1075).
4. **Proposition 2.1** (eqs. 2.7–2.10). If the building block is TVD
   under (2.3c), the scheme is TVD under CFL number $s$ (2.7). Proof: with
   the frozen linear operator, the stages expand into powers of it
   (2.9–2.10), and with the stage coefficients (2.6) the cycle equals the
   $s$-th power of the identity plus $1/s$ times that operator, a power of
   a TVD operator. The scheme is a linear iteration with frozen
   coefficients, not a Runge–Kutta method applied to the nonlinear
   $\mathcal L$.
5. **Remarks 2.1–2.4.** With upwind differencing (2.7) is optimal by the
   domain of dependence. The nonlinear coefficients are computed once per
   cycle, a saving that grows for systems and on vector machines. $s$ may
   vary with the residue $\Vert u^n - u^{n-1}\Vert$, small early and large
   near steady state: on a forced Burgers boundary-value problem with two
   entropy-satisfying steady states, a fixed large $s$ ($s \ge 6$) started
   from the unstable state stays there, while variable $s$ "converges very
   fast to the correct solution" (Remark 2.3; no data shown).
6. **Multilevel TVD schemes** (§3, eqs. 3.1–3.8). In registry form an
   $m$-step scheme is
   $$u^{n+1} = \sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(a_i u^{n+1-i} + \Delta t\,b_i\,\mathcal L(u^{n+1-i})\right),$$
   with $\tilde{\mathcal L}$ in place of $\mathcal L$ where $b_i < 0$,
   $a_i \ge 0$, and $a_i = 0$ only if $b_i = 0$ (3.8). The downwind
   operator is the same TVD scheme applied to the time-reversed equation
   and is TVD under the same condition; it "does not mean the inverse" of
   $\mathcal L$ (3.6). The paper indexes levels by the number of *past*
   levels, so its $m$ is the registry $m - 1$.
7. **Proposition 3.1.** (a) If $\sum_i a_i = 1$ (3.9), the scheme is TVD
   for $\Delta t \le \min_i(a_i/\vert b_i\vert)\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{FE}}$
   (3.10). Proof: the step is the convex combination, with weights $a_i$,
   of forward-Euler steps of size $(\vert b_i\vert/a_i)\Delta t$ from the
   past levels (3.12–3.13), so the total variation at every level is
   bounded by its maximum over the starting levels (3.16). (b) It is of order
   $p_{\mathrm t}$ in time if (3.9) holds and (3.11a–b) hold, which,
   re-indexed to the registry form above, read
   $$\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left[(i-1)^{l}a_i - l\,(i-1)^{l-1}b_i\right] = (-1)^{l},\qquad l = 1,\dots,p_{\mathrm t}$$
   (with $0^0 = 1$; compiler's re-indexing, checked on every row of
   Table 1). The Taylor derivation is referred to Lambert. This is the
   convexity argument of the later SSP framework, here for multistep
   methods and for the total variation only.
8. **Corollary 3.1** (eq. 3.17). For any TVD space discretization of
   order $r$ there are TVD multistep schemes of global order $r$ with
   positive CFL number: take $m = p_{\mathrm t} = r$ and the $a_i$ given;
   the order conditions are then an $r\times r$ linear system for the
   $b_i$ with a Vandermonde-type determinant $(r-1)!\,V(1,\dots,r-1) \ne 0$,
   e.g. with all $a_i = 1/r$. The CFL number vanishes only if some
   $a_i = 0$ with $b_i \ne 0$. Existence only; the $b_i$ may be negative.
9. **Remarks 3.1–3.3.** The coefficients are chosen to maximize (3.10),
   and more levels give a larger CFL number. Requiring all $b_i \ge 0$
   avoids $\tilde{\mathcal L}$, which "will reduce the work of each
   timestep by one half". Without $\tilde{\mathcal L}$ a negative $b_i$
   means solving the equation backward in time, "a well-known disaster",
   equivalent for an upwind scheme to a downwind scheme, "unstable in any
   sense" (Remark 3.2). Runge–Kutta-type methods are self-starting and
   need less storage, but "for very high-order methods (order $\ge 5$)
   multilevel methods may be simpler" (Remark 3.3). TVB versions work
   verbatim, and Shu's TVB paper ran TVB schemes with (3.7) (p. 1077).
10. **Table 1** (p. 1079): twelve multistep methods of orders 2–5,
    "by no means exhaustive, nor … the optimal of the corresponding
    order"; reproduced in registry form on
    [SSP linear multistep methods](/methods/ssp-linear-multistep.md).
    Every fourth- and fifth-order method has a negative $b_i$; the
    second- and third-order methods with all $b_i \ge 0$ are the three-
    and four-step second-order ones ($\mathcal C = 0.5$, $0.67$) and the
    four-, five- and six-step third-order ones ($0.33$, $0.5$, $0.57$).
    The four-step third-order row is inconsistent as printed (see
    Citations).
11. **Source terms** (§3, eqs. 3.18–3.20, Proposition 3.2). With a
    source Lipschitz in $u$ and $x$, the operators with the source added
    give TVB schemes of the same order. Lax–Wendroff-type (Taylor) time
    discretizations with a $u$-dependent source are "very messy" and may
    lose the TVD or TVB property.
12. **Several dimensions** (§3, eqs. 3.21–3.29, Propositions 3.3–3.4).
    Dimension-by-dimension sums keep the order (Proposition 3.3), so all
    of Table 1 applies in multi-D. The scheme is bounded in the maximum
    norm for
    $\Delta t \le \frac{1}{2d}\min_i(a_i/\vert b_i\vert)\,\Delta t_{\mathrm{FE}}$
    (3.24), with $\Delta t_{\mathrm{FE}}$ the one-dimensional limit; the
    proof splits each step into $d$ directional forward-Euler steps
    (3.25–3.28). The bound can be doubled using the conservation form;
    the factor $1/d$ then amounts to a CFL condition on the sum of the
    directional wave speeds, "which is natural" (Remark 3.4). Maximum-norm
    boundedness "does not provide enough
    information to imply convergence".
13. **Goodman–LeVeque, quoted** (p. 1082, eq. 3.30). No local-stencil
    scheme of order above one is proven TVD in $d \ge 2$; Goodman and
    LeVeque proved that in 2D a TVD scheme, for the total variation (3.30)
    summed over both directions with mesh-size weights, is at most first
    order. Shu: this does not exclude high-order TVB multi-D schemes;
    dimension-split 1D TVD schemes do increase (3.30) numerically, mostly
    by "distortions of level curves rather than by oscillations", so (3.30)
    "may not be the best choice" of a 2D total variation.
14. **Context** (pp. 1074, 1077). TVD schemes degenerate to first order
    at nonsonic critical points, which TVB modifications remedy. Fully
    discrete Lax–Wendroff time discretizations are proven TVD only at
    second order. TVD space discretizations with the TVB modification
    reach order 15. ENO is not proven TVB, "however, see [15]".

# Tests

All in §4: scalar steady-state problems for the §2 scheme, with the
third-order $\beta$-scheme of Osher & Chakravarthy in space and a TVB
boundary treatment (extrapolation, fixed inflow data, the scheme computes
the outflow).

1. **Linear equations** (Example 1, eqs. 4.1–4.2, Table 2).
   $u_t + u_x = -e^{-x}$ on $[0, 1]$, $u(0, t) = 1$, $u(x, 0) = 1$,
   steady state $e^{-x}$; and $u_t + u_x = \pi\cos\pi x$ on $[-1, 1]$,
   $u(-1, t) = 0$, $u(x, 0) = 0$, steady state $\sin\pi x$. Forward Euler
   ($s = 1$) against variable $s = 2, 4, 6$ when the $L_1$ residue
   $\Vert u^n - u^{n-1}\Vert_1$ is $> 1$, in $[0.01, 0.1]$ or $< 0.01$
   (as printed); stop at $\Vert u^n - u^{n-1}\Vert_1 < 10^{-4}\Delta t$.

   | eq. | $h$ | $L_1$ error | $L_\infty$ error | $s = 1$ steps | variable $s$: cycles / stages |
   |---|---|---|---|---|---|
   | 4.1 | 1/10 | $4.2\times10^{-5}$ | $2.1\times10^{-4}$ | 321 | 54 / 264 |
   | 4.1 | 1/20 | $6.6\times10^{-6}$ | $3.4\times10^{-5}$ | 343 | 64 / 314 |
   | 4.2 | 1/20 | $8.9\times10^{-4}$ | $9.9\times10^{-3}$ | 490 | 179 / 886 |
   | 4.2 | 1/40 | $1.4\times10^{-4}$ | $2.3\times10^{-3}$ | 750 | 170 / 822 |

   The multistage scheme "does save the computation cost significantly",
   because the nonlinear coefficients are computed once per cycle: far
   fewer cycles than forward-Euler steps, at a similar stage count.
2. **Osher's singular perturbation problem** (Example 2, eqs. 4.3–4.4,
   Tables 3–4). $\varepsilon y'' - (\tfrac12 y^2)' - y = 0$ on $[0, 1]$,
   $y(0) = A$, $y(1) = B$, with the perturbation parameter $10^{-6}$
   (Shu's $\varepsilon$, not the WENO $\varepsilon$); four cases with
   exact solutions, the last with a shock at $x = 0.625$. Osher's
   first-order monotone scheme is replaced by the third-order flux. Table 3
   ($h = 1/20$): $L_1$ errors first/third order $0/5.5\times10^{-5}$,
   $7.0\times10^{-3}/8.1\times10^{-4}$, $1.6\times10^{-2}/3.3\times10^{-3}$,
   $1.3\times10^{-2}/3.6\times10^{-3}$; 135 iterations for every
   first-order run, 485, 193, 242 and 450 for third order with forward
   Euler. $h = 1/40$ "verifies third-order accuracy … away from boundary
   layers and discontinuities" (no table). Table 4 (shock case, cycles to
   steady state): $s = 1$: more than 2000; 2: 182; 3: 135; 4: 112; 5: 99;
   6: 90. Forward Euler with the third-order flux does not converge within
   2000 steps there; the multistage scheme with $s \ge 2$ does.
3. No time-dependent test of the §3 multistep schemes; results are
   referred to Shu's TVB paper (p. 1079).

# Summary

Roe argues that the exact Riemann solution used by Godunov- and
Glimm-type schemes is mostly wasted by the differencing, so only some of
its features are worth computing (pp. 358–359). He replaces each Riemann
problem by the exact solution of a *linear* problem with a constant matrix
of the two data states, and accepts only matrices with four properties he
calls **Property U** ("uniform validity across discontinuities"). The
decisive one is the exact jump relation, which gives conservation and
resolves an isolated shock exactly (pp. 359–360). For the Euler equations
he builds the matrix from a **parameter vector**
$\sqrt\rho\,(1, v_x, v_y, v_z, (E+p)/\rho)^{\mathsf T}$: every component of
the state and of the three fluxes is quadratic in it, so jumps factor
exactly through its arithmetic means. The eigenstructure is that of the
Jacobian at the $\sqrt\rho$-weighted averages of velocity and total
specific enthalpy (eqs. 11–22). The eigensystems for steady supersonic
marching (eqs. 23–28) and for pseudo-unsteady flow (eqs. 29–33) are also
given. On Sod's shock tube the first-order scheme with the approximate
solver is practically indistinguishable from Godunov's with the exact
solver, at about half the CPU time (Table I). Roe states that the
linearization admits entropy-violating expansion shocks at sonic points
and offers only an empirical cure (pp. 370–371). Merged into
[approximate Riemann solvers](/methods/approximate-riemann-solvers.md)
(the user decided there is no separate Roe page) and the
[Sod shock tube](/benchmarks/sod-shock-tube.md).

The paper has no numbered sections; its unnumbered headings are the
introduction (pp. 357–360), "Construction of Ã" (pp. 360–362), "The
Parameter Vector" (pp. 362–363), "Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues for the
Euler Equations" (pp. 364–367), "A Numerical Experiment" (pp. 367–370), "A
Note on Entropy Conditions" (pp. 370–371) and "Concluding Remarks"
(p. 371). Claims below are cited by page and equation.

# Key claims

1. **Linearized Riemann problem** (p. 359). Approximate solutions are
   "exact solutions to an approximate problem": a linear hyperbolic system
   with a constant matrix (the Roe matrix) and the data unaltered. Roe
   rejects the Jacobian average of the two states and the Jacobian at the
   arithmetic-mean state because "in general, neither … would satisfy
   condition (iii)".
2. **Property U: four conditions** (p. 359). The Roe matrix of the states
   $u^-$, $u^+$ must
   * (i) be a linear mapping from the vector space of states to that of
     fluxes;
   * (ii) tend to $A(u)$ as $u^-$ and $u^+$ tend to $u$;
   * (iii) map $u^- - u^+$ exactly to $f(u^-) - f(u^+)$, for any $u^-$,
     $u^+$;
   * (iv) have linearly independent eigenvectors.

   The name is explained on p. 360: "christened Property U (since it is
   intended to ensure uniform validity across discontinuities)". Real
   eigenvalues are not required of the Roe matrix; hyperbolicity is
   assumed for $A$ itself (p. 357).
3. **What each condition buys** (pp. 359–360, citing his ref. [1]).
   Condition (iii) suffices for conservation. Conditions (iii) and (iv) are
   "necessary and sufficient conditions for the algorithm to 'recognize' a
   shockwave": if the flux jump is a shock speed times the state jump
   (eq. 5), that speed is an eigenvalue of the Roe matrix, the jump projects
   only onto its eigenvector, and "the solution of the Riemann problem will
   be exact". Condition (ii) is needed to "recover smoothly the linearized
   algorithm". Heuristically, on $O(N^2)$ points, $O(N)$ lie near a single
   discontinuity, where Property U recognizes it, and $O(1)$ near
   interacting discontinuities, where one relies on conservation.
4. **Existence and non-uniqueness** (pp. 361–362). Integrating the Jacobian
   along the straight path from $u^-$ to $u^+$ gives a valid matrix (by the
   mean-value theorem) that may lack a closed form; Osher & Solomon take a
   "similar" approach with a subtler path. For rational fluxes, a rational
   matrix follows from the identities
   $[\![ab]\!] = \{\!\{a\}\!\}[\![b]\!] + \{\!\{b\}\!\}[\![a]\!]$ and
   $[\![1/a]\!] = -[\![a]\!]/(a^-a^+)$ (eqs. 6b–c), but this construction is
   "far from unique" (a triple product depends on the order of
   multiplication) and cumbersome. Matrices built that way for the unsteady
   Euler equations are in Sells (his ref. [11]), not in this paper.
5. **Parameter vector** (pp. 362–363, eqs. 8–16). For the 3D Euler
   equations of a perfect gas, "every component of u, F, G, H is merely
   quadratic in the components of" the parameter vector
   $\sqrt\rho\,(1, v_x, v_y, v_z, (E+p)/\rho)^{\mathsf T}$ (eq. 11), e.g.
   eqs. 12a–b for the energy and the $x$-momentum flux. Hence the state jump
   and the flux jump are each a $5\times5$ matrix, linear in the arithmetic
   means of the parameter vector, times its jump (eqs. 13–16), and the Roe
   matrix is the product of the second with the inverse of the first
   (eq. 17). Roe attributes the simplicity to homogeneity, but adds that
   homogeneity "is not essential for the existence of a parameter vector"
   and leaves the shallow-water equations as an exercise (p. 363). A
   parameter vector "has so far worked each time that it has been tried"
   (p. 362), a statement of experience, not a theorem.
6. **The Roe averages** (p. 364, eqs. 18–19). The eigenvalue problem
   reduces after dividing by the mean of $\sqrt\rho$. The velocity
   "means"
   $$v_{x,\mathrm{Roe}} = \frac{\sqrt{\rho^-}\,v_x^- + \sqrt{\rho^+}\,v_x^+}{\sqrt{\rho^-} + \sqrt{\rho^+}},$$
   likewise $v_y$, $v_z$ and the total specific enthalpy $(E+p)/\rho$, and
   $c_{\mathrm{Roe}}^2 = (\gamma-1)\left[\left((E+p)/\rho\right)_{\mathrm{Roe}} - \tfrac12\vert\mathbf v_{\mathrm{Roe}}\vert^2\right]$
   (p. 365). **No averaged density is printed**: the geometric mean
   $\sqrt{\rho^-\rho^+}$ appears nowhere in the paper, and the
   eigenstructure needs only the averaged velocity, enthalpy and sound
   speed.
7. **Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, wave strengths** (pp. 364–365, eqs. 20–22).
   $\lambda^{(1)} = v_{x,\mathrm{Roe}} - c_{\mathrm{Roe}}$,
   $\lambda^{(2)} = \lambda^{(3)} = \lambda^{(4)} = v_{x,\mathrm{Roe}}$,
   $\lambda^{(5)} = v_{x,\mathrm{Roe}} + c_{\mathrm{Roe}}$ (eq. 21). With
   $H$ the averaged total specific enthalpy and the averaged velocities
   written without subscript, the eigenvectors are
   $(1, v_x - c, v_y, v_z, H - v_x c)^{\mathsf T}$,
   $(0, 0, v_y, 0, v_y^2)^{\mathsf T}$, $(0, 0, 0, v_z, v_z^2)^{\mathsf T}$,
   $(1, v_x, v_y, v_z, \tfrac12\vert\mathbf v\vert^2)^{\mathsf T}$ and
   $(1, v_x + c, v_y, v_z, H + v_x c)^{\mathsf T}$ (eq. 20), and the wave
   strengths of the state jump follow in closed form (eqs. 22a–e). For
   computation the factors $v_y$, $v_z$ are to be extracted from the second
   and third eigenvectors so that their strengths are never indeterminate.
   The eigenvector determinant is $2c^3/(\gamma-1)$, "never small unless the
   Mach number is very large". The paper prints neither the central flux
   form nor wave strengths in primitive jumps.
8. **How the solver enters a scheme** (pp. 359, 368, 370). The intended use
   is the "mechanism" of his ref. [1] by which a linear-advection algorithm
   generalizes to nonlinear systems; unpublished results for Burgers,
   shallow water and steady and unsteady Euler reportedly show that "all
   qualitative features of each algorithm are faithfully transmitted" and
   that "accuracy also carries over, at least to third order" (p. 359). The
   first-order scheme splits the flux jump
   $f(u^+) - f(u^-) = \sum_p\lambda^{(p)}\,\ell^{(p)}(u^+ - u^-)\,r^{(p)}$
   into a left-moving part ($\lambda^{(p)} < 0$) and a right-moving part
   ($\lambda^{(p)} > 0$) (p. 370), a flux-difference splitting.
9. **Entropy** (pp. 359, 370–371). Compared with Harten–Lax, the
   approximation "suffers the theoretical disadvantage of not having a
   naturally constructed entropy condition" (p. 359). The splitting fails
   "if one of the waves … should in fact be a fan which spans x = 0". In the
   extreme case of equal fluxes from unequal states, where an expansion
   should occur, both parts vanish and the method gives "no motive for
   breaking up the unrealistic discontinuity". The remedy is empirical:
   "For each interval which contains a sonic point one creates artificially
   an equal and opposite contribution to" the two parts; "Work is in hand to
   provide a fuller theoretical justification" (p. 371). No formula and no
   parameter are given. Transonic aerofoil runs needed no special treatment
   near the sonic line, but users "would be advised to employ either an
   empirical device … or some form of artificial viscosity".
10. **Multi-D** (p. 360). With operator splitting the method applies to each
    1D operator. This works while shocks are grid-aligned; oblique shocks
    cause problems "particularly severe as the solution attempts to reach a
    steady state", because "neither of the split operators, by itself,
    recognizes the oblique shock as being in equilibrium". This concerns
    steady-state convergence, not the carbuncle.
11. **Steady supersonic marching** (pp. 365–366, eqs. 23–28). Marching in
    $x$ with the $y$-flux jump in place of the $x$-flux jump gives
    eigenvalues from
    $(\lambda v_x - v_y)^3\left[(\lambda v_x - v_y)^2 - c^2(1 + \lambda^2)\right] = 0$
    (eq. 23); the two acoustic roots involve
    $\sqrt{(v_x^2 + v_y^2)/c^2 - 1}$ (eqs. 25–26). The eigenvector
    determinant is troublesome only at very large Mach number or with the
    $x$-Mach number near 1. For constant total enthalpy the second strength
    vanishes.
12. **Pseudo-unsteady flow** (p. 367, eqs. 29–33). Constant total enthalpy
    gives eigenvalues that are not the real-flow ones (eq. 31); assuming
    $\partial_x p = c^2\partial_x\rho$ instead gives
    $\lambda^2 - 2v_x\lambda + v_x^2 - c^2 = 0$ (eq. 33), "just as in the
    real-time flow", implemented by deleting the fifth eigenvector component
    and the fourth eigenvector.

# Tests

One numerical experiment (pp. 367–370).

* **Riemann-solution comparison** (Fig. 1, p. 368). Sod's data (density
  ratio 1:8, pressure ratio 1:10): exact and approximate density against
  $x/t \in [-2, 2]$. "The comparison is not very close", but the areas
  under the two curves are identical, "and can readily be proved so".
* **First-order shock tube** (Fig. 2, Table I, pp. 368–369). Godunov's
  scheme with the exact solver against first-order upwind passed through
  the mechanism of his ref. [1] with the approximate solver; both 35 steps
  at $\Delta t/h = 0.411$ ("Sod's standard comparison"), on 99 grid
  intervals (inferred from the timing arithmetic, p. 369; domain length and
  end time not printed). Differences are "insignificant everywhere",
  largest near the shock. Table I, density at indices 72–80, Godunov /
  approximate: 0.2658 / 0.2655, 0.2654 / 0.2652, 0.2631 / 0.2629,
  0.2460 / 0.2458, 0.1878 / 0.1881, 0.1368 / 0.1370, then identical
  (0.1260, 0.1251, 0.1250); the largest difference is 0.0003.
* **Cost** (DEC KL-10, optimized FORTRAN, pp. 369–370). Exact solver
  (Sod's algorithm, improved): 1.6 iterations on average at 0.20 ms each,
  $0.20\times1.6\times99\times35\times10^{-3} = 1.11$ s of Riemann solving,
  2.02 s in all, of which 0.91 s is the Godunov differencing. Approximate
  solver: 0.27 ms per problem, 0.94 s, 1.09 s in all; the wave increments
  are "immediately available in usable form". Higher order (from his ref.
  [1]): a typical second-order algorithm 1.20 s, with monotonicity logic
  1.78 s, third-order monotone 1.90 s. Van Leer's faster exact solver was
  not available; Roe judges it would change little at 1.6 iterations.
* *Compiler reading* (not printed): 35 steps at $\Delta t/h = 0.411$ reach
  $t = 0.144$ for $h = 0.01$, or about 0.145 for $h = 1/99$.

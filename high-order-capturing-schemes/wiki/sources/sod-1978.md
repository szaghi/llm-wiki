# Summary

A survey, labelled "Review" on page 1, of the finite-difference methods
for the 1D Euler equations available in 1977. Plain schemes: Godunov,
two-step Lax–Wendroff, MacCormack, Rusanov and an upwind scheme
(Table I), with Lapidus artificial viscosity (eq. 20). Corrective
procedures: Harten's artificial compression method (ACM, eqs. 21–27),
the Harten–Zwas self-adjusting hybrid (eqs. 28–38), Boris–Book
antidiffusion, i.e. flux-corrected transport (eqs. 39–44), and Hyman's
predictor–corrector (eq. 45). Glimm's random-choice method with Chorin's
modification is treated in full (eqs. 9–19), with the exact Riemann
solution in an appendix (eqs. 46–62) and a flow chart for programming it.

The paper defines one test, the shock tube now called
[Sod's problem](/benchmarks/sod-shock-tube.md) (§3), and runs every
method on it with $h = 0.01$. Methods are compared by contact and shock
width in zones, by overshoots and rounding at the rarefaction corners, and
by CDC 6600 run time (Table IV). Glimm's method gives zero-width shocks
and contacts and sharp rarefaction corners, but random discontinuity
positions and a ragged rarefaction. Among the plain schemes, Godunov and
Hyman are best (§4).

# Key claims

1. **Contact smearing rate** (§1, p. 2, citing Harten's 1974 report).
   Contacts spread much more than shocks: the width grows like the number
   of time steps to the power $1/(k+1)$ for a scheme of order $k$.
   Artificial viscosity does not spread a contact, truncation error does;
   shocks are smeared by both.
2. **High order abandoned** (§1, p. 3). Fourth- and sixth-order methods
   were tried, but "the appropriate high-order artificial viscosity" made
   the time step "prohibitively small", and with Lapidus's viscosity "all
   benefits obtained by using a high-order method are lost". The
   Harten–Zwas hybrid is the only "high"-order method kept.
3. **Schemes and stability** (Table I, p. 4). Sod's Courant number is
   $\max(\vert v\vert + c)\,\Delta t/h$, i.e. the registry $\nu$. Godunov
   (order 1), two-step Lax–Wendroff (2), MacCormack (2) and the upwind
   scheme (1) need $\nu \le 1$. Rusanov (order 1) needs $\nu \le 1$ and a
   viscosity parameter between $\nu$ and $1/\nu$. The upwind scheme uses
   centred pressure differences.
4. **Lapidus artificial viscosity** (eq. 20, p. 7) is a conservative
   fractional step consistent with a nonlinear diffusion of the form
   $h^2(\vert u_x\vert u_x)_x$, applied only near discontinuities and never
   to the mass equation, to limit mass diffusion across the contact.
5. **Exact Riemann solver** (eqs. 12–19, p. 6). Godunov's iteration as
   modified by Chorin: the star pressure and velocity from the two
   Lagrangian wave speeds, iterated with an under-relaxation that is
   halved on non-convergence (flow chart, p. 28). The appendix gives the
   sampling formulas for every region of the fan.
6. **Glimm/Chorin random choice** (eqs. 9–10, p. 5). On a staggered grid
   with $\Delta t \le h/(2\max(\vert v\vert + c))$, so that the Riemann
   problems do not interact, one equidistributed sample in
   $[-\tfrac12, \tfrac12]$ is drawn per time step (Chorin), not per cell.
   Glimm's original per-cell choice is "disastrous" with finite $h$
   because it creates spurious states (§3, p. 14). The method is at best
   first order and conservative only "on the average" (§§3–4).
7. **Artificial compression** (eqs. 21–27, pp. 8–9) adds a compression
   flux that is nonzero only inside a jump, which turns a contact into a
   shock of a modified equation; it is applied by operator splitting after
   a first-order step. ACM "cannot be applied directly to higher-order
   schemes", because it relies on a viscous profile (p. 9), nor in the
   presence of oscillations (p. 16).
8. **Harten–Zwas hybrid** (eqs. 30–37, pp. 9–10) blends a first-order and
   a higher-order flux built on MacCormack. The first-order share is a
   normalized difference of adjacent density jumps (power 1), set to 0
   below a tolerance, and taken as the larger of the two cell values at an
   interface; it tends to 1 at discontinuities. The stability limit is
   $\nu \le \sqrt3/2$, stricter than MacCormack's. The same switch gates
   ACM (eq. 38).
9. **Boris–Book antidiffusion** (eqs. 39–44, pp. 11–12): two-step
   Lax–Wendroff, then diffusion, then antidiffusion limited by the FCT
   minmod-type flux limiter (eq. 44), with $\nu \le 1$. This is the
   flux-corrected transport that the
   [positivity-preserving flux limiter](/methods/positivity-preserving-flux-limiter.md)
   names as its root.
10. **Hyman predictor–corrector** (eq. 45, p. 12): improved Euler in time
    with a Rusanov-type viscosity, reduced by a factor 1/3 where the local
    wave speed jumps upward by more than $h/3$ ("a type of artificial
    compression", p. 13).
11. **Conclusions** (§4, pp. 24–25).
    * Without corrective procedures, Godunov and Hyman are best; Glimm
      resolves shocks and contacts best.
    * ACM does "far better" than Boris–Book antidiffusion; antidiffusion
      also needs slightly more storage.
    * Antidiffusion, the hybrid and ACM all carry problem-dependent
      parameters.
    * The shock tube is a "sitting duck" for Riemann-solver-based methods.
      The "true value" of a method lies in multidimensional problems,
      where Glimm-type methods "may not have any advantage" (p. 25).

# Tests

**The shock tube** (§3, pp. 13–24, Figs. 2–15, Tables II–IV), the only
test. Set-up as printed (p. 14): $(\rho, v, p) = (1, 0, 1)$ on the left
(region 1) and $(0.125, 0, 0.1)$ on the right (region 5), gas at rest
on both sides of a diaphragm, $\gamma = 1.4$; $h = 0.01$ in all runs
(Table IV: "100 spatial grid points"); $\nu = 0.9$. Scheme parameters:
Rusanov 1.0, Boris–Book diffusion 0.125, Hyman 0.8, Lapidus 1.0 (2.0
for Godunov + ACM, so that the solution entering ACM is free of
oscillations). The run stops "before any wave has reached the left or
right boundary" (p. 13).

**Not printed:** the diaphragm position, the end time and the boundary
conditions. The figures span $x \in [0, 1]$.

**Results** (contact and shock width in zones, text of pp. 16–24):

| Scheme | Fig. | Contact | Shock | Remarks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Godunov | 4 | 7–8 | 5–6 | rarefaction corners rounded |
| Godunov + ACM | 5 | 3–4 | 1–2 | slight undershoot at the right corner of the rarefaction; oscillations at the contact |
| Two-step Lax–Wendroff | 6 | 6–8 | 4–6 | slight overshoots, more at the shock |
| MacCormack | 7 | 7–8 | 5–6 | as Lax–Wendroff |
| Rusanov | 8 | 14–16 | 6–8 | "extremely diffusive" |
| Rusanov + ACM | 9 | 2–3 | 1–2 | greatly improved |
| Upwind | 10 | — | — | nonphysical shock at the left end of the rarefaction (centred pressure differences) |
| Glimm | 11 | 0 | 0 | positions random, exact on average |
| Boris–Book antidiffusion | 12 | 5–7 | 1–2 | slight overshoot at the right corner of the rarefaction |
| Harten–Zwas hybrid | 13 | 8–9 | 5–6 | oscillation-free |
| Hybrid + ACM | 14 | 3–4 | 2–3 | — |
| Hyman | 15 | 6–8 | 3–4 | oscillation-free, well-defined plateaus |

Table II gives Glimm profiles on nine interior points ($h = 0.1$): the
plateau $\rho = 0.426$, $v = 0.927$, $p = 0.303$ and the post-shock state
$\rho = 0.266$, $v = 0.927$, $p = 0.303$. Table III gives Glimm totals
over 20 steps: mass 0.543–0.554 and energy 2.213–2.275, "conserved on
the average". Table IV gives CDC 6600 seconds per step, including the
exact solution, printing and plotting: Godunov 0.226 (0.247 with ACM),
Lax–Wendroff 0.226, MacCormack 0.224, Rusanov 0.224 (0.240), upwind
0.225, Glimm 0.364, antidiffusion 0.242, hybrid 0.258 (0.269), Hyman
0.276.

# Summary

The paper models the grasping phase of a free-floating space robot
capturing a fast-tumbling, non-cooperative object as a continuous
contact process rather than an instantaneous event. The normal contact
force is compliant: a stiffness term plus Hunt–Crossley-type nonlinear
damping, with the stiffness built from the Young's moduli, the Poisson
ratios and an effective contact radius. Friction uses the 3-D bristle
model of Liang et al. [21], which captures stick, slip and stick-slip.
The controller, "resolved motion admittance control" (RMAC), revises the
desired end-effector trajectory by an admittance law once contact is
detected and tracks it with a computed-torque controller through the
generalised Jacobian. One MATLAB/Simulink/SimMechanics simulation of a
7-joint manipulator on a 500 kg base capturing a 50 kg object tumbling
at about 8 deg/s compares RMAC with resolved motion rate control
(RMRC): capture completes at 3.8 s under RMAC, while under RMRC the
handle bounces off the gripper. The paper has no plasma or propulsion
content.

# Key claims

Symbols are the paper's own (see the notation map).

1. **Contact models** (§1): impulse-momentum models [6,7] do not suit
   robotic capture, which involves many simultaneous contacts and
   continuous sliding and jamming; local-deformation (compliance) models
   are more suitable. Stated, with citations.
2. **Friction** (§1, §3.2): the Coulomb law is discontinuous between
   stick and slip [9]; the continuous bristle model (Haessig & Friedland
   [20], extended to 3-D by Liang et al. [21]) is adopted. Stated.
3. **Robot dynamics** (§2.2, Eqs. 1–12): free-floating dynamics in joint
   space with the base eliminated through momentum conservation,
   $\mathbf H^*\ddot{\boldsymbol\theta}+\mathbf c^*=\boldsymbol\tau+\mathbf J^{*T}\mathbf w_e$
   (Eq. 10), with generalised inertia
   $\mathbf H^*=\mathbf H_m-\mathbf H_{bm}^T\mathbf H_b^{-1}\mathbf H_{bm}$
   (Eq. 11) and generalised Jacobian
   $\mathbf J^*=\mathbf J_m-\mathbf J_b\mathbf H_b^{-1}\mathbf H_{bm}$
   (Eq. 7); unlike the zero-momentum case, the momentum vector (Eq. 6)
   carries the time integrals of the contact force and torque. A
   standard construction, derived.
4. **Normal force** (§3.1, Eqs. 18–22):
   $\mathbf f_n=\lambda\delta\dot\delta\,\mathbf n+k\delta\,\mathbf n$, with
   $\lambda=\tfrac32\alpha k$ (after Hunt–Crossley [10]) and
   $k(\delta)=c\,\frac{E_1E_2}{E_1(1-\upsilon_2^2)+E_2(1-\upsilon_1^2)}\,a(\delta)$,
   where $\tfrac43\le c\le2\pi$ and $a(\delta)=\sqrt{A/\pi}$; bodies may
   be pre-cut into sub-bodies to keep accuracy over large contact areas.
   A model, stated.
5. **Bristle friction** (§3.2, Eqs. 23–26):
   $\mathbf f_f=-k_b\mathbf s-c_b\dot{\mathbf s}$; the bristle deflection
   integrates the tangential velocity while $|\mathbf s|<s_{\max}$ and is
   clamped otherwise; $s_{\max}=\mu_k|\mathbf f_n|/k_b$ above the
   velocity threshold $v_d$ and $\mu_s|\mathbf f_n|/k_b$ below it. The
   model of [21].
6. **RMAC** (§4, Eqs. 27–33): a weighted least-norm resolved-motion
   inverse of Eq. 7 (Eq. 27); a computed-torque law with PD feedback on
   the error to the admittance-revised trajectory (Eqs. 29–30); an
   admittance law
   $\mathbf M\Delta\ddot{\mathbf x}+\mathbf B\Delta\dot{\mathbf x}+\mathbf K\Delta\mathbf x=\mathbf w_e$
   (Eqs. 31–32), active only when contact is detected. A design.
7. **RMAC against RMRC** (§5.2, Figs. 8–11): much smaller contact forces
   and torques under RMAC, small deviation of base and target after
   contact, capture complete at 3.8 s; under RMRC the handle "is bounced
   off the gripper after a large collision" and capture fails. Shown in
   one simulation only; no force values are given in the text and there
   is no experiment.
8. **Bandwidth** (§5.2, Fig. 7): the control bandwidth is about 60 Hz;
   under 10 N sinusoidal excitations the gripper follows from 25 to
   60 Hz and vibrates weakly or not at all above. Shown in simulation.

# Methods

* Assumptions (§2.1): rigid links and target; known motion states and
  mass properties; no external force besides the mutual contact forces,
  so total momentum is conserved.
* Models: free-floating base plus an $n$-link serial manipulator with
  single-DOF joints (§2.2, Eqs. 1–12); the target as a free rigid body
  with z-y-x Euler angles and a wrench transfer from the contact point
  to its centre of mass (§2.3, Eqs. 13–17); contact as above (§3). No
  explicit contact-detection or interference-geometry algorithm is
  given; the contact-dynamics code is written in C and embedded as an
  S-function (§5.1, Fig. 6).
* Controller (§4, Fig. 3): admittance controller, contact-detection
  module and computed-torque controller with resolved-motion-rate
  mapping to joint space.
* Simulation (§5.1): MATLAB/Simulink with SimMechanics; integrator and
  time step not stated. Vision noise of 1 mm and 0.1° standard
  deviation sampled at 20 Hz; force-sensor noise of 0.1% of the maximum
  sampled at 10000 Hz. Contact parameters "assumed to the best of our
  knowledge" from [9,32,33], friction parameters from [21], bristle
  stiffness suggested at ten times the largest dominant stiffness of the
  system [21].

# Tests and results

* One example (§5, Fig. 4): a 7-joint manipulator with a gripper on a
  free-floating servicing satellite capturing a target by a grasping
  handle. A second case (a probe inserted into the target's nozzle cone)
  is described in §4 but not simulated.
* Masses (Table 1): base 500 kg (0.50 m, principal inertias
  20.83 kg·m²); links 7.50–14.3 kg; gripper 22.2 kg; target 50.0 kg
  (0.40 m; inertias 1.03, 0.528, 0.583 kg·m²).
* Contact parameters (Table 2): $E_1=E_2=2.06\times10^{11}$ N/m²,
  Poisson ratios 0.3, $\alpha=0.09$ s/m, $\mu_k=0.25$, $\mu_s=0.3$,
  $k_b=5\times10^4$ N/m, $c_b=80$ (unit printed "Nm/s"),
  $v_d=10^{-3}$ m/s. Admittance gains in Table 3.
* Initial target angular velocity (§5.2): $(-0.630, 3.712, 6.989)$ deg/s,
  magnitude 7.94 deg/s (the "about 8 deg/s" of §6).
* Capture is "guaranteed" when three quarters of the handle are inside
  the open gripper (Fig. 5).
* Results (§5.2): first contact at 0.53 s and capture complete at 3.8 s
  under RMAC; relative position and attitude go to zero (Fig. 8);
  contact forces and torques much smaller under RMAC (Fig. 9, plots
  only); base and target hardly deviate under RMAC and do under RMRC
  (Figs. 10–11); joint trajectories in Fig. 12.

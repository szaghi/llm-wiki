# Summary

A policy history of the Philippine national space programme: its
origins (astronomy from 1897, UNCOPUOS membership in 1977, the Agila-2
satellite in the 1990s), the failed 2012 Philippine Space Act bill, the
acceleration after Super-Typhoon Haiyan (2013), the PHL-Microsat
programme (2014–2018; the Diwata-1 and Diwata-2 microsatellites built
with Tohoku and Hokkaido Universities), the National SPACE Development
Program NSDP (2015–2016), and the 2017 consolidated bills HB6413 and
SB1572 that would create the Philippine Space Agency (PhilSA). It
describes the proposed space development and utilisation policy (six
Key Development Areas), the four NSDP roadmap documents, the country's
position on five UN space-law treaties (Table 1) and the DOST grant
mechanisms for a domestic space industry. The evidence is mainly
stakeholder interviews, official bills and strategy documents, and
conference presentations. The paper has no plasma, propulsion,
numerical or engineering content; it closes (§8) with four lessons for
developing countries entering space.

# Key claims

1. **Failure of the 2012 bill** (§3): the Philippine Space Act bill
   (House Bill 6725, ref. [7]) failed "probably" through lack of
   resonance with public needs (a space-science and telecommunication
   focus) and its proponent's electoral troubles. The authors'
   interpretation.
2. **Haiyan as turning point** (§3): Super-Typhoon Haiyan/Yolanda
   (November 2013; 6300 victims, over 16 million people affected,
   damages above US\$800 million, ref. [8]) refocused the programme on
   disaster reduction and national security. Stated, with sources.
3. **Diwata-1** (§4.1, refs. [13,14,16]): the first satellite built and
   developed by Filipino engineers, a 50 kg microsatellite launched
   23 March 2016 and released from the ISS (Kibo) on 27 April 2016 into a
   circular orbit of 400 km altitude and 51° inclination; instruments: a
   high-precision telescope (3 m resolution at 400 km), a multispectral
   imager with a liquid-crystal tunable filter (80 m) and a wide-field
   camera (7 km). Diwata-2 launch set for 2018.
4. **NSDP** (§4.2): a 12-month project created in September 2015 and
   completed on 30 November 2016; its outputs are confidential but are
   reflected in the bills.
5. **PhilSA bills** (§5.3, §6.2): six bills with identical text (four
   House, two Senate), consolidated in 2017 into HB6413 (14 sections)
   and SB1572 (25 sections numbered 1–26, without a section 9). SB1572
   places PhilSA under DOST rather than the Office of the President.
6. **Budget** (§6.2.1): the original bills give PhilSA an initial
   1 billion pesos (about US\$19.6 million) and a Philippine Space
   Development Fund of 10 billion pesos (about US\$196.4 million), of
   which 2 billion pesos (about US\$39.3 million) a year for five years,
   at 1 peso = US\$0.019637 (August 2017, footnote 2).
7. **Policy** (§6.3): six Key Development Areas (national security and
   development; hazard management and climate studies; space R&D; space
   industry capacity building; space education and awareness;
   international cooperation). The DOST Secretary's goal is "at least 800
   trained aerospace scientists and engineers within the next 10 years"
   [37].
8. **NSDP roadmaps** (§6.4): Space R&D Agenda (five research clusters);
   Satellite Development Roadmap (Fig. 2: Diwata-1/-2, LEO and GEO
   telecommunication, optical-infrared, SAR and microwave satellites over
   2016–2030); Satellite Data Sharing Policy (open access,
   standardisation, commercialisation); Space Industry Development
   Roadmap ("at least 800 high-level workers" and "3200 support workers"
   within 10 years; four niche areas). The priority-application study
   behind Fig. 1 is called "still a work in progress" (§6.4.2).
9. **Treaties** (§6.5, Table 1): Outer Space Treaty (1967) signed,
   priority high, ratification planned; Rescue Agreement (1968) signed,
   low, none; Liability Convention (1972) signed, high, ratification;
   Registration Convention (1975) unsigned, very low, none; Moon Treaty
   (1979) ratified (one of 18 states, against 105 for the Outer Space
   Treaty).
10. **Industry** (§7.1–7.2): the industry roadmap's niches (subsystem
    production building on semiconductors; assembly, integration and
    test; space applications and services; launch-vehicle services) and
    the DOST grants TECHNICOM, NICER, CRADLE 1/2, BIST, i2Tech and
    iScale.
11. **Lessons** (§8): reliance on international partners (Japan); a
    goal-driven start with small satellites; a communication strategy
    attuned to public opinion; planned reliance on the private sector.
    The authors judge that the proposed policy "perfectly fits" a
    developing country's needs (opinion).

# Methods

Qualitative historical and policy analysis. Sources (§2, in decreasing
order of importance): interviews and private correspondence with DOST,
NSDP and University of the Philippines stakeholders (meetings at
APRSAF-24; a field trip to Manila on 9–12 January 2018); official
documents (roadmaps, strategy documents, bills); presentations at
international events; newspaper articles quoting stakeholders. No
model, equation, code or experiment.

# Tests and results

None: no test, simulation or experiment. The content is descriptive:
Fig. 1 (prospective priority applications by satellite type, from
ref. [4]), Fig. 2 (proposed technological roadmap 2016–2030, from
ref. [38]) and Table 1 (treaty positions, claim 9).

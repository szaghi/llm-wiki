# Summary

The paper formulates a two-impulse Earth-to-Earth transfer between
non-coplanar circular orbits in which an intermediate lunar flyby performs
most of the plane change at no propellant cost. Two models are used: an
extension of the authors' 3D lunar patched-conic approximation [20]
(geocentric ellipse, selenocentric hyperbola through the whole lunar
sphere of influence, second geocentric ellipse), closed by a two-unknown
two-point boundary-value problem (TPBVP) for a prescribed departure mutual
inclination $\alpha_E$; and the spatial circular restricted three-body
problem (SCR3BP), closed by a three-unknown TPBVP initialised from the
patched-conic solution. Both are solved by Newton–Raphson; the SCR3BP is
integrated with RKF45 in FORTRAN 90. Parametric sweeps and comparisons
with single-impulse, non-coplanar Hohmann and bi-parabolic transfers show
that the flyby transfer saves up to about 5 km/s against a single-impulse
plane change and up to about 3 km/s against Hohmann, and in some cases
beats the bi-parabolic transfer, at flight times of weeks to over 100
days. The paper has no plasma or propulsion-device content; the
impulses are idealised.

# Key claims

Symbols are the paper's own (see the notation map). All numbers are from
the paper's own computations.

1. **3D patched conic with a complete flyby** (§2.2): the selenocentric
   hyperbola of [20] is continued to the far edge of the sphere of
   influence with $f_s(t_3)=-f_s(t_1)$ (Eq. 25) and patched to a second
   geocentric ellipse (Eqs. 27–30).
2. **TPBVP** (§2.2.5, §2.3.2): patched conic, unknowns $(v_0,u_i)$ with
   constraints $g_1:r_{GP}-r_f=0$ and
   $g_2:[\cos\tilde\Omega_f-\cos\Omega_f]^2+[\sin\tilde\Omega_f-\sin\Omega_f]^2=0$;
   the SCR3BP adds the flight time $T$ and
   $g_3:\mathbf r_{GP}\cdot\mathbf v_{GP}=0$; $\alpha_E$ is prescribed.
3. **Departure inclination sweep** (§3, Fig. 4; $I_i=2^\circ$,
   $I_f=86^\circ$, $h_i=h_f=167$ km): the patched-conic TPBVP does not
   converge for $\alpha_E>4.4^\circ$; the variation of $\Delta v_{Total}$
   is dominated by $\Delta v_2$; flight times reach almost 150 days;
   minimum periselenium altitude close to 7000 km (no lunar collision).
4. **Against a single impulse** (Tables 2–3): the SCR3BP flyby saves
   4.248 km/s ($I_f=86^\circ$) and 5.137 km/s ($I_f=97^\circ$), of the
   order of the circular speed at 167 km, 7.804 km/s.
5. **Against non-coplanar Hohmann** (Tables 4–8): the SCR3BP flyby saves
   3.082, 2.364, 2.032, 1.709, 1.416 km/s for $h_f=3286.5$, 7911.5,
   12761.5, 22011.5, 30761.5 km.
6. **Against bi-parabolic** (§3, Fig. 8, Table 6): for $h_i=167$ km the
   patched-conic $\Delta v_{Total}$ falls below the bi-parabolic transfer
   for $h_f>9818$ km, maximum margin 103.392 m/s at $h_f=26861.5$ km; at
   $h_f=12761.5$ km the patched conic is 44 m/s and the SCR3BP 22 m/s
   below.
7. **Same-altitude transfers** (§3, Figs. 9–10, $\alpha_E=3^\circ$): the
   flyby beats a single impulse for $I_f>65^\circ$ (167 km) and
   $I_f>56^\circ$ (5000 km), and the bi-parabolic transfer from
   $I_f=87.5^\circ$ (167 km) and $70.5^\circ$ (5000 km); minimum
   $\Delta v_{Total}=6.450316$ km/s at $I_f\approx89^\circ$ (14.579 m/s
   below bi-parabolic, 167 km) and 4.877621 km/s at $I_f\approx73^\circ$
   (25.652 m/s below, 5000 km). A single impulse exceeds the bi-parabolic
   cost from $I_f=47^\circ$.
8. **Model agreement** (§3, Tables 9–10, Fig. 11): the patched conic
   agrees "well" with the SCR3BP and preserves the trajectory shape even
   for ~100-day flights. Stated; the agreement is not quantified by a
   tolerance (see Recorded as printed).
9. **Lower periselenium** (§3, Fig. 7): varying $\Omega_i$ to lower the
   periselenium raises cost and shortens the flight: 10322 km
   ($\Omega_i=0.5^\circ$) gives 4.572234 km/s and 26.123 d; 1991 km
   ($\Omega_i=44.5^\circ$) gives 7.076887 km/s and 8.226 d.
10. **Moon angular velocity** (§3 remark): using
    $\omega=\sqrt{(\mu_E+\mu_M)/D_M^3}$ instead of
    $\omega_M=\sqrt{\mu_E/D_M^3}$ in the patched conic changes
    $\Delta v_{Total}$ by $O(10^{-6})$ km/s. Stated.
11. **Attributed background** (§1): Ivashkin et al. [13] first proposed a
    lunar flyby for an Earth-to-Earth mission; AsiaSat-3/HGS-1 (1998)
    reached GEO by a lunar flyby after a launch failure [14]; a 60°
    inclination change costs one circular speed [12].

# Methods

* Patched conic (§2.2), hypotheses: Earth fixed; circular lunar orbit;
  inverse-square central fields; three phases delimited by the lunar
  sphere of influence; two impulses at the pericentres of the initial and
  final orbits, perpendicular to the position vector but not necessarily
  tangential.
  * Departure geometry by spherical trigonometry,
    $\cos I_g=\cos\alpha_E\cos I_i-\sin\alpha_E\sin I_i\cos u_i$ (Eq. 2),
    and Eqs. 3–6 with quadrant analysis.
  * Sphere-of-influence entry: $r_g(t_1)=\sqrt{D_M^2+R_S^2-2D_MR_S\cos\lambda_1}$
    (Eq. 7); inner Newton–Raphson on $\lambda_1$ enforcing
    $R_S-r_s(t_1)=0$ (Eq. 24); propagation by Lagrange coefficients
    (Eqs. 9–14).
  * Selenocentric flight time $\Delta t_s=(M_s(t_3)-M_s(t_1))\sqrt{-a_s^3/\mu_M}$
    (Eq. 26); arrival geometry Eqs. 35–45; impulses
    $\Delta\mathbf v_1=\mathbf v_0-\mathbf v_i$ (Eq. 47),
    $\Delta\mathbf v_2=\mathbf v_{GP}-\mathbf v_f$ (Eq. 50).
* SCR3BP (§2.3): barycentric inertial frame (Eqs. 53–55, not a rotating
  frame); Earth and Moon positions with $\mu=\mu_M/\mu_E$ and
  $\omega=\sqrt{(\mu_E+\mu_M)/D_M^3}$ (Eqs. 56–59); initial conditions
  Eqs. 60–67; arrival vectors Eqs. 68–71.
* Numerics (§3): Newton–Raphson with tolerance $10^{-7}$ in both models;
  RKF45 with step-size control; FORTRAN 90; canonical units DU = 6378.2 km,
  DT = 806.82 s, $\mu_E=1$ DU³/DT²; continuation in $I_f$ by 1° steps
  (Figs. 9–10).
* Constants (Table 1): $R_E=6378.2$ km, $R_M=1738$ km,
  $\mu_E=398600$ km³/s², $\mu_M=4902.83$ km³/s², $D_M=384400$ km,
  $R_S=66300$ km.

# Tests and results

All terminal orbits circular; $I_i=2^\circ$, $\Omega_i=0.5^\circ$,
$h_i=167$ km (the Hiten departure altitude [25]), $\Omega_f=212^\circ$
unless stated.

* Fig. 5 ($h_f$ from 167 to 26461.5 km, $\alpha_E=3^\circ$): $\Delta v_2$
  decreases with $h_f$; a minimum-time $h_f$ exists; higher $h_f$ requires
  a closer lunar approach.
* Fig. 6 ($I_f$ from 1° to 97°, $h_f=167$ km): $\Delta v_{Total}\approx13.7$
  km/s at $I_f=10^\circ$ against ≈6.5 km/s at $86^\circ$.
* Fig. 8 ($h_f$ up to 32861.5 km): the flyby beats Hohmann over the whole
  range (still by 1.424400 km/s at 32861.5 km); no solution above
  32861.5 km.
* Figs. 9–10 ($\alpha_E\in\{4^\circ,3^\circ,2^\circ,0^\circ\}$): no solution
  at $I_f=100^\circ$ with $\alpha_E=3^\circ$ at 5000 km, but solutions with
  $\alpha_E=0^\circ$.

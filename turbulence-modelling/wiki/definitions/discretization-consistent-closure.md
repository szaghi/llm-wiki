# Discretization-consistent closure

In an LES whose filter is induced by the grid and the discrete operator, the
equation actually solved is not the continuous filtered Navier–Stokes
equation. Its exact closure term is therefore not the subgrid stress
$\tau_{ij}$ alone. It also contains a commutation error and a term produced
by the discretization itself. A **discretization-consistent closure** is a
model designed, trained or optimized for that discrete equation, so it
accounts for all three terms (Beck & Kurz 2023, §I).

The sources reach it by three routes. Beck & Kurz (2023) and
[Kurz et al. (2023)](/sources/kurz-2023.md) optimize the closure in the
loop with the actual LES operator, by
[reinforcement learning](/methods/reinforcement-learning-closure.md);
[Sirignano & MacArt (2023)](/sources/sirignano-2023.md) do so with the
adjoint of the LES equations (see
[model-consistent training](/methods/model-consistent-training.md)).
Agdestein & Sanderse (2025) discretize first and filter next: training data
then satisfy the coarse discrete equations exactly (see
[divergence-consistent LES](/methods/divergence-consistent-les.md));
van Gastelen et al. (2024) do the same for 1D model equations and show
that a cell-averaging filter splits the discrete energy exactly.
[Lozano-Durán & Bae (2023)](/sources/lozanoduran-2023.md) generate the
training data of a wall model with the deployment solver itself.
[Bezgin et al. (2025)](/sources/bezgin-2025.md) take the in-the-loop
route with automatic differentiation through the solver, and the trained
object is the discretization itself, not an explicit closure (below).

## Decomposition of the closure term (Beck & Kurz 2023, §I)

Beck & Kurz work with a 1D model problem. Let $\bar u$ be the filtered
solution of the inviscid Burgers equation under a filter of width $\Delta$,
and let $\delta/\delta x$ denote the discrete derivative of the LES scheme
(page-local). Then (Eqs. 2–5)

$$\frac{\partial\bar u}{\partial t}+\frac12\frac{\delta(\bar u\,\bar u)}{\delta x}=C_1+C_2+C_3,$$

where:

- $C_1=\tfrac12\big(\partial_x\overline{u^2}-\overline{\partial_xu^2}\big)$
  is the **commutation error** between filter and derivative. It is
  non-zero when $\Delta$ varies in space or is anisotropic, and appears
  already for linear advection.
- $C_2=\tfrac12\big(\partial_x(\bar u\bar u)-\partial_x\overline{u^2}\big)$
  is the classical subfilter term, $-\tfrac12\partial_x(\overline{uu}-\bar u\bar u)$,
  the 1D analogue of the subgrid-stress divergence.
- $C_3=\tfrac12\big(\delta_x(\bar u\bar u)-\partial_x(\bar u\bar u)\big)$
  is the **discretization-induced term**. Following Geurts & van der Bos,
  it can be written as the derivative of a "computational subgrid stress"
  under a numerically induced filter $\mathcal F_h$ (page-local symbol;
  Beck & Kurz write it as a hat, which this bundle reserves for the test
  filter), assuming $\delta_xf=\partial_x\mathcal F_h(f)$ (Eq. 5).

Beck & Kurz state three properties of $C_3$ (the first two citing Geurts &
van der Bos, the third citing their refs. [11, 17]):

1. For consistent schemes on smooth, band-limited fields, it vanishes with
   the design order as $h\to0$, but not for grid-filtered turbulence.
2. For $\Delta\approx h$, the setting of implicitly filtered LES, the
   computational subgrid stresses "can be considerably larger" than $C_2$.
3. Modern high-order schemes are nonlinear in under-resolved settings
   (upwinding, non-oscillatory reconstruction, limiters). $C_3$ then
   depends on the current solution and cannot be determined a priori.

They conclude that "the closure model has to be optimized in conjunction
with the LES operator", and that a segregated optimization on
independently generated data "is not constructive". A note in their
reference list extends the argument to finite volumes: the cell average
(box filter) is exact, but the reconstruction of its integrand is a
nonlinear approximation, so $C_3$ appears there too.

## The implicit filter of DG (Kurz et al. 2023)

Kurz et al. (2023, §1, §3.3, Fig. 3) describe the filter that a DG
discretization induces. A priori it is the element-local $L_2$ projection
onto the polynomial basis; in the running scheme the elements are coupled
through the numerical fluxes, so the "hybrid DG operator" acts as a
complex filter "with typically non-linear kernel". Its form is unknown, so
the exact closure term of an implicitly filtered LES cannot be computed
even from the full DNS solution: doing so would require applying the
scheme's spatial and temporal operators. Supervised learning of the
closure is then, "from our perspective, ill-posed, since the training
targets are generally unknown" (§1). Citing their ref. [8], they state
that training on targets from an approximating explicit filter (a box
filter for finite volumes) causes "severe inconsistencies between the
training and actual simulations" and inaccurate or unstable LES. RL
avoids the problem because the policy is trained by interacting with the
LES, so the implicit filter is in the training loop by design.

## The discrete commutator error (Agdestein & Sanderse 2025, §3.2)

Agdestein & Sanderse make the same decomposition exact at the discrete
level. They define the DNS as a discrete system, apply a discrete
coarse-graining filter to it, and write the filtered DNS as the coarse LES
operator plus a remainder. That remainder, their **discrete commutator
error**, "lumps" the subfilter closure term together with the coarse-grid
discretization error (Eqs. 11–12). It is the exact target for a closure
used with that coarse operator. Training data generated this way are
consistent with the discrete LES by construction: the authors call this
model–data consistency (§1, Fig. 1).

Two consequences follow:

- **Discretization error in the closure target.** Under-resolved central
  differences produce grainy oscillations in the coarse right-hand side.
  These oscillations appear in the commutator error, and the closure must
  predict them. Filter-first LES would instead have to remove them by
  other means, such as explicit filtering (§5.1.2, Fig. 8).
- **Size of the term.** At $32^2$ and $128^3$ coarse points with face
  averaging, more than half of the filtered right-hand side is commutator
  error (fractions 0.56 and 0.51), although 92% and 91% of the kinetic
  energy is resolved. At $256^2$ the commutator error is still 7.7% of the
  right-hand side, with all of the energy resolved (§5.1.3, Table 2).

Their analysis of the 2D Taylor–Green vortex (App. F) separates the
continuous and discrete commutators. Under a top-hat filter, the continuous
convective commutator is $-\tfrac{\Delta^4}{480}\sin 2x+O(\Delta^6)$
(Eq. F.12). The discrete one has the same $\sin 2x$ dependence but a
different coefficient, which depends on the DNS spacing (Eqs. F.29–F.30).
A model fitted to one is therefore inconsistent with the other.

## Discrete energy and momentum under the filter (van Gastelen et al. 2024)

[van Gastelen et al. (2024)](/sources/vangastelen-2024.md) take the same
discretize-first route for 1D Burgers and Korteweg–de Vries equations and
add a structural result. Page-local notation: $\boldsymbol u\in\mathbb R^{N_x}$
is the fine-grid ("DNS") solution of $d\boldsymbol u/dt=f_h(\boldsymbol u)$,
the coarse grid has $I$ cells of $J$ fine cells each ($N_x=IJ$),
$\mathbf W$ is the discrete cell-averaging filter, $\bar{\boldsymbol u}=\mathbf W\boldsymbol u$,
$\mathbf R$ the piecewise-constant reconstruction (a right inverse,
$\mathbf W\mathbf R=\mathbf I$), $f_H$ the same scheme on the coarse grid,
and $\boldsymbol u^<=\boldsymbol u-\mathbf R\bar{\boldsymbol u}$ the subgrid
content (the paper's $\boldsymbol u'$), with $\mathbf W\boldsymbol u^<=0$ (§2.3, Eqs. 15–22). The exact
coarse equation is (§2.4, Eqs. 23–24)

$$\frac{d\bar{\boldsymbol u}}{dt}=f_H(\bar{\boldsymbol u})+\underbrace{\mathbf Wf_h(\boldsymbol u)-f_H(\bar{\boldsymbol u})}_{c(\boldsymbol u)},$$

and the closure term $c$ is "the discrete equivalent of the commutator
error" that "also includes the discretization error", as in Agdestein &
Sanderse.

With this filter–reconstruction pair, inner products are invariant under
reconstruction and $\mathbf R\bar{\boldsymbol u}$ is orthogonal to
$\boldsymbol u^<$ in the fine-grid mass-weighted inner product (App. A,
Eqs. A.1–A.3). The discrete kinetic energy therefore splits exactly into a
resolved and a subgrid part, and the discrete momentum is invariant under
filtering, so the exact closure term adds no momentum (§2.5–2.6, Eqs.
28–32; proofs in App. A). The authors chose the piecewise-constant
reconstruction on purpose: a more accurate one would shrink $\boldsymbol u^<$
but lose the energy invariance (§2.3). On this exact split they build an
[energy-conserving neural closure](/methods/neural-network-sgs-model.md)
that is stable for any network weights and still lets the resolved energy
rise (backscatter) while the total falls (see
[energy backscatter](/definitions/energy-backscatter.md)).

The price of the discrete route, in their words, is the dependence of the
trained closure on the compression factor $J$; they list training over
several $J$, finite-element formulations, graph convolutions and Fourier
neural operators as possible remedies for 2D and 3D (§5).

## Filters induced by compact differences and interpolation (Caban et al. 2026)

[Caban et al. (2026)](/sources/caban-2026.md) write the filters of a
linear compact-difference discretization explicitly, building on van der
Bos & Geurts, Salvetti & Beux and Boguslawski et al. as they cite them
(§3.2). Page-local: $a$ an advection speed, $\phi_k$ the Fourier
coefficient of a 1D periodic field at wavenumber $k$, $\mathcal F_h^{(r)}$
the filter induced by the discrete $r$-th derivative, defined by
$\delta^{(r)}_x\phi=\mathcal F_h^{(r)}*\partial^{(r)}_x\phi$ (Eq. 24), and
$\hat{\mathcal F}_h^{(r)}(k)$ its transfer function, the ratio of the
discrete to the exact derivative amplitude; for $r=1$ it is the modified
wavenumber over $k$ (Eqs. 25, 28–29). For a linear scheme on a periodic
grid the convolution commutes with the derivative,
$\mathcal F_h^{(1)}*\partial_x\phi=\partial_x(\mathcal F_h^{(1)}*\phi)$, so
$\mathcal F_h^{(1)}$ is the $\mathcal F_h$ of the computational subgrid
stress of Beck & Kurz (Eq. 5) for the convective term *(compiler check,
not in the sources)*.

**What each derivative filter does (§3.2.1, Eqs. 26–27).** The
semi-discrete advection–diffusion equation with central schemes reads

$$\frac{d\phi_k}{dt}+ia\,\hat{\mathcal F}_h^{(1)}(k)\,k\,\phi_k=-\nu\,\hat{\mathcal F}_h^{(2)}(k)\,k^2\phi_k,$$

so $\phi_k(t)=\phi_k(0)\exp\!\big(-\nu\hat{\mathcal F}_h^{(2)}k^2t\big)\exp\!\big(-ia\hat{\mathcal F}_h^{(1)}kt\big)$:
the first-derivative filter changes only the phase speed, and the
second-derivative filter lowers the viscosity of each mode to
$\nu\hat{\mathcal F}_h^{(2)}\le\nu$. In physical space both filters have
non-zero weights over the whole periodic stencil, the central one
dominant and the others decaying with alternating sign (Eq. 30, Fig. 3,
32 points), for the second-order scheme as for the sixth- and tenth-order
compact ones. The authors add, citing global spectral analysis, that once
time integration is included the effective viscosity can exceed $\nu$ in
some wavenumber ranges (§3.2.1).

**Interpolation filter (§3.2.2, Eqs. 31–35).** On their half-staggered
grid (code SAILOR), velocities are interpolated to half nodes and back at
each projection step. For second-order interpolation one step has
transfer function $\cos(kh/2)$, and the two steps together are the
trapezoidal filter $(\phi_{i-1}+2\phi_i+\phi_{i+1})/4$, of transfer
function $\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos kh=\cos^2(kh/2)$; the authors state that
the squaring holds for higher-order compact interpolation too. Their
model of the full numerical filter is the product of the first-derivative
filter and the squared interpolation filter, and they stress that it is
an approximation: no single filter represents the first- and
second-derivative filters jointly (§4.1).

**Use in deconvolution (§4–5).** The authors insert this numerical filter
into the effective filter of the
[approximate deconvolution model](/methods/scale-similarity-model.md),
multiplied by the LES filter, which, to their knowledge, "has not been
done before" (§1). In LES of the transitional
[Taylor–Green vortex](/benchmarks/taylor-green-vortex.md) at $Re=1600$ on
$64^3$ points:

- *Benefit only for a low-order LES filter.* Including the numerically
  induced filters in the effective filter helps only when the LES filter
  is of low order (§5.4.2, §6).
- *The interpolation filter adds nothing.* With dissipative compact
  interpolation, deconvolution with the first-derivative filter alone and
  with the product of it and the squared interpolation filter give
  "virtually the same" errors, because the interpolation filter acts on
  low-energy scales; adding the LES filter improves only minimally on
  the Layton–Lewandowski model (§5.4.2, Table 8).
- *Exact numerical filter, unstable run.* With spectral interpolation and
  no regularization, deconvolution with the sixth-order derivative filter
  and compact LES filters is unstable shortly after the peak of
  dissipation, which the authors attribute to the amplification of
  subfilter energy and the aliasing of the deconvolved product (§5.4.2,
  Table 6).
- *Effective Reynolds number.* In the laminar Taylor–Green vortex the
  derivative-induced filters leave the Reynolds number inferred from the
  energy decay at about its nominal value, while dissipative interpolation
  or explicit regularization lowers it, by several times for fourth-order
  compact interpolation (§5.1, Eq. 42, Figs. 10–11). Their conclusion is
  that "the overall effect of discretization-induced filtering is
  non-dissipative". Their classification of dissipative interpolation and
  regularization without a model as implicit LES is on
  [implicit LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md).

The interpolation results apply, in the authors' words, to algorithms
with explicit interpolation (staggered or half-staggered grids); collocated
grids with discrete kinetic-energy-conserving schemes do not have this
dissipation (§6).

## A contrary stance: filter-agnostic supervised training (Wu & Lele 2026)

[Wu & Lele (2026)](/sources/wu-2026.md) (arXiv v2) share the diagnosis
and reject the remedy. They agree that the filter of an LES "is
implicit and is a product of both the grid and the numerical method", and
that a network trained on box-filtered DNS meets a different stress field
in the solver (§1, §3). But they argue that reinforcement learning and
end-to-end differentiable-solver training, citing Kim et al. (2022), Bae
& Koumoutsakos (2022), Kurz et al. (2023), Sirignano & MacArt (2023) and
others, learn "the combined effect of solver-specific numerical effects
and turbulence physics", and
that such models are therefore "limited in their transferability
to other solvers" and costlier to train, since LES must run during
training (§1). Their alternative is supervised training on data filtered
with several filter shapes that "do not mimic the specific transfer
function of any specific LES solver": a box filter, a positive raised-cosine kernel, and a
box filter followed by the two-thirds dealiasing cutoff. A network trained
on all three runs, without retraining, in a Fourier-spectral solver with
dealiasing and in a sixth-order compact finite-difference solver without
it, and beats the box-only network in both (§5.2.1, §7–8; see
[neural-network SGS models](/methods/neural-network-sgs-model.md)). It is
not optimal in either: the authors trace its over-predicted tails of
enstrophy production in the compact solver to training on a filter (the
dealiasing one) that does not apply there.

## A learned discretization as the closure (Bezgin et al. 2025)

Bezgin et al. (2025) build on the premise of
[implicit LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md) that "the nonlinear
discretization error of the convective term functions as an SGS model"
(§1) and learn that error source directly. Their machine-learned implicit
LES (ML-ILES) keeps every component of an HLLC Godunov-type finite-volume
scheme with TENO6-A reconstruction and TVD-RK3 time integration, and
replaces only the TENO weighting: three multilayer perceptrons, one each
for density, velocity (applied to each component) and pressure, map the
five first-order differences of the six-point stencil to the convex
weights of its four fixed Harten-type candidate polynomials through a
softmax (§3.1, Eqs. 19–20; 3 hidden layers of 20 units, 1044 parameters
per network, 3132 in all). The trained object is thus the nonlinear
reconstruction, which the reference-list note of Beck & Kurz (2023, above)
names as the source of $C_3$ in finite volumes. See
[WENO schemes](/methods/weno-scheme.md) for the TENO building blocks.

**Training through the solver (§4.2, Eqs. 41–42).** The fully
differentiable solver JAX-Fluids is unrolled for $N_T\in\{1,2,5\}$ coarse
steps; the loss is the cell-wise mean absolute error of $\rho$,
$\boldsymbol u$, $p$ and $T$ against filtered DNS, with gradients from
automatic differentiation and no adjoint equations derived. The authors
state that for discretization learning end-to-end optimization "is
essential since there exists no a priori knowledge on the optimal choice
of the discretization scheme but rather on the optimal solution
trajectory" (§4.2). The targets are $512^3$ runs of compressible decaying
[isotropic turbulence](/benchmarks/homogeneous-isotropic-turbulence.md) at
$M_t=0.4$, 0.6, 0.8 and $Re_\lambda\approx200$, box-filtered by a factor
16 in space ($32^3$) and in time; the authors write that the box filter
"corresponds to implicit filtering commonly used in finite-volume methods
(the LES grid itself acts as a filter)" (§4.1).

**Results (§5.2–5.4, Tables 3, 5, Figs. 7–9).** Over the full decay at
$32^3$ the summed spectral mean absolute error of the $N_T=1$ model is
0.8849, 0.7923 and 0.6178 at the three Mach numbers, against 1.110, 1.153,
1.173 for TENO6-A and 1.099, 1.252, 1.428 for the compressible adaptive
local deconvolution method; the $N_T=5$ model, best in the multi-step
cell-wise error, is worst here (1.480, 1.562, 1.428; see
[a priori and a posteriori testing](/definitions/a-priori-and-a-posteriori-testing.md)).
Trained at $32^3$, the scheme run at $64^3$ "clearly outperforms TENO6-A"
in kinetic-energy and density spectra (Fig. 9). The pressure network
discards the downwind polynomial, while the velocity weights "somewhat
resemble" the spectrally optimized linear weights of TENO6-A (§5.3). One
step costs 2004.14 ns per cell against 452.70 ns for TENO6-A on an RTX
A6000 (Table 5), a ratio of 4.43 *(compiler check, not in the sources;
the text says "roughly 4.5 times")*.

**Stance on transfer.** Bezgin et al. (§1) state that machine-learned ILES
models, in which "ML surrogates are part of the discretization, may
provide better extrapolation capabilities to unseen meshes and flow
configurations" than explicit closures, and that restricting the output
to weights of consistent polynomials "provides a fallback mechanism …
while guaranteeing convergence upon mesh refinement" (§5.2). They cite
the DPM of Sirignano et al. (2020), whose explicit networks "account
implicitly for the discretization error of the chosen numerical
discretization scheme" (§1).

## Closures built this way

- **Element-local Smagorinsky coefficients in DG.** Beck & Kurz (2023, §V)
  optimize the coefficients by RL in a
  [DGSEM](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin-spectral-element-method.md) LES
  of forced isotropic turbulence. The quadratic intra-element coefficient
  learns a centre-peaked eddy viscosity that vanishes at the faces. The
  authors read it as accounting for $C_3$ as well as $C_2$, i.e. as
  compensating DG dissipation that acts through the face fluxes (Fig. 11).
  They flag this reading as needing investigation.
- **DG/finite-volume blending as an implicit model.** Beck & Kurz (2023,
  §V C) also control the blending factor $\alpha$ of the hybrid
  DG/finite-volume subcell scheme by RL. It is a closure made of
  discretization alone. [Kurz et al. (2025)](/sources/kurz-2025.md) (§2.3)
  cite this result: the blending has "been demonstrated to yield a
  sensible turbulence model if tuned correctly". Their GPU code
  [GALÆXI](/software/galaexi.md) runs every LES of the paper (the
  [Taylor–Green vortex](/benchmarks/taylor-green-vortex.md) at Mach 0.1
  and 1.25, the [NASA Rotor 37](/benchmarks/nasa-rotor-37.md) cascade)
  with no explicit subgrid model, the split-form DG (with the
  DG/finite-volume blending in the supersonic vortex and the cascade)
  supplying the only dissipation; in the cascade the blending factor
  stays between 0.1 and 0.7 near the leading-edge shock only (§2, §5.2, §6.3, Fig. 13). The paper calls the
  cascade run "wall-resolved LES" and does not use the term implicit LES;
  no blending factor is tuned as a turbulence model there.
- **Resolution-specific policies in DG.** Kurz et al. (2023, §4.2, §4.4,
  Figs. 6, 8) train an element-local Smagorinsky coefficient by RL at four
  DG resolutions. The coefficient distribution depends on the resolution
  the policy was trained on: at most about 0.4 for the 36-DOF policy and
  0.3 for the 48-DOF policy, whichever of the two grids each is run on,
  and almost the whole range up to 0.5 at 24 DOF. The authors conclude
  that different discretizations induce different implicit filters that
  need different policies, "discretization-adapted turbulent models for
  implicit LES" (§4.4).
- **Learned closure of all unclosed terms.** Sirignano & MacArt (2023, §2,
  Eqs. 2.2–2.3) let one neural network replace the subgrid-stress
  divergence together with the extra terms of a non-uniform filter on a
  non-uniform mesh (commutation errors, citing Ghosal 1996) and
  the boundary terms from filtering at an immersed body. The network is
  trained through the solver; the authors raise the risk of overfitting
  "to the numerical methods used for training" and test it
  (Qualifications).
- **Solver-consistent wall-model data.** Lozano-Durán & Bae (2023, §2.5)
  train the [building-block-flow wall model](/methods/building-block-flow-wall-model.md)
  on wall-modelled LES run in the deployment solver (charLES), with a
  subgrid correction that forces the mean profile onto DNS and a wall
  condition sampling the DNS wall-stress PDF, so that the data are
  "consistent with the numerical errors and gridding" of that solver. The
  stated reason is that the subgrid tensor of implicitly filtered LES does
  not coincide with the stress of the filtered Navier–Stokes equations.
- **CNN closures on a staggered grid.** Agdestein & Sanderse (2025, §4.4.2)
  train a CNN on the discrete commutator error. The authors read its small
  stencils as learned finite-difference operators.

## Relation to implicit LES and numerical dissipation

Sources on [implicit LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md) make a related
observation: the numerical scheme and the explicit model act together and
cannot be designed independently.

- **The interaction can defeat explicit models.** Flad & Gassner (2017,
  §1, §4) find that adding Smagorinsky models on top of a dissipative,
  de-aliased DG scheme gives no significant improvement "independent of
  the SGS model constants used". With a
  [kinetic-energy-preserving](/properties/kinetic-energy-preservation.md)
  split-form baseline, which adds no artificial kinetic-energy
  dissipation, stability and model dissipation are strictly separated, and on
  it a Smagorinsky model with $C_s=0.12$ beats implicit LES at $48^3$
  degrees of freedom.
- **The numerics can swamp the model.** Vicente Cruz & Lamballais (2023,
  §6.3) show that a Smagorinsky LES with filter width equal to the cell
  size is dominated by differentiation errors at $C_s=0.1$. It converges
  numerically only when the filter width is five cells (equivalently
  $C_s=0.5$ on the cell size). Their conclusion (§8) reverses the
  justification of implicit LES as an ersatz of explicit modelling:
  explicit SGS models "should be seen as an ersatz of regularization".
- **The terminology spans both views.** Duraisamy (2021, §VII) notes that
  in LES, especially without explicit filtering, disambiguating numerical
  from modelling errors is hard, and possibly unresolved even with explicit
  filtering. Model-consistent training absorbs numerical error into the
  closure, "at least as far as reconstructing the training data is
  concerned".

See [numerical dissipation](/properties/numerical-dissipation.md) for
where element methods dissipate, and
[a priori and a posteriori testing](/definitions/a-priori-and-a-posteriori-testing.md)
for why closures validated only on the continuous equations fail in the
solver.

## Qualifications

- **Two routes, two scopes.** Beck & Kurz (2023) argue that the closure
  must be optimized in the loop because $C_3$ of a nonlinear scheme
  depends on the running solution. Agdestein & Sanderse (2025, §5.2) train
  a CNN a priori on the discrete commutator error and find it stable and
  accurate, with a posteriori training adding little. Their coarse
  operator has none of the solution-adaptive mechanisms Beck & Kurz name
  (upwinding, non-oscillatory reconstruction, limiters): second-order
  central differences on a staggered grid, with an explicit Runge–Kutta
  method. Neither source tests the other's setting: the a priori route is
  shown only for such a non-adaptive scheme, and the in-the-loop route
  only for DG with upwind interface fluxes and for DG/finite-volume
  blending.
- **Consistency binds the closure to its discretization.** A
  discretization-consistent closure is tied to one scheme, polynomial
  degree and grid. Beck & Kurz (2023, §VI) present their models as a way
  of developing discretization-specific closures, not as ready-to-use
  ones. Agdestein & Sanderse (2025, §6) state that their filter's
  properties hold only for the second-order staggered divergence, with
  uniform weights and filter width equal to the coarse spacing.
  Lozano-Durán & Bae (2023, §5(3)) state that their wall model must be
  re-trained for each flow solver. Kurz et al. (2023, §5) do not propose
  their trained models for practical use.
- **How far the binding reaches is measured differently.** Kurz et al.
  (2023, §4.4, Fig. 8) run the 48-DOF policy at 36 DOF and the 36-DOF
  policy at 48 DOF (same $N=5$, other element counts): both stay stable
  and accurate with comparable spectral errors, still beat static
  Smagorinsky and no-model LES and match the dynamic model. Sirignano &
  MacArt (2023, §6, Fig. 15) move a network trained in their Python
  solver (fourth-order Runge–Kutta, coordinate-transformed second-order
  differences) into a semi-implicit solver without that transformation:
  the primary separation is similar and the wake less accurate, but the
  difference is smaller than the gap to the conventional models, and the
  models are "at least stable, and in some cases accurate" on out-of-sample
  numerics.
- **A supervised closure that judges the discretization error small.**
  [Kim et al. (2024)](/sources/kim-2024.md) train pointwise
  [neural-network SGS models](/methods/neural-network-sgs-model.md) on
  box-filtered DNS of a cylinder wake and run them in a second-order
  central-difference LES, i.e. on filter-first data. Following Sirignano
  et al., they estimate the discretization error as the mean relative
  difference between velocity gradients differenced on the LES and on the
  DNS grid: 0.4986 to 0.5690 over five LES grids (Eqs. 4.2–4.3, Table 4).
  They call these errors "not so significant" and state that the networks
  "may not suffer" from them (p. A6-21). Their grid-filtered and fusion
  networks are accurate on the training grid, and the fusion networks also
  on untrained coarser and finer grids (Table 3); on still coarser grids
  the LES with the fusion network, the dynamic Smagorinsky model and no
  model all diverge, and the fusion network runs only with its stresses
  clipped where backscatter occurs (App. C). The
  paper does not train a discretization-consistent counterpart, so the
  two routes are not compared on one case. Kim et al. themselves note that
  their a priori-trained models do not remove the a priori/a posteriori
  inconsistency (§5).
- **The same argument in RANS data assimilation.**
  [Fanizza et al. (2025)](/sources/fanizza-2025.md) (§1) argue that a
  correction field inferred on a diffusive second-order finite-volume
  RANS discretization absorbs discretization error as well as model error,
  so it may not extrapolate to other meshes or codes, and that high-order
  discretizations will be "mandatory" if learned corrections are to carry
  modelling error only. They run the inversion on a high-order modal DG
  discretization and recommend the lowest polynomial degree at which the
  baseline is free of grid dependence (§4.1, §4.3.2; see
  [field inversion and machine learning](/methods/field-inversion-machine-learning.md)).
  The argument is stated, not tested: no finite-volume inversion is run
  for comparison.
- **Binding against robustness: what each side prints.** Kurz et al.
  (2023, §1) call supervised learning of an implicitly filtered DG-LES
  closure ill-posed and, citing their ref. [8], state that surrogate
  targets from an explicit filter cause "severe inconsistencies"; Beck & Kurz (2023) and
  Lozano-Durán & Bae (2023) bind the closure to its solver on purpose.
  Wu & Lele (2026, §1) call that binding a limit on transferability and
  train on explicitly filtered data, with several filters. The settings
  differ: the binding side works in DG (Kurz et al.;
  Beck & Kurz), staggered finite volumes (Agdestein & Sanderse) or charLES
  (Lozano-Durán & Bae), Wu & Lele in a Fourier-spectral and a sixth-order
  compact solver; Wu & Lele do not run an in-the-loop closure, and the
  in-the-loop sources do not run a multi-filter one. The transfer tests
  that exist are those of Kurz et al. (2023) across resolutions and of
  Sirignano & MacArt (2023) across two solvers (above); Wu & Lele state
  the "limited transferability" without a test of their own. No source
  runs both kinds of closure on one flow.
- **Mesh transfer of a learned discretization.** Bezgin et al. (2025)
  assert convergence upon mesh refinement from the polynomial
  construction (§5.2) and show one refinement, $64^3$ against the
  training $32^3$ (Fig. 9); no convergence-order test is printed, and the
  overall scheme is stated, not shown, to be "at least third-order
  accurate" (§3.1). Different flow configurations are not run.
- **Box-filtered targets: what each side prints.** Kurz et al. (2023, §1),
  citing their ref. [8], state that supervised targets from an explicit
  box filter cause "severe inconsistencies between the training and
  actual simulations". Bezgin et al. (2025, §4.1–4.2) use box-filtered DNS
  as the trajectory target of in-the-loop training and call the box
  filter the implicit filter of finite-volume methods. Kurz et al. state
  theirs for supervised closure targets with a DG solver; Bezgin et al.
  use the box filter for trajectory targets of a finite-volume solver
  unrolled in training.
- **Reference data from the trained-against scheme.** The $512^3$
  reference of Bezgin et al. (2025) is itself computed with HLLC +
  TENO6-A, with Kolmogorov resolution $k_{\max}\eta\approx2.3$ ($k_{\max}$ the largest resolved wavenumber, page-local), while the
  authors state that $k_{\max}\eta\ge\pi$ "is required to fully resolve all
  scales" and that TENO6-A "acts as an ILES model" when not all scales
  are resolved (§4.1, Figs. 2–3). The baseline TENO6-A of the comparison
  is the scheme that produced the reference.
- **"Dissipative" in Caban et al. (2026).** The paper calls the effect of
  the first-derivative filter "non-dissipative" (§1, §3.2.1), "dissipative
  … without inducing numerical diffusion" (abstract) and "only dissipative
  … (not diffusive)" (§5.1). By its Eq. 27 that filter changes only the
  phase speed.
- **Definitions of "implicit LES" differ.** Agdestein & Sanderse (2025,
  §1) call any LES that keeps the DNS equations in their original form and
  adds a closure, whether or not the filter is known, "implicit LES".
  Beck & Kurz (2023) and Vicente Cruz & Lamballais (2023) reserve the term
  for LES in which the discretization supplies the closure. See
  [implicit LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md).

# Summary

Liu et al. (2022b) build a two-dimensional partitioned fluid–structure
interaction (FSI) solver for vortex-induced vibration of flexibly mounted
rigid bodies. The fluid side is the authors' in-house
[flux reconstruction](/methods/flux-reconstruction.md) (FR/CPR) solver for
the compressible Navier–Stokes equations in arbitrary Lagrangian–Eulerian
(ALE) form on moving unstructured triangles: nodal degree $N=2$ (third
order), Roe common fluxes, BR1 viscous fluxes, the correction field
evaluated in physical space, and the temporal
[geometric conservation law](/properties/geometric-conservation-law.md)
enforced by GCL error compensation. The coupling follows the linearized
fluid–solid Riemann problem of Banks et al.: interface velocity and stress
are impedance-weighted averages, and in the rigid-body limit the fluid
impedance adds a damping term to the body equation, so that the scheme
stays stable at zero body mass. Time integration is a four-stage
third-order Rosenbrock–Wanner method or SSPRK3; the grid deforms by an
algebraic quintic blending.

The scheme reaches third order on stationary and deforming grids. On the
[circular cylinder](/benchmarks/circular-cylinder-wake.md) at $Re_D=100$
with zero mass it gives plunge amplitude 0.41–0.44 $D$, $St=0.145$–0.149
and $C_D=1.58$–1.66 on three grids, inside the range of five earlier
studies. A cylinder with a nonlinear energy sink at $Re_D=106$ reproduces
the amplitude reductions of Mehmood et al., and a
[NACA 0012](/benchmarks/naca-0012-airfoil.md) with prescribed pitch and
flow-driven plunge at $Re_c=1000$ harvests energy at 20–34% efficiency.
All flows are laminar at $M=0.1$. Turbulence appears only as future work:
an extension to [implicit LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md) at moderate
Reynolds numbers and to RANS or
[wall-modelled LES](/methods/wall-modelled-les.md) at high Reynolds
numbers. The motivation for high order is that vortex-dominated FSI is
sensitive to [numerical dissipation](/properties/numerical-dissipation.md)
and dispersion.

# Key claims

1. The partitioned approach is more flexible than the monolithic one but less robust because of the added-mass effect; the paper uses a linearized fluid–solid interface Riemann problem to make it stable (§1). **Stated**
2. High-order CFD matters for FSI problems such as vortex-induced vibration because they are vortex-dominated and sensitive to numerical dissipation and dispersion (§1). **Stated**
3. The ALE equations are solved in the physical-domain form $\partial_tQ+\partial_xF+\partial_yG-\boldsymbol V_g\cdot\nabla Q=0$ (Eq. 8), obtained with the three GCL identities (Eq. 6); the two spatial identities are satisfied by the high-order spatial discretization, the temporal one is enforced by GCL error compensation (§2.1). **Stated**
4. The FR/CPR correction field is evaluated in physical space, $\delta^{\mathrm{cor}}=\tilde\delta^{\mathrm{Cor}}/\mathcal J$, to minimize errors on curved elements (Eq. 11, §2.2). **Stated**
5. Interface velocity and stress of the linearized fluid–solid Riemann problem are impedance-weighted averages (Eqs. 17–18); imposing them is stable even for light solids where traditional coupling fails (§2.3, after Banks et al.). **Stated**
6. In the rigid-body limit $z_s/z_f\to\infty$: $v_I=v_{s,0}$ and $\sigma_I=\sigma_{f,0}+z_f(v_{f,0}-v_{s,0})$ (Eqs. 19–20). **Derived**
7. Taking the body velocity at the new time level in the interface stress gives $m_b\dot v_b+z_fA_bv_b=\sigma_{f,t-\Delta t}A_b+z_fA_bv_{f,t-\Delta t}+f_{\mathrm{ext}}(t)$ (Eq. 23), so that the body does not overreact even with $m_b=0$ (§2.3). **Derived**
8. In two dimensions the Riemann coupling is applied in the normal direction and the interface condition is enforced strictly in the tangential direction; in the authors' experiments this suppresses the added-mass instability at zero solid mass (§2.3, §3.1.2). **Shown**
9. Within a time step the flow is advanced (SSPRK3 or Rosenbrock–Wanner) to convergence, then the rigid body by SSPRK3 with the interface forces, then the grid (§2.4). **Stated**
10. Third-order FR/CPR reaches its design order on stationary and deforming triangular grids: $L_2$ errors $8.22\times10^{-3}\to1.25\times10^{-5}$ (orders 3.4, 3.0, 3.0) stationary and $1.24\times10^{-2}\to1.76\times10^{-5}$ (orders 3.1, 3.2, 3.2) deforming, on $10\times10$ to $80\times80$ quadrilaterals split into triangles (Table 1, §3.1.1). **Shown**
11. Zero-mass cylinder at $Re_D=100$, SSPRK3, $\Delta t=0.0002$ s: 1440 elements give plunge amplitude $0.44D$, $St=0.149$, $C_D=1.66$; 3360 elements 0.42, 0.148, 1.61; 13,120 elements 0.41, 0.145, 1.58, agreeing reasonably with Yu & Wang (0.42, 0.146, 1.60), Shiels et al. (0.47, 0.156, 1.73), Eldredge (0.45, 0.148, 1.66), Zhu & Peng (0.47, 0.175, 1.75) and Yu et al. (0.42, about 0.155) (Table 2). **Shown**
12. The implicit Rosenbrock–Wanner method at $\Delta t=0.01$ s (50 times the explicit step) on the 3360-element mesh gives amplitude $0.41D$ and $St=0.146$, agreeing with SSPRK3 (§3.1.2; the printed drag value is listed under Printed slips). **Shown**
13. Without the energy sink the cylinder's maximum normalized displacement at $Re_D=106$ is 0.3757 (Mehmood et al.: 0.4167) (Table 3). **Shown**
14. Sink mass ratio $\mu_m=0.01$: cylinder maximum 0.3207, sink maximum 0.5153, a reduction of about 15%; sink and cylinder synchronize in frequency and phase after a sink-amplitude surge between normalized times 125 and 140 (§3.2.2, Fig. 5, Table 3). **Shown**
15. $\mu_m=0.03$: cylinder maximum 0.0933, sink maximum 0.3842, a reduction of about 75.2%, with wave-packet-like motion of non-constant frequency and amplitude (§3.2.2, Fig. 6). **Shown**
16. $\mu_m=0.05$: cylinder maximum 0.1186, sink maximum 0.4486, with a much longer start-up; results agree well with Mehmood et al. (cylinder 0.3541, 0.0911, 0.1147; sink 0.5848, 0.3773, 0.4447 for the three ratios) (Fig. 7, Table 3). **Shown**
17. In the oscillating-foil harvester, pitch amplitudes $\pm70°$ at $k=1.0$ give plunge histories of equal amplitude and opposite sign, a shift set by the initial pitch direction since the plunge has no stiffness, as Zhu & Peng observed (Fig. 10a). **Shown**
18. The foil reaches a periodic state after about one period (Fig. 10). **Shown**
19. Plunge amplitude decreases with reduced frequency for every pitch amplitude; at fixed $k$ a larger plunge velocity (amplitude) is said to give a larger efficiency (Table 4, §3.3.2). **Shown** (see Printed slips for the $k=0.8$ row)
20. Maximum efficiency is at $70°$ for $k=0.8$ (25.88%) and $k=1.0$ (28.66%) and at $90°$ for $k=1.2$ (33.91%), but the last lacks periodicity in the power coefficient, so $70°$ is recommended (Table 4, Fig. 11). **Shown**
21. Weak compressibility at $M\le0.1$ has negligible effect on forces and flow structures of oscillating cylinders and foils at low Reynolds number, so the compressible equations are used at $M=0.1$ without low-Mach preconditioning (§3, citing earlier studies). **Stated**
22. Each harvester case ran on 128 processes for about 24 h wall-clock on two-socket 8-core Intel E5-2650v2 nodes (§3). **Stated**
23. Future work: implicit LES at moderately high Reynolds numbers, RANS or wall-modelled LES at very high ones; the challenges are robustness with non-smooth eddy-viscosity fields and the cost of wall-distance-based models on moving grids (§4). **Stated**

# Printed slips

- §3.1.2: with the Rosenbrock–Wanner method "the time-averaged drag coefficient Cd is 0.159. All the values agree well with those from the explicit SSPRK3 method", while the SSPRK3 values in Table 2 are 1.58–1.66.
- Eq. (31) prints the sink stiffness term as $K_{NES}(\dot Y_2-\dot Y_1)^3$, a cubed velocity difference, while Eqs. (32), (36) and (38) use cubed displacement differences; Eq. (34) prints $\tilde K_{NES}(\dot Y_1^*-Y_2^*)^3$, which mixes a velocity and a displacement.
- Eq. (33) prints the damping coupling of the cylinder equation as $\tilde C_{NES}(\dot Y_1^*-\dot Y_2^*)$, opposite in sign to Eq. (31), $C_{NES}(\dot Y_2-\dot Y_1)$, and to Eq. (36), $-\tilde C_{NES}(y_2-y_4)$.
- Eqs. (34), (36), (38) and the Table 3 header write $\mu$ (and $\ddot Y_1$ without star) for $\mu_m$ (and $\ddot Y_1^*$); $\mu$ is also the viscosity.
- §3.3.1 defines the flow power as $P_f=0.5\rho_fU_\infty^2y_p$, a force per unit span, while $P=c\dot y^2$ is a power per unit span; for the efficiency $\bar P/P_f$ to be dimensionless the flow power must carry $U_\infty^3$ *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
- Table 3 "Relative motion ($y_1-y_3$)" prints values equal to the difference of the two printed maxima, sink maximum minus cylinder maximum ($0.5153-0.3207=0.1946$, $0.3842-0.0933=0.2909$, $0.4486-0.1186=0.3300$), that is with the sign of $y_3-y_1$ *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
- §3.3.2 states that at fixed reduced frequency a larger plunge amplitude gives a larger efficiency, while Table 4 at $k=0.8$ prints amplitude 0.4634 with 23.33% at $50°$ and the larger amplitude 0.4968 with 21.07% at $90°$ *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
- Time steps are quoted in seconds ("0.001 s", "0.0002 s", "0.01 s", "0.0001 s") in an otherwise nondimensional setting, with no reference scales.
- §3.3.1 writes $c^*=c/(\rho U_\infty C)$ with $\rho$ unsubscripted and then defines $\rho_f$.

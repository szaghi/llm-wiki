# Summary

Lv et al. (2021) argue that running a wall-model solver outside the LES
(the [equilibrium wall model](/methods/equilibrium-wall-model.md) on a
one-dimensional grid, the three-dimensional RANS
[non-equilibrium wall models](/methods/nonequilibrium-wall-model.md), the
integral wall model) is the root of three problems of
[wall-modelled LES](/methods/wall-modelled-les.md): cost, an ambiguous LES
solution in the wall-adjacent cell, and incompatibility with high-order
discretizations. Their "wall-model integrated" framework drops the
external solver: in the wall-adjacent cell one monomial of the scheme's
basis is replaced by the [law of the wall](/definitions/law-of-the-wall.md),
$\phi_w(y)=\kappa^{-1}\ln((y+y_0)/y_0)$, the LES solver computes all
coefficients from the filtered Navier–Stokes equations, and the wall-shear
stress follows from them as $\tau_w/\rho=a_w^2+\nu a_1$. With the basis
$\{1,y,\phi_w\}$ the authors call the method "practically" the integral
wall model of Yang et al. (2015), with "exactly 0" added cost, and they
assign non-equilibrium effects to the linear coefficient. The idea is
traced to the von Kármán–Pohlhausen momentum-integral method.

The framework runs in the SUPES solver, a
[discontinuous Galerkin](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin-spectral-element-method.md)
code extended with a PPM finite-volume scheme, the DG variational form
being kept only in wall-adjacent cells, with the
[Vreman model](/methods/vreman-model.md), a P3 scheme, local
Lax–Friedrichs and interior-penalty fluxes. On the
[turbulent channel](/benchmarks/turbulent-channel-flow.md) at
$Re_\tau=180$, 2000 and 50 000 on uniform grids of $32^3$ to $128^3$ the
mean velocity follows DNS for every grid; the log coefficient tracks the
first-cell velocity and the linear coefficient correlates with a
non-equilibrium measure. On the [periodic hill](/benchmarks/periodic-hill.md)
at $Re_h=10\,595$ the integrated model on the regular grid gives a better
$C_f$ than the equilibrium model on the leeside and near the crest, though
neither captures $C_f$ between $x/H\approx0$ and 2, and grid resolution
matters more than the wall model. The outlook asks
[machine-learning wall models](/methods/machine-learning-wall-model.md) to
seek basis functions rather than networks predicting $\tau_w$.

# Key claims

Page-local symbols: $u_\parallel$ wall-parallel velocity in the
wall-adjacent cell; $a_0$, $a_1$, $a_w$ the coefficients of the constant,
linear and log-law bases; $\phi_w$ the log-law basis; $y_0$ a viscous or
roughness length; $a'_{(\cdot)}$, $a''_{(\cdot)}$ the $x$ and $z$
components; $\mathcal N_x$ the source's non-equilibrium measure (its
$N_x$, renamed because the registry's $N_x$ counts grid points); P$p$ "an
order-$p$ scheme" as printed.

1. Having a wall-model solver outside the LES "is the root of many issues in WMLESs": cost, ambiguity of the LES solution in the wall-adjacent cell, and difficulty with high-order schemes (§I, Abstract). **Stated**
2. A piecewise-linear finite-volume reconstruction in the wall cell constrained by the wall-model gradient gives a wall velocity $U_w=U_{h_{\mathrm{wm}}}-\tau_wh_{\mathrm{wm}}/(\rho\nu)$, "negative and unphysical"; taking the wall-model solution instead gives a discontinuity at the first off-wall point (§I, Fig. 2). **Derived** (schematic)
3. Forcing the wall-model gradient at the wall "is also why Frere et al. found oscillation in the solution" in their DG WMLES (§I). **Stated**
4. For $u_\parallel=a_1y+a_0$ in a resolved wall cell, $\tau_w/\rho=\nu a_1$; in an unresolved wall layer this underestimates $\tau_w$ (§II A, Eqs. 1–2). **Derived**
5. With the basis $\phi_w=\kappa^{-1}\ln((y+y_0)/y_0)$, $\kappa=0.41$, the coefficient is the friction velocity, $\tau_w/\rho=u_\tau^2=a_w^2$; with $\{1,y,\phi_w\}$, $u_\parallel=a_w\phi_w+a_1y+a_0$ and $\tau_w/\rho=a_w^2+\nu a_1$, "the sum of wall-shear stresses carried by the logarithmic term and linear term" (§II A, Eqs. 3–6). **Derived** *(Compiler check, not in the sources: the viscous wall gradient of $a_w\phi_w$ is $\nu a_w/(\kappa y_0)$, which equals $a_w^2$ only for $y_0=\nu/(\kappa a_w)$; the $a_w^2$ term comes from the identification $a_w=u_\tau$, and the paper does not say how $y_0$ is set.)*
6. Wall modelling is recast as "solution reconstruction according to a pre-specified set of basis functions in the wall-adjacent cell"; since the LES solver computes the coefficients, no stand-alone wall-model solver is needed, which removes "RANS legacies" from WMLES (§II A, §II B 1). **Stated**
7. The idea is inspired by the von Kármán–Pohlhausen method, $u_\parallel/u_\infty=\sum_{i=1}^4\beta_i(y/\delta)^i$ (Eq. 7); its polynomial bases and those of Eq. (5) "are both weak solutions to the NS equations" (§II A). **Stated**
8. General basis of a P$p$ scheme: $\{y^i,\ i=0,\dots,p-2;\ \ln((y-y_0)/y_0)\}$ (Eq. 8); only the log term and $y$ carry wall-shear stress, since $dy^i/dy=0$ at $y=0$ for $i\ge2$; "Hence, P3 seems to be a good compromise between cost and accuracy" (§II B 3). **Derived**
9. "The integral wall model truncates at $p=3$"; with the bases of Eq. (5) one "is practically applying the integral wall model—but without an algebraic solver outside the LES" (§II B 3). **Stated** (no side-by-side comparison)
10. Costs of conventional wall models, as reported in the cited works: for grids of $O(10^6$–$10^7)$ the non-equilibrium wall model adds 100–150% and the ODE equilibrium model 30–40%; integral and algebraic equilibrium models usually no more than 5% (§II B 4). **Stated**
11. "The integrated wall model has exactly 0 cost": a WMLES with it costs the same as an LES without a wall model, its scalability set by the LES solver alone (§II B 4). **Stated** (no timing shown)
12. The slip-wall approach of Bose and co-authors was "the first attempt" to remove RANS legacies and "has numerical difficulty in some codes" (§II B 1). **Stated**
13. Frere et al. (2017) solved the polynomial coefficients in the LES but took the wall flux from the log law; the earlier DG wall model of Lv, Yang, Park & Ihme (Ref. 24, Comput. Fluids 2021) added the log function as an extra basis without removing a polynomial, so its coefficient had to be solved outside the LES; the present method replaces a basis (§II B 2). **Stated**
14. Two wall-parallel components: $\tau_{w,x}/\rho=\sqrt{(a'_w)^2+(a''_w)^2}\,a'_w+\nu a'_1$ and likewise for $z$ (Eqs. 9–10); for a P3 code with quadratic $x$ and $z$ factors, Eqs. (11)–(12) (§II C). **Derived** ("it is not difficult to show")
15. The SUPES DG algorithm achieves a 10× speed-up over a state-of-the-art unstructured finite-volume solver but remains more costly on structured curvilinear meshes (§III, citing their Ref. 34). **Stated**
16. A strongly imposed wall state (Euler flux with a boundary state of zero velocity and fixed temperature) "rigorously enforces the conservation laws" and avoids mass and energy drift in internal flows, though it "might not converge as fast" as the weak condition (§III B). **Stated**
17. Channel at $Re_\tau=2000$: the mean velocity "follows the DNS data closely, irrespective of the grid resolution" ($32^3$, $64^3$, $128^3$); rms velocities and Reynolds shear stress approach DNS as resolution increases (§IV B 1, Figs. 5–6). **Shown**
18. The log coefficient $a'_w$ "correlates very well" with the instantaneous streamwise velocity at the first off-wall point, as expected in an equilibrium flow (§IV B 1, Fig. 7). **Shown** (contours)
19. The linear coefficient $a'_1$ correlates with $\mathcal N_x=\tilde{\boldsymbol u}\cdot\nabla\tilde u+\rho^{-1}\nabla p\,|_{y=\Delta}$ as printed, at the first off-wall point (convection plus pressure gradient): similar contours, a correlated joint PDF, persisting across three grids; coarser grids give a more concentrated $\mathcal N_x$ PDF (§IV B 1, Figs. 8–10). **Shown**
20. When the grid resolves the wall layer ($Re_\tau=180$, $y_1^+=2.81$) the linear term "would take over" and the WMLES follows DNS also in the viscous sublayer and buffer layer, with Reynolds stresses "slightly off in the buffer layer" (§IV B 1, Figs. 11–12). **Shown**
21. The WMLES captures the log law "at any Reynolds numbers" ($Re_\tau=180$, 2000, 50 000) without an external wall-model solver (§IV B 1, Fig. 11). **Shown**
22. Periodic hill: grid resolution has "a more notable impact on the results than the wall model"; the coarse grid is worse because it captures the pressure force less well (Fig. 16), consistent with Xu et al.: capturing the pressure force matters more than skin friction where pressure is the dominant momentum sink (§IV B 2, Figs. 15–16). **Shown** + **Stated**
23. "The acid test for a wall model is the friction coefficient": neither wall model captures $C_f$ "from 0 to about 2", but the integrated model on the regular grid gives "considerable more accurate estimates of the skin friction" than the equilibrium model on the leeside and near the crest (§IV B 2, Fig. 17), with $C_f$ printed as $\tau_w/(2\rho U_b^2)$, $U_b$ the bulk velocity at the hill top. **Shown**
24. Accuracy depends on the LES scheme: higher-order schemes carry more reconstruction terms and are more accurate and costlier; P3 is "a good compromise" (§V). **Stated**
25. Machine learning for WMLES "ought to focus on finding the most appropriate basis functions (rather than finding a network that predicts the wall-shear stress)" (§V). **Stated**

# Printed slips

- **Eq. (3) against Eq. (8):** Eq. (3) prints $\phi_w(y)=\frac1\kappa\log\left(\frac{y+y_0}{y_0}\right)$, Eq. (8) prints "$\log((y-y_0)/y_0)$". *(Compiler check, not in the sources: with $y-y_0$ the basis is undefined for $0\le y<y_0$, the wall included.)*
- **Table II, Hill-Equil:** printed $N=64^3$, like Hill-R (the text calls both "the regular grid LESs"), but $\Delta_1/H=$ "0.063–0.094", the range printed for Hill-C ($64\times32\times32$), not Hill-R's "0.031–0.047". *(Compiler check, not in the sources: assuming uniform wall-normal cells, the local heights $2.036H$ (crest) to $3.036H$ (inlet) over 32 cells give 0.064–0.095 and over 64 cells 0.032–0.047, so the Hill-Equil range is that of a 32-cell wall-normal grid.)*
- **§IV B 2, Fig. 15 discussion:** "Both Hill-C and Hill-Equil capture the mean flow very well. The Reynolds stress and the turbulent kinetic energy in Hill-C and Hill-Equil are slightly off at the leeside", followed by "Hill-C, i.e., the coarse grid LES, is noticeably worse than Hill-R and Hill-Equil" and "Hill-C does not do very well in capturing the mean flow".
- **§IV B 1:** "In Sec. III, we argue that the linear term responds to and accounts for deviations from the log law"; the argument is in Sec. II (Sec. III describes the solver).
- **Normalization of $a'_1$:** the Fig. 8 caption says "normalized by $u_\tau^2/h$", the Fig. 9 axis reads $a'_1/(u_\tau h_{wm}/h^2)$; $h_{wm}$ appears although the framework has no matching location; the Fig. 8 caption says "non-equilibrium terms $N$" where panel and text use $N_x$.
- **Overhead of the non-equilibrium wall model:** "∼100%" (§I, §V) against "100%–150%" (§II B 4).
- **§IV A:** the channel domain is printed "$L_x\times L_y\times L_z\in2\pi h\times2h\times2\pi h$".

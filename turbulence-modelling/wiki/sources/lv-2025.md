# Summary

Lv et al. (2025) aim to cut the cost of "vortex-resolved" compressible
flow computations by coupling a modal discontinuous Galerkin method
(quadratic Taylor basis, $N=2$; HLLC inviscid flux; the direct
discontinuous Galerkin viscous flux of Cheng et al. 2016; SSP-RK3) with
h-adaptive [mesh refinement](/methods/mesh-adaptation.md) on quadrilaterals
with one level of hanging nodes. It is a modal DG, not the nodal
[DGSEM](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin-spectral-element-method.md). No
turbulence or subgrid model is used. Refinement is driven by a vortex
indicator found earlier by the same group with symbolic regression (Feng
et al. 2025, an SSRN preprint): a tanh of a closed form in the $x$- and
$y$-gradients of two characteristic eigenvalues and the cell size, whose
"base term" flags rotation of the local characteristics (vortex core) and
whose $h^2$-weighted "activation term" flags their "departure" (vortex
boundary), credited with catching dissipative, under-resolved vortices.
It is an indicator, not a closure. The paper sets it beside the
[vortex identification criteria](/definitions/vortex-identification-criterion.md)
it surveys (vorticity, $Q$, $\Omega$, $\Delta$, $\lambda_{ci}$, FTLE),
which it says need case- and grid-sensitive thresholds.

The paper's own steps are the indicator's evaluation on non-conforming
grids, by direction- and area-weighted averages of neighbour cell means,
and the adaptation loop: refine where the indicator exceeds 0.04, coarsen
elsewhere, every time step, up to two or three levels, with $L^2$
projection and fine-side face quadrature for conservation. Three 2D
tests compare indicator-driven and $\Omega$-driven refinement with coarse
and fine uniform grids: a laminar [square cylinder](/benchmarks/square-cylinder-flow.md)
at $M=0.3$, $Re=150$; an inviscid Kelvin–Helmholtz double shear layer;
and a [NACA 0012](/benchmarks/naca-0012-airfoil.md) at $M=0.3$, $15°$
(Reynolds number printed both as $10^6$ and $10^5$), computed without a
turbulence model. Evidence is visual comparison of fields plus serial CPU
times: adaptation saves 81–85% against the fine grid on the cylinder and
airfoil, and costs 26.03 against 8.37 min on the Kelvin–Helmholtz case.
The motivation cites the low [numerical dissipation](/properties/numerical-dissipation.md)
of DG and the loss of vortices on coarse grids. No error norm, force
coefficient, Strouhal number or spectrum is reported. The paper does not
call its computations [DNS](/definitions/direct-numerical-simulation.md).

# Key claims

Page-local symbols, as on [mesh adaptation](/methods/mesh-adaptation.md):
$\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{1}$, $\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{2}$ characteristic
eigenvalues of the inviscid flux Jacobian in the $x$ and $y$ directions
(the source's $\lambda_{n_1}$, $\lambda_{n_2}$, from $u\pm a$, $v\pm a$; not
the Taylor microscale), with subscripts ${}_{,x}$, ${}_{,y}$ their
derivatives; $I_{\mathrm{SR}}$ the post-processed indicator (the source's
$\tilde S^*$) and $I_{\mathrm{SR},e}$ its element value; $M_{\mathrm I}$,
$M_\Omega$ the refinement thresholds (the paper's $M$, $M_\Omega$; not
the Mach number); $l_{\max}$ the maximum refinement level; $h_0$ the
initial cell size.

1. The symbolic-regression indicator of Ref. 13 can be evaluated on non-conforming quadrilateral grids with hanging nodes (level difference at most one) through the weighted left/right averages of Eqs. (26)–(31). §IV A 2. **Derived**
2. The post-processed indicator is $I_{\mathrm{SR}}=\tanh\big(-\big[(\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{1,x}-\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{2,y})^2+4\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{1,y}\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{2,x}+h^2(\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{1,x}\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{2,y}-\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{1,y}\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{2,x})\big]\big)$. §IV A 1, Eq. (25). **Stated** (from Ref. 13; the post-processing of Eq. (24) is not shown)
3. When $h$ is small, $I_{\mathrm{SR}}>0$ is dominated by a negative base term, "which physically implies that the local characteristics exhibit rotation"; when $h$ is not small, $I_{\mathrm{SR}}>0$ is "equivalent to" a negative base term or $\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{1,x}\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{2,y}-\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{1,y}\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{2,x}<0$ ("rotation or departure"). §IV A 1. **Stated** (analysis deferred to Ref. 13)
4. The base term identifies the vortex core and the activation term the vortex boundary, which "might offer supplementary benefits" on coarse grids or for incipient and small vortices. §IV A 1. **Stated**
5. The regression of Ref. 13 used 10 000 samples, features $[\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{1,x},\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{1,y},\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{2,x},\lambda^{\mathrm c}_{2,y},h]$, labels $\pm1$, a population of 10 000, operators $+$, $-$, $*$ and square, loss $\sum(\tanh(S_k)-Y_i)^2$, termination at loss below $10^{-5}$ and complexity below 15, and stopped at the 2000-iteration limit. §IV A 1, Eqs. (20)–(23). **Stated**
6. $L^2$-projection transfer (Eqs. 32–33) and fine-side quadrature on non-conforming faces "can strictly preserve the conservation of the DDGM with the AMR strategy". §IV B 2. **Stated**
7. The indicator-driven adaptation is "almost parameter-free": only $M_{\mathrm I}$ and $l_{\max}$ are set; lowering $M_{\mathrm I}$ "poses low risk" but adds noise and cost, raising it risks missing dissipative vortices. §V. **Stated**
8. The minimum resolvable scale is $h_0/2^{l_{\max}}$. §V. **Stated**
9. Square cylinder ($M=0.3$, $Re=150$, $t=2$): both indicators concentrate cells in the wake vortex street and give vortex resolution similar to the fine uniform grid. §V A, Figs. 6–7. **Shown** (visual)
10. Square-cylinder serial CPU times (min): coarse 12.05, fine 230.37, $\Omega$-driven 40.88, $I_{\mathrm{SR},e}$-driven 43.58; savings against the fine grid 82.25% and 81.08%. §V A, Table I. **Shown**
11. Kelvin–Helmholtz ($t=1$, $l_{\max}=3$): $I_{\mathrm{SR},e}$-driven adaptation gives "the highest vortex resolution", $\Omega$-driven is comparable to the fine uniform grid, the coarse grid "can barely resolves any vortex structures". §V B, Figs. 8–9. **Shown** (visual)
12. Kelvin–Helmholtz CPU times (min): coarse 2.73, fine 8.37, $\Omega$-driven 12.53, $I_{\mathrm{SR},e}$-driven 26.03; the text calls the last "relatively costly but still acceptable", and the abstract and conclusion claim "extremely high vortex resolution with slight increase in computational resources". §V B, Table II. **Shown**
13. In Fig. 9 the $I_{\mathrm{SR},e}$ field marks whole shear-layer bands and the grid is refined in broad strips around both interfaces, whereas $\Omega$ refines patches around the roll-up cores. §V B, Fig. 9(c)–(d). **Shown** (qualitative)
14. NACA 0012 ($M=0.3$, $15°$): both indicators capture vortices of different scales, including small leading-edge vortices, "without relying on pre-refined boundary layer grids". §V C, Figs. 11–13. **Shown** (visual)
15. On the NACA 0012 the $I_{\mathrm{SR},e}$-driven field is closest to the fine uniform grid, while $\Omega$-driven refinement "exhibits quantifiable deviations in both the morphology and size of the vortices", attributed to $\Omega$ capturing dissipative vortices less well. §V C. **Shown** (visual; no quantity given)
16. NACA 0012 CPU times (min): coarse 60.83, fine 415.01, $\Omega$-driven 62.27, $I_{\mathrm{SR},e}$-driven 65.40; savings 85.00% and 84.24%. §V C, Table III. **Shown**
17. Some vortex criteria ($Q$ cited) need case- and grid-sensitive thresholds, and some "might fail to capture dissipative vortices with scales smaller than the grid scale", leaving small vortices under-resolved during adaptation. §I. **Stated**
18. The global time step is printed $\Delta t^n=\max_{e\le N}\{\frac{\mathrm{CFL}}{2N+1}\frac{h_e}{\lambda_{\max}}\}$ with CFL $=0.18$ for all unsteady problems (the paper's $k$ for $N$). §III B, Eq. (16). **Stated**
19. Direct-DG coefficients $\beta_0=2$, $\beta_1=1/12$, interface length $\lvert\boldsymbol l_{ef}\cdot\boldsymbol n_{ef}\rvert$ (centroid-to-centroid vector projected on the face normal). §III A, Eq. (13). **Stated**
20. "Fixed number" and "fixed fraction" strategies are "typically employed for steady flows"; the fixed-value strategy is used here for unsteady flows. §IV B 1. **Stated**

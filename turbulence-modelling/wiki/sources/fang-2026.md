# Summary

Fang et al. (2026) review the use of artificial intelligence, mainly deep
learning, in turbulence research, citing 214 works. Section 2 gives the
generic equations of the architectures: multilayer perceptrons and the
operator learners built on them (DeepONet, MscaleDNN,
[physics-informed networks](/methods/physics-informed-neural-network.md),
the Fourier neural operator), convolutional and recurrent networks,
autoencoders and variational autoencoders, generative adversarial
networks, transformers and policy-gradient reinforcement learning.
Section 3 sorts applications into turbulence modelling
([neural-network SGS models](/methods/neural-network-sgs-model.md),
[super-resolution SGS models](/methods/super-resolution-sgs-model.md),
[neural-operator LES surrogates](/methods/neural-operator-les-surrogate.md),
[reinforcement-learning wall models](/methods/reinforcement-learning-closure.md)
and [machine-learning wall models](/methods/machine-learning-wall-model.md)),
flow prediction, flow control (largely outside this bundle) and the
discovery of mechanisms by clustering, for instance the linear growth of
structure size with wall distance read as support of the
[attached-eddy hypothesis](/definitions/attached-eddy-hypothesis.md).
Section 4 covers the challenges: data volume and noise, physical
consistency and interpretability (physics-constrained networks,
[symbolic regression](/methods/symbolic-regression-closure.md) of
[SST](/methods/k-omega-sst-model.md) corrections), observable-augmented
manifold learning, and trustworthy AI, where it restates the
[a priori against a posteriori](/definitions/a-priori-and-a-posteriori-testing.md)
problem of CNN closures (stable a priori, unstable a posteriori, mitigated
by transfer learning and explicit
[backscatter](/definitions/energy-backscatter.md) modelling) and lists
uncertainty-quantification methods relevant to
[model-form uncertainty](/definitions/model-form-uncertainty.md).
Section 5 surveys climate, aviation, wind-energy and combustion uses,
graph networks and attention, and coupling of experiment and simulation,
including Bayesian optimization of RANS coefficients
([Bayesian calibration](/methods/bayesian-closure-calibration.md)).

The review proposes no method and runs no computation; every number is
quoted from a cited study without verification. It is a pointer list for
this bundle: its claims corroborate, and never originate, the methods
cited on the concept pages. Several attributions do not match the printed
reference list (see Printed slips).

# Key claims

All claims are the review's restatements of cited work (evidence kind
**Stated** unless marked); the cited study is named in brackets.

1. The review synthesizes "over 200 recent studies" on AI in turbulence research, organized by architecture and by task (modelling, flow prediction, control, physical-insight discovery) (Abstract, §1). **Stated**
2. A Web of Science Core Collection survey of 2005–2024 shows that "growth in AI-based approaches has significantly surpassed that of traditional turbulence modeling techniques" (Fig. 1, §1). **Shown** (bibliometric figure)
3. Deep learning contributes to turbulence modelling in a "three-stage process": feature extraction and preprocessing (POD, DMD; Reynolds stress, turbulent kinetic energy and anisotropy as key variables), training on high-dimensional data, and integration with traditional models to compensate for their systematic biases (§3.1, Fig. 7). **Stated**
4. Multi-agent reinforcement-learning wall models that "respect known physical invariants" ([60], Bae & Koumoutsakos, attributed in the text to "Koumoutsakos et al.") are called "a conceptual shift toward embedding learning agents within the numerical simulation loop" (§3.2). **Stated**
5. An LSTM trained on channel DNS "with 1000 time-steps under a Re = 180 condition" predicted the centre-region shear Reynolds stress with relative error "less than 5%", against "about 12%" for the $k$-$\varepsilon$ model ([88]) (§3.4, Table 3). **Stated**
6. A transformer inflow generator for spatially developing boundary layers, trained on DNS with $Re_\theta$ "from 661.5 to 1502.0", gave "less than 5% error" in turbulence intensity and energy spectrum ([89]) (§3.4, Table 3). **Stated**
7. Physics-informed neural-operator predictive control achieved "a 39% reduction in drag" in high-Reynolds-number drag-reduction experiments ([90]) (§3.4, Table 3). **Stated**
8. turbo-RANS calibrates RANS coefficients by Bayesian optimization with an objective function named GEDCP, on holistic, sparse or dense data, and "significantly improves the prediction accuracy by 12–26% compared to default RANS model" ([95]) (§3.4, §4.3, Table 3). **Stated**
9. A turbulence-model-augmented PINN reconstructs mean flows from sparse velocity data better than the RANS model, with mean-velocity error "from 11.7% to 3.1%, a reduction of about 73%", and avoids the discretization error of variational data assimilation ([96]) (§3.4, §4.2.2, Table 4). **Stated**
10. Physics-induced RL puts turbulence stability and kinetic-energy dissipation into the reward and "reduces the 3D turbulence control error by approximately 35%" ([97]) (§3.4, Table 4). **Stated**
11. RL control results quoted: channel blowing/suction with "more than 35% drag reduction even at Re = 1000" ([100]); a turbulent separation bubble reduced by "25.3%, compared to 15.7% with periodic control" ([101]); PPO bluff-body control with "29% drag reduction and 18% reduction in lift oscillations" ([102]); multi-agent RL with "ten pairs of synthetic jets" on a 3D cylinder giving "a 9% drag reduction" ([103]) (§3.4, Table 4). **Stated**
12. A genetic-algorithm search for multi-actuator excitation behind a cylinder improved energy efficiency "by 30% compared with the single-frequency control" (Qiao et al., no reference number printed) (§3.4). **Stated**
13. Clustering of $Q^-$ events in channel turbulence found that the scale of coherent structures "increases linearly with the wall distance", "further confirming Townsend's wall-attached vortex hypothesis" ([117]) (§3.5). **Stated**
14. Gaussian-mixture clustering on invariants of the strain-rate and rotation-rate tensors separates viscous-dominated from inviscid regions of boundary layers and wakes ([116]) (§3.5). **Stated**
15. The Johns Hopkins Turbulence Database "alone comprises over 350 TB of spatio-temporal data" ([122]) (§4.1). **Stated**
16. A single high-resolution snapshot with a spectral loss and data augmentation "can suffice for training super-resolution models" ([127]); temporal dynamics in the loss recover time evolution and long-term statistics ([128]) (§4.1). **Stated**
17. A CNN-β-VAE compresses 2D turbulent fields into "a 10-dimensional latent space, achieving over 97% energy retention", against 268 POD modes ([134]) (§4.1). **Stated**
18. Parametric PINNs take physical parameters as inputs ("train once, generalize across parameters"), predict 3D backward-facing-step flows and cost "up to 1/16 of traditional CFD methods" ([142]) (§4.2.2, Fig. 9). **Stated**
19. PINNs with embedded RANS models (standard $k$-$\varepsilon$ and a mixing-length-type model) predicted a backward-facing step at Reynolds number 5100 from sparse data ([143]) (§4.2.2). **Stated**
20. The implicit U-Net enhanced FNO beats the FNO, the U-FNO and "physics-based dynamic Smagorinsky models" on spectra, vorticity and spatial statistics, at lower cost, and generalizes to higher $Re_\lambda$ ([149]) (§4.2.2). **Stated**
21. CNN SGS closures that succeed a priori can show "instability and energy divergence during a posteriori rollout", especially over long horizons or at higher Reynolds number; transfer learning and "explicit modeling of backscatter dynamics" mitigate this ([55]) (§4.2.4). **Stated**
22. Of deep ensembles, MC dropout, Bayesian neural networks and stochastic variational inference, deep ensembles had the highest predictive accuracy but were "prone to overconfidence in out-of-distribution regimes", while variational inference was "more conservative" ([164]); Bayesian neural networks quantify aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty of reacting-flow closures a priori ([165]); Mondrian forests are a non-parametric alternative ([166]) (§4.2.4). **Stated**
23. The long-term rollout stability of FNOs for 3D turbulence "is closely tied to the spectral distribution of the learned dynamics and temporal autocorrelation properties" ([167]) (§4.2.4). **Stated**
24. Mixed precision cut solution time by about 38% and energy by 2.8 times in Nekbone, and by about 29% and 24% in Neko ([180]); data-loading optimizations raised GPU utilization "from about 65–95%" ([181]); parallel preprocessing cut data-preparation time "by about 40%" ([182]) (§4.3). **Stated**
25. A linear-attention FNO "reduces the turbulence prediction error by 40%" on 3D isotropic and free-shear turbulence "without increasing the computational cost" ([197]); a self-supervised graph transformer cut RMSE "from 0.970 and 0.561 to 0.616 and 0.305" for cylinder and airfoil ([199]); an attention network for hypersonic turbulence runs "more than a hundred times" faster than CFD ([200]) (§5.2). **Stated**
26. A graph neural network has been embedded in an industrial LES solver for online wall-shear-stress prediction ([195]) (§5.2). **Stated**
27. Physically constrained symbolic regression extracts RANS closures that "preserve invariance properties, remain analytically tractable, and generalize well across flow configurations", here an SST enhancement ([202]) (§5.2). **Stated**
28. A network trained on cloud-resolving simulations represents subgrid convection in a climate model ([183]); a DNN trained on DNS predicts atmospheric-boundary-layer SGS stresses "with higher relevance than the traditional Smagorinsky model" across grid resolutions ([184]) (§5.1). **Stated**
29. A CGAN plus XGBoost model for LiDAR flight-turbulence identification reaches "78%" accuracy ([188]) (§5.1). **Stated**
30. The challenges are tabulated under data (volume, multiscale, quality, cost), interpretability (black box, physical consistency, mechanism, generalization, data dependence) and computing (HPC, training time, hyper-parameters, software environments) (Table 5, §4). **Stated**

The governing equations of §1 in registry notation: the RANS form (Eq. 2)
is $\partial_t\langle\boldsymbol u\rangle+\langle\boldsymbol u\rangle\cdot\nabla\langle\boldsymbol u\rangle=-\nabla\langle p\rangle+\nu\nabla^2\langle\boldsymbol u\rangle-\nabla\cdot R+\boldsymbol f$
and the LES form (Eq. 3) $\partial_t\bar{\boldsymbol u}+\bar{\boldsymbol u}\cdot\nabla\bar{\boldsymbol u}=-\nabla\bar p+\nu\nabla^2\bar{\boldsymbol u}-\nabla\cdot\tau+\boldsymbol f$,
$p$ kinematic; the review defines neither the filter nor $\tau_{ij}$ in
terms of the velocity. The a priori and a posteriori tests are defined
as "a priori model validation—where a learned closure is tested on a
dataset—and a posteriori validation—where it is deployed in full LES
simulations" (§4.2.4).

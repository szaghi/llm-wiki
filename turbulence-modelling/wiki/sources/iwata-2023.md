# Summary

Iwata et al. (2023) run three-dimensional
[direct numerical simulations](/definitions/direct-numerical-simulation.md)
of a cellular gaseous detonation propagating into isotropic turbulence,
with a detailed 19-reaction hydrogen mechanism instead of the one-step
chemistry of earlier detonation–turbulence DNS. The question is how the
cell regularity of the mixture conditions its response to turbulence. Two
stoichiometric mixtures are compared, 2H2–O2–7Ar at 60 kPa (regular cells)
and 2H2–O2–Ar at 40 kPa (irregular cells), with pressures chosen so that
both have a 1.92 mm cell width (the lateral domain size), each without and
with incoming turbulence at $Re_\lambda=240$, $M_t=0.30$. The inflow is a
decaying [isotropic turbulence](/benchmarks/homogeneous-isotropic-turbulence.md)
field convected in through the inlet of a detonation-fixed frame, a form of
[inflow turbulence generation](/methods/inflow-turbulence-generation.md).
The in-house code FK3 uses fifth-order [WENO](/methods/weno-scheme.md)
reconstruction of the conserved variables, an HLLC/Lax–Friedrichs flux
switched by a pressure [shock sensor](/definitions/shock-sensor.md), a
uniform 4 µm grid ($2.76\times10^8$ cells) and about 50 h per case on
28 800 Fugaku cores.

Turbulence corrugates shock and flame and creates small unburned pockets.
It severely disturbs the numerical soot foil of the regular mixture and
barely changes that of the irregular one. The velocity deficits (0.976 to
0.981 and 0.947 to 0.951 of the Chapman–Jouguet velocity) do not depend on
turbulence. Averaged profiles show unchanged reaction progress in the
regular mixture and faster reaction in the irregular one; the paper calls
both results "different from" the turbulence-induced delay that Jin et al.
found with one-step chemistry. Behind the shock
the Taylor microscales are anisotropic (about 25 µm streamwise, 50 µm
lateral), as in inert [shock-generated turbulence](/results/shock-generated-turbulence.md),
and the post-shock turbulence is mainly generated by the detonation itself.
Turbulent species and heat fluxes exceed the molecular ones by orders of
magnitude, but flame curvature and heat release are uncorrelated: the
flame front stays governed by shock-induced self-ignition. The authors
attribute the faster reaction to a broader shock-strength distribution,
which shortens the mean induction length from 0.321 to 0.295 mm.

# Key claims

1. DNS of detonation–turbulence interaction with detailed chemistry (19 elementary reactions, nine species including Ar) is performed at two argon dilutions to isolate the effect of cell regularity; earlier DNS (refs 12–14) used one-step chemistry (§I, §II A). **Stated**
2. A unity Lewis number is assumed; the authors argue it has "an insignificant impact since turbulent transport mostly governs the post-shock flow", supported by longitudinal turbulent species fluxes two orders of magnitude above molecular diffusion (§II A; §IV B, Fig. 11a). **Shown**
3. FK3 reproduces the Chapman–Jouguet velocity in a one-dimensional 2H2–O2–7Ar test at 4 µm spacing (starting overdriven, converging to it) and the ZND temperature and pressure profiles (§II B, Fig. 2). **Shown**
4. The half-reaction length is almost identical in the two mixtures and resolved by 30 grid points, meeting the requirement for mixtures of moderate cell regularity (ref 15) (§II C). **Stated**
5. The resolution criterion $k_{\max}\eta\ge1.5$ is met: $k_{\max}\eta=2.31$ (case 1-T) and $4.25$ (case 2-T) (§II C, Table I). **Shown**
6. One detonation cell is accommodated in each lateral direction; the 1.92 mm cell width is "comparable enough" to the Caltech detonation database (§III A). **Stated**
7. Cell numbers and transverse-wave modes in three-dimensional simulations are sensitive to initial conditions, disturbances and resolution (refs 33–35); fixing equal cell numbers across cases isolates the turbulence effect (§III A). **Stated**
8. With turbulence, shock and flame fronts are corrugated at small scale, wave curvature intensifies, and many small unburned pockets appear in both mixtures (§III A, Fig. 4). **Shown**
9. Turbulence severely deteriorates the cell regularity of the regular mixture (case 1-T), whereas the irregular mixture (2-T) is barely changed: "the cell structure of an originally regular mixture is more vulnerable to turbulence" (§III B, Fig. 5). **Shown**
10. Even the most disturbed case keeps a general periodicity, so turbulence alone does not explain the absence of cellular patterns in rotating-detonation-engine optical measurements; more complicated disturbances, such as turbulence with non-uniform mixing, are suggested (§III B). **Stated**
11. Mean velocity ratios $D_{\mathrm{average}}/D_{\mathrm{CJ}}$ are 0.981 (1), 0.976 (1-T), 0.947 (2), 0.951 (2-T); the deficit is "almost the same regardless of the existence of turbulence" and consistent with experiments in Ar-diluted H2–O2 (ref 39) (§III C, Table I, Fig. 6). **Shown**
12. Turbulence turns the periodic velocity history of case 1 into random cycle-to-cycle amplitude fluctuation; in case 2 the history is already irregular and turbulence increases the variation within a cycle (§III C, Fig. 6). **Shown**
13. Space–time-averaged temperature and pressure profiles barely differ with and without turbulence; they are broader than ZND and the von Neumann peak is far milder, attributed to multidimensionality and shock-front variation, as in refs 12–14 (§III D, Fig. 7). **Shown**
14. The averaged profiles reach a slightly over-reacted equilibrium; reaction proceeds faster in case 2-T than in case 2, while cases 1 and 1-T are indistinguishable (§III D, Fig. 8). **Shown**
15. Jin et al. (refs 12–13) found the reaction delayed by turbulence, "different from the results of the present study for both mixtures"; the authors note the one-step chemistry of Jin et al. and that with the same mechanism the two mixtures already behave qualitatively differently (§III D). **Shown** (own result) / **Stated** (interpretation)
16. Post-shock Taylor microscales are about 25 µm ($\lambda_x$) and 50 µm ($\lambda_y$) in all cases; the anisotropy comes from shock compression and deceleration in the normal direction, as in inert shock–turbulence interaction (ref 40) (§IV A, Fig. 9). **Shown**
17. In and behind the flame region the Taylor scales expand in a fluctuating manner; convected turbulence suppresses the expansion, and $\lambda_x$ exceeds $\lambda_y$ there through longitudinal acceleration and heat-release expansion (§IV A, Fig. 9). **Shown**
18. The Taylor scales are smaller than the ZND induction length (124 µm) and the cell widths, so turbulence can deform shock and flame (§IV A). **Stated**
19. The post-shock turbulent velocity scale from $k$ is about 480 m/s (cases 1, 1-T) and 670 m/s (cases 2, 2-T), comparable to the post-shock longitudinal velocity (400–600 m/s); the contribution of convected turbulence is "quite minor", the post-shock turbulence being mainly detonation-originated (slip lines from triple points) (§IV A, Fig. 10). **Shown**
20. The eddy-turnover time, $\approx3\times10^{-8}$ to $1\times10^{-7}$ s, is smaller than or comparable to the ZND induction time of about $3\times10^{-7}$ s, so turbulence can influence the induction region (§IV A). **Derived**
21. The longitudinal turbulent H2 flux exceeds molecular diffusion by two orders of magnitude, is common to all cases and slightly decreased by convected turbulence; the lateral flux is of the order of molecular diffusion and is boosted by convected turbulence (§IV B, Fig. 11). **Shown**
22. Longitudinal and lateral turbulent heat fluxes exceed molecular conduction by several orders of magnitude; convected turbulence contributes less longitudinally and enhances lateral transfer (§IV B, Fig. 12). **Shown**
23. Joint PDFs of flame curvature $\kappa_{\mathrm{fl}}$ and heat-release rate $\dot Q$ (24 fields) show a broadened curvature distribution in 1-T but no clear correlation: the flame front is governed by shock-induced self-ignition regardless of convected turbulence (§IV B, Eqs. 2–3, Fig. 13). **Shown**
24. Post-shock temperature PDFs (shock at $p=0.15$ MPa, state four grid sizes downstream) are smoother with turbulence; case 1-T has more low temperatures, case 2-T more high temperatures (§IV C, Fig. 14). **Shown**
25. ZND predictions started from the DNS post-shock states give narrower induction-length PDFs than DNS but reproduce the most frequent values and, qualitatively, the increase of short induction lengths in 2-T (§IV C, Fig. 15). **Shown**
26. The mean DNS induction length is 0.321 mm (case 2) and 0.295 mm (case 2-T); the turbulence-induced change of the shock Mach number reduces the induction length, the main effect of turbulence being the "creation of different shock strength" (§IV C, Abstract, §V). **Shown**
27. The turbulent Mach number is expected to be an important indicator for the shock-front effect (§IV C). **Stated**
28. Cost: about 50 h per case on 28 800 cores of Fugaku (§II C). **Stated**

The inflow field is a decaying isotropic turbulence in a periodic 1.92 mm
cube on the same 4 µm grid, initialized with (Eq. 1)

$$E(k)=16\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\frac{u'^2_{\mathrm{rms}}}{k_e}\Big(\frac{k}{k_e}\Big)^4\exp\Big[-2\Big(\frac{k}{k_e}\Big)^2\Big],$$

with $k_e$ the wavenumber of maximum energy, and run until the
velocity-derivative skewness $S_u$ lies in $-0.6<S_u<-0.4$ (§II C).

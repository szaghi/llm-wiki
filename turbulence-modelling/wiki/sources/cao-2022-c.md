# Summary

Cao et al. (2022b) ask why [implicit LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md)
(iLES) beat explicit eddy-viscosity LES (eLES) on the same under-resolved
grids in their earlier compressible periodic-hill computations. The test bed
is the compressible [Taylor–Green vortex](/benchmarks/taylor-green-vortex.md)
at $M=0.1$, $Re=1600$, computed with the fourth-order two-stage
finite-volume [high-order gas-kinetic scheme](/methods/high-order-gas-kinetic-scheme.md)
on $256^3$ cells (and $128^3$, $384^3$), against a $1024^3$
[DNS](/definitions/direct-numerical-simulation.md) of the same scheme. The
eddy viscosity enters the BGK model by enlarging the collision time,
$\tau_{\mathrm{col}}+\tau_{\mathrm{col},t}=(\mu+\mu_t)/p$, with the static
[Smagorinsky](/methods/smagorinsky-model.md) ($C_s=0.1$),
[Vreman](/methods/vreman-model.md) and
[dynamic Smagorinsky](/methods/dynamic-procedure.md) (domain-averaged
coefficient, discrete top-hat test filter of twice the cell width) models; the
cell is the only filter.

The kinetic energy and the total dissipation rate are insensitive to the
model, but the enstrophy dissipation and the solenoidal dissipation are
closest to DNS for iLES; among eLES the dynamic model is best. An
[a priori](/definitions/a-priori-and-a-posteriori-testing.md) box filtering
of the DNS shows coexisting forward and backward
[subgrid energy transfer](/definitions/energy-backscatter.md), with an
ensemble production far smaller than the solenoidal dissipation. Following
Moser et al., the discrete momentum equation is written with a numerical
SGS stress next to the modelled one, and the ensemble total SGS dissipation
is estimated a posteriori as the total dissipation minus the physical one
(solenoidal, [bulk-viscous](/definitions/bulk-viscosity.md) and
[pressure-dilatation](/definitions/pressure-dilatation.md) parts). The static
models roughly double the iLES value, the dynamic model lies between. The
authors conclude that the scheme's [numerical dissipation](/properties/numerical-dissipation.md)
acts as a built-in SGS dissipation sufficient for this low-Reynolds-number
flow, that the extra eddy viscosity "pollutes" the resolved vorticity, and
that LES must account for the competition between numerical and modelled SGS
dissipation. The evidence is one transitional decaying flow and one scheme
family ([large-eddy simulation](/definitions/large-eddy-simulation.md)).

# Key claims

The source's $\varepsilon_1$, $\varepsilon_2$ are written $\varepsilon_s$,
$\varepsilon_d$ (registry); $\varepsilon_3=-\frac{1}{\rho_0V}\int p\,\partial_ku_k\,dV$
(page-local, $V$ the box volume), $E_k$ the volume-averaged kinetic energy
per $\rho_0$ (page-local), $\langle\cdot\rangle$ the volume ("ensemble")
average, $\Pi=-\tau_{ij}\tilde S_{ij}$ the registry SGS flux of the a priori
filtered DNS, and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sgs}}=\varepsilon(E_k)-(\varepsilon_s+\varepsilon_d+\varepsilon_3)$
the a posteriori ensemble total SGS dissipation with $\varepsilon(E_k)=-\mathrm dE_k/\mathrm dt$.

1. On the same under-resolved grids, iLES is closer to DNS than eLES for the compressible Taylor–Green vortex ($M=0.1$, $Re=1600$, $256^3$) in the enstrophy dissipation and in $\varepsilon_s$ (§IV A, Figs. 2–3). **Shown**
2. $E_k$ is insensitive to grid resolution and to iLES against eLES (§IV A, Fig. 1). **Shown**
3. Among eLES models the dynamic Smagorinsky model "reasonably outweighs" the static Smagorinsky and Vreman models (§IV A, Fig. 2). **Shown**
4. At $M=0.1$, $\varepsilon_s$ dominates the physical dissipation; $\varepsilon_d$ and $\varepsilon_3$ are negligible and similar for iLES and eLES (§IV A, Fig. 3). **Shown**
5. In the authors' periodic-hill computations ($M=0.2$, $Re_b=2800$; Ref. 7) eLES over-predicts the normalized Reynolds stresses while iLES agrees with DNS (§I). **Stated**
6. An eddy-viscosity model follows from an extended BGK equation with $\tau_{\mathrm{col}}+\tau_{\mathrm{col},t}=(\mu+\mu_t)/p$ via the Chapman–Enskog expansion (§III, Eqs. 7–8). **Stated** (from Ref. 31)
7. A priori filtered DNS (box filter coarsening $2^3$ cells into one) shows that forward and backward SGS kinetic-energy transfer coexist (positive and negative $\Pi$) (§IV B, Figs. 5–6). **Shown**
8. Backscatter can be modelled neither by iLES nor by eLES with positive static coefficients; the dynamic coefficient is usually clipped to non-negative values for stability and is then also deficient (§IV B). **Stated**
9. $E_k$ is of order $10^4$ to $10^2$ times $\langle k_{\mathrm{sgs}}\rangle$ during the evolution (Table I, abstract). **Shown**
10. $\varepsilon_s$ is "approximately 20 times larger" than the ensemble filtered SGS production $\langle\Pi\rangle$ (§IV B, Table I, abstract). **Shown** (see Printed slips)
11. On under-resolved grids the built-in numerical dissipation may exceed the filtered SGS production, so explicit modelled SGS dissipation is "not required under such circumstance" (§IV B). **Stated** (inferred from the a priori analysis)
12. The discrete momentum equation (Eq. 20) groups all discretization errors phenomenologically into a numerical SGS stress $\tau^{\mathrm{num}}_{ij}$ next to the modelled $\tau^{\mathrm{mod}}_{ij}$; the numerical error is grid-, scheme- and flow-dependent and very hard to quantify term by term in a finite-volume framework (§IV C). **Stated**
13. The pointwise total SGS dissipation is $-\tau^{\mathrm{mod}}_{ij}\tilde S_{ij}-\tau^{\mathrm{num}}_{ij}\tilde S_{ij}$, the modelled part vanishing in iLES and the numerical part usually ignored in eLES (§IV C, Eq. 21). **Derived**
14. $\varepsilon(E_k)$ is well predicted at all grid levels by iLES and eLES (§IV C, Fig. 7). **Shown**
15. $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sgs}}$ of static eLES is "approximately two times larger" than that of iLES because the eddy viscosity always adds positive modelled dissipation (§IV C, Fig. 8, Table II). **Shown** (see Printed slips)
16. The dynamic model gives a smaller $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sgs}}$ than static eLES but larger than iLES, its domain-averaged coefficient being always positive in this flow (§IV C, Fig. 8, Table II). **Shown**
17. The a posteriori $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sgs}}$ (Table II) is larger than the a priori $\langle\Pi\rangle$ (Table I) "except for the late decaying stage at $t=15$" (§IV C). **Shown** (see Printed slips)
18. The additional explicit models "pollute" the resolved turbulent structures, blurring the resolved vorticity (§IV C, §V, Fig. 2). **Shown** (interpretation of Figs. 2, 8)
19. The numerical dissipation of iLES can be treated as the built-in SGS dissipation, and explicit modelled SGS dissipation is not required in low-Reynolds-number turbulence with a fourth-order finite-volume scheme (§IV C, §V, abstract). **Shown** (one flow, one scheme)
20. On $128^3$ and $384^3$ grids iLES still outperforms eLES; all solutions approach DNS with refinement; static eLES on $384^3$ is only comparable with iLES on $256^3$; iLES keeps the smallest $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{sgs}}$ on each grid (Appendix B, Figs. 9–12). **Shown**
21. Reliable LES needs work on both numerical methods and subgrid models; eLES should not be regarded "as a holy grail in LES on unresolved grids without considering the numerical implementation" (§V). **Stated**
22. At higher Mach number, shocklets make the numerical dissipation depend on the shock-capturing scheme and eLES models need compressibility corrections (future work) (§V). **Stated**
23. The gas-kinetic scheme's accuracy, robustness and cost are comparable with widely used high-order finite-difference methods, with an advantage in supersonic turbulence from its robustness (§I, Refs. 5, 9). **Stated**

# Summary

Ruan et al. (2023) ask how the plasma turbulence inferred from the nonthermal
broadening of solar-flare EUV lines forms. The paper uses no subgrid model
and discusses neither numerical dissipation nor convergence; its run is
described on [compressible MHD turbulence](/results/compressible-mhd-turbulence.md)
in a stratified, reconnecting plasma.

The 3D resistive MHD simulation (MPI-AMRVAC) covers a $100\times100\times100$
Mm box with a $64^3$ base grid and five levels of
[adaptive mesh refinement](/methods/mesh-adaptation.md), an effective $1024^3$
with smallest cells below 100 km. It includes gravity, field-aligned thermal
conduction with the transition-region adaptive conduction method, optically
thin radiative losses and a static background heating. A two-stage
anomalous resistivity triggers reconnection in a vertical current sheet;
the downflows hit the flare arcade and form a termination shock and
reflected upflows. Synthetic SDO/AIA 131 Å images, GOES fluxes and Hinode/EIS
Fe XXIV 255 Å spectra are compared with observations.

The synthetic nonthermal-velocity map reproduces the observed distribution,
with a maximum above 200 km/s above the loop top near the termination shock.
The turbulence is generated by the Kelvin–Helmholtz instability in the shear
between downflows and reflected upflows, in planes perpendicular to the
local guide field, where magnetic tension cannot stabilize it. The
fluctuations propagate along the field to the chromospheric footpoints as
Alfvénic perturbations. The energy diagnostics assume that the turbulence
develops only perpendicular to the field (§3.5); the paper does not invoke
[critical balance](/properties/critical-balance.md). The turbulent kinetic and magnetic energies
are of similar size, and the turbulent energy is about 10% of the
time-integrated downflow kinetic energy. The Rayleigh–Taylor and
Richtmyer–Meshkov fingers reported by Shen et al. (2022)
([Rayleigh–Taylor mixing](/benchmarks/rayleigh-taylor-mixing.md)) appear
later and lower and do not produce large turbulent velocities in this run.

# Key claims

1. The 3D simulation reproduces the observed spatial distribution and characteristic velocity of flare turbulence: a wide distribution above the 131 Å loops with the maximum nonthermal velocity above the loop top (§3.2, Fig. 2; Abstract; §4). **Shown**
2. Maximum nonthermal velocities exceed 200 km s$^{-1}$ around the reconnection termination shock; Gaussian fits of the synthetic 255 Å spectra give 245 and 148 km s$^{-1}$ in two boxes (Abstract; §4; Fig. 2c, d). **Shown**
3. The spectral profiles of the high-nonthermal-velocity regions are "perfect Gaussian distributions", a distinctive feature of flare turbulence (§3.2, Fig. 2c, d). **Shown**
4. The high nonthermal velocity below the loops in the synthetic EIS map comes mainly from shock-related chromospheric evaporation, not turbulence (§3.2). **Stated**
5. The turbulence is produced by the Kelvin–Helmholtz instability: vortices appear near $Z\sim35''$, where the vertical velocity jumps by more than 1000 km s$^{-1}$ between $X\sim21''$ and $X\sim23''$ (§3.3, Fig. 3b–e). **Shown**
6. For flow parallel to the field the instability needs half the velocity jump to exceed the local Alfvén speed $v_A$ (Keppens et al. 1999); here the guide field is almost perpendicular to the plane of the shear, so vortices form without stabilizing tension, a condition "unconditionally unstable for KHI" (§3.3). **Stated** (criterion) / **Shown** (field geometry)
7. The growth-rate estimate $\sigma_{\mathrm{KH}}=kv_0$ (Goedbloed et al. 2019), with $1/k$ of several Mm and half shear speed $v_0$ of hundreds to 1000 km s$^{-1}$, gives growth times of seconds, the order of the vortex generation in the run (§3.3). **Derived**
8. The shear arises from the nonlinear interaction of reconnection downflows with the arcades: the collisions form a termination shock and reflected upflows whose distribution varies with $x$ (§3.3, Fig. 3f–i). **Shown**
9. The vortices form preferentially in planes perpendicular to the local magnetic field; the turbulence is "anisotropic due to magnetic tension" (§3.3, §4). **Shown**
10. Along the guiding field line the field is about 40 G in the legs and below 20 G at the apex; real flares can exceed 100 G above the loops, but the instability with flow perpendicular to the field remains hard to inhibit (§3.3). **Shown** / **Stated**
11. The Rayleigh–Taylor and Richtmyer–Meshkov fingers of Shen et al. (2022) appear in a later phase and below the region of maximum nonthermal velocity; at the upper border of the 131 Å loops the nonthermal velocity is below 50 km s$^{-1}$; the turbulent zone is "most likely driven by KHI" (§3.1, §3.3, Fig. 3a). **Shown**
12. Turbulent motions reach about 10 km s$^{-1}$ at $z=2$ Mm (mean $n_e\sim10^{11}$ cm$^{-3}$, $T\sim1$ MK) at the footpoints of the dense arcades, and an order of magnitude less at $z=1$ Mm ($n_e\sim10^{13}$ cm$^{-3}$) (§3.4, Fig. 4a). **Shown**
13. The low-atmosphere turbulence propagates down along the field rather than forming locally: $B_x$ and $u_x$ are in antiphase for structures moving towards $s>0$ and in phase towards $s<0$ (field pointing from $s<0$ to $s>0$), indicating Alfvénic perturbations; the fitted speeds (430 and 1600 km s$^{-1}$ in Fig. 4b) are close to the local Alfvén speed (§3.4, Fig. 4b–e). **Shown**
14. The turbulent kinetic and magnetic energy densities of Eqs. 7–8 have similar magnitude and distribution, and the kinetic one follows the magnetic one in time, "a feature of Alfvénic motions" (§3.5, Fig. 5). **Shown**
15. The total turbulent energy is about 10% of the time-integrated kinetic energy of the reconnection downflow at each time (Abstract: "more than 10%"); the cumulative transfer exceeds the instantaneous turbulent energy because the latter is carried to the chromosphere and diffused (§3.5, Fig. 5d). **Shown**
16. The maximum nonthermal velocity lies in a weak-field region (mean field below 25 G), proposed by Chen et al. (2020) and observed by Fleishman et al. (2022) as an electron-acceleration site (§3.2, Fig. 2b; §4). **Shown** / **Stated**
17. The model applies to any reconnecting flare regardless of size or duration because it rests on scale-invariant MHD; downflows stopped by lower-lying fields are diverted perpendicular to the field, making the shear orthogonal to it, an environment for the instability that two-dimensional simulations miss (§4). **Stated**
18. Resolution: smallest cells below 100 km, against 260 km in Shen et al. (2022) (§2). **Stated**
19. The transition-region adaptive conduction is not crucial for the impulsive-phase turbulence but matters for coronal rain in the gradual phase (§4). **Stated**

# Printed slips

- **§3.5, p. 9:** "The total time-integrated amount that is transferred from bulk kinetic energy of the reconnection downward flows to turbulence energy is more than the instantaneous turbulence energy Ek"; the preceding paragraph defines $E_k$ as the time-integrated downflow kinetic energy and $E_{tur}$ as the turbulence energy.
- **Eqs. 7–8, pp. 7–8:** the "kinetic energy density" is printed $\int\rho v_x^2dx-[\int\rho v_x dx]^2/\int\rho dx$ without a factor $\tfrac12$, and the "magnetic energy density" $\int B_x^2dx-[\int B_xdx]^2/\int dx$ without $\tfrac12$ or $1/(8\pi)$, while Fig. 5 gives both in erg cm$^{-2}$ and the field is quoted in gauss. The paper does not state the units of $B$ in Eq. 8, so the equipartition of claim 14 cannot be checked from the printed formulas.
- **In-text citations:** two different works are cited as "Zhou et al. 2021" without suffix, one for the Richtmyer–Meshkov instability (§3.1, Physica D) and one for the conduction method (§2, A&A).

# Summary

Bae & Koumoutsakos (2022) propose scientific multi-agent
[reinforcement learning](/methods/reinforcement-learning-closure.md)
(SciMARL) to build [machine-learning wall models](/methods/machine-learning-wall-model.md)
for [wall-modelled LES](/methods/wall-modelled-les.md). Agents sit on wall
grid points and share one policy. Each observes local flow quantities at the
matching height $h_{\mathrm{wm}}$ and acts by multiplying its local modelled
wall stress by a factor in $[0.9,1.1]$ at every time step. The reward
measures the step-to-step change of the error against the true mean wall
stress, plus a bonus when the error is below 1%. Training runs inside WMLES
of [turbulent channel flow](/benchmarks/turbulent-channel-flow.md) at
$Re_\tau\approx2000$, 4200 and 8000. The authors argue that this
[model-consistent training](/methods/model-consistent-training.md) lets the
policy absorb numerical and SGS errors, unlike supervised models trained on
single-step targets and judged by
[a priori testing](/definitions/a-priori-and-a-posteriori-testing.md).

Two models differ in their states. The velocity-based model (VWM) observes
the velocity, its wall-normal gradient and the height, scaled with the
modelled friction velocity. The log-law-based model (LLWM) observes the
instantaneous local [log-law](/definitions/law-of-the-wall.md) slope and
intercept built from the same quantities. A posteriori, in channels from
$Re_\tau=5200$ to $10^6$, the VWM works only while $h^+_{\mathrm{wm}}$ stays
in the trained range. The LLWM keeps the error below 5% at all tested
$h^+_{\mathrm{wm}}$, comparable to the
[equilibrium wall model](/methods/equilibrium-wall-model.md). The authors
assert this comparison; the preprint read does not plot the equilibrium
model for the channel. Without retraining, the LLWM predicts the skin
friction of a zero-pressure-gradient
[boundary layer](/benchmarks/turbulent-boundary-layer.md) for
$Re_\theta=1000$–7000. Its wall stress correlates with the off-wall velocity
at about 0.3, the DNS value the paper quotes; in the equilibrium model the
correlation is perfect.

Training costs $O(10^3)$ CPU-hours, against $O(10^7)$ for generating DNS
data for supervised learning. The solver is a staggered second-order
finite-difference code with the anisotropic minimum-dissipation SGS model.
The wall stress is imposed through a wall eddy viscosity, which the authors
state is better against [log-layer mismatch](/definitions/log-layer-mismatch.md)
than a Neumann condition. The introduction cites the
[grid-point scalings](/results/les-grid-point-requirements.md) of LES and
the taxonomy of wall modelling, including
[hybrid RANS/LES](/definitions/hybrid-rans-les.md) and the
[dynamic](/methods/dynamic-procedure.md) slip wall model.

# Key claims

1. Wall-resolved LES grid-point count scales as $O(Re^{13/7})$, and WMLES grid-point count scales "at most as $O(Re)$" (Introduction pp. 1–2, citing refs 4–6). **Stated**
2. Dynamic wall models (refs 21, 22) are robust to Reynolds number and grid but sensitive to the numerical method and the SGS model, which is attributed to near-wall numerical errors (ref 23). The methodology has only been used in structured, incompressible solvers (Introduction p. 2). **Stated**
3. Supervised-learning closures trained on single-step targets are not trained to compensate for systematic DNS–LES discrepancies and compounding errors. "Wall models are more sensitive than SGS models" (ref 22), so compounding errors are expected to be more harmful in WMLES (Introduction p. 3). **Stated**
4. In SciMARL the agents are the computational elements, and their actions compensate both for closure terms and for numerical errors of the solver. The resulting wall models are "robust with respect to the numerical discretizations, as these errors are taken into consideration in the training process" (Introduction p. 3). **Stated** (no test with another solver or SGS model in this paper)
5. States are non-dimensionalized with $\nu$ and the modelled friction velocity $u_{\tau,\mathrm{wm}}=(\tau^{\mathrm m}_w/\rho)^{1/2}$, not with the true $u_\tau$, so no prior knowledge of the flow is needed (Eq. 2, p. 4). **Stated** (design choice)
6. The VWM learns to raise or lower the wall stress according to whether the local pair (height, velocity), in modelled wall units, lies above or below the log law, without prior knowledge of the log-law coefficients (Fig. 2a, p. 5). **Shown**
7. The LLWM state-action map is likewise split, with most states centred on the true $1/\kappa$ and $B$ ($\kappa=0.41$, $B=5.2$). For states far from the origin, the classification above or below the log law depends on $h_{\mathrm{wm}}$ (Fig. 2b, p. 5). **Shown**
8. In channel flow the VWM performs as expected only while $h^+_{\mathrm{wm}}$ lies in the trained range $150<h^+_{\mathrm{wm}}<1200$. $Re_\tau=2\times10^4$ and $5\times10^4$ at $\Delta y=0.05\delta$ give high errors, which "decrease significantly" when the wall-normal grid is refined to bring $h^+_{\mathrm{wm}}$ back into range (Table 1, Fig. 3, p. 6). **Shown**
9. The LLWM's prediction error in the friction velocity is "less than 5%", and its mean velocity profiles align with the log law regardless of $h^+_{\mathrm{wm}}$, for $Re_\tau$ from 5200 to $10^6$ (Figs. 3–4, p. 6). **Shown**
10. These results are "comparable" to those of the equilibrium wall model (EQWM), which uses empirical coefficients tuned for this flow (pp. 6, 8). **Shown** (asserted: the EQWM channel results are not plotted in Figs. 3–4 of the preprint)
11. The LLWM, trained only in channel flow, predicts the skin-friction coefficient $C_f=\tau^{\mathrm m}_w/(\rho U_\infty^2/2)$ of a zero-pressure-gradient flat-plate boundary layer for $Re_\theta=1000$–7000 comparably to the empirical $C_f$ of Schlichting & Kestin (ref 47) (Fig. 5, pp. 6–8). **Shown**
12. In deterministic wall models such as the EQWM the wall stress is perfectly correlated with the velocity at the sampling location (refs 50, 51), against a correlation coefficient of 0.3 in DNS (ref 48) (p. 8). **Stated** (DNS value) / **Shown** for the EQWM (Figs. 6a, 7)
13. The LLWM gives a maximum correlation of about 0.3 between wall stress and off-wall streamwise velocity (at $h_{\mathrm{wm}}=0.1\delta$), matching the DNS value (Figs. 6b, 7, p. 8). **Shown**
14. Training the LLWM at $Re_\tau=4200$ costs $O(10^3)$ CPU-hours with less than 1 GB of storage, against $O(10^7)$ CPU-hours and more than 100 TB for DNS data for supervised learning (p. 8). **Stated** (no hardware given)
15. The RL models are trained in situ with WMLES and need no DNS data: the reward uses only the mean wall stress (p. 8). **Stated**. The reward still requires the true mean $\tau_w$ at each training $Re_\tau$ (Eq. 9); the paper does not say where these values come from.
16. The LLWM states (streamwise velocity, its wall-normal gradient, wall distance) "do not depend heavily on numerics or SGS models, unlike filtered velocities or eddy viscosity values required in the dynamic model" (p. 10). **Stated**
17. The framework extends to roughness, stratification and compressibility by adding a dimension to the state vector, because all flows with inner–outer scale separation exhibit a log law (ref 46); a single model could transition smoothly between roughness patches (pp. 10–11). **Stated** (not shown)
18. The LLWM generalizes to a broader set of grid resolutions and Reynolds numbers than the VWM (Discussion p. 10). **Shown** (Figs. 3–4)
19. The LLWM's wall-stress/velocity correlation "implies that the policy of the LLWM replicates the natural mechanisms of wall-shear stress control that can be obtained so far only through highly-resolved simulations" (Discussion pp. 10–11). **Stated** (interpretation of Fig. 7)
20. Evaluating $y$ in $1/\kappa_{\mathrm{wm}}$ at one of the two finite-difference grid points instead of their midpoint changes the wall stress by about 5% (Methods, Testing: channel flow, p. 14). **Shown** (stated result, no figure)
21. Imposing the wall stress through a wall eddy viscosity, $\nu_t|_w=(\partial_yu|_w)^{-1}\tau^{\mathrm m}_w/\rho-\nu$ (Eq. 10), is "better at resolving the so-called log-layer mismatch" than the "more widely used Neumann boundary condition" (Methods p. 13, ref 60). **Stated**
22. The anisotropic minimum-dissipation (AMD) SGS model "is known to perform well in highly anisotropic grids" (Methods p. 13, ref 59). **Stated**
23. Test runs span $300\delta/u_\tau$, against a training episode of $2\delta/u_\tau$ (Methods p. 14). **Stated** (setup)

The method, in registry notation. The policy $\pi_{\boldsymbol\theta}$ maximizes
$J=\mathbb E_\pi\sum_n\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}^nr_{n+1}$ with
$\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}=0.995$ (Eq. 1). The action is
$\tau^{\mathrm m}_w(x,z,t_{n+1})=a_n(x,z)\,\tau^{\mathrm m}_w(x,z,t_n)$,
$a_n\in[0.9,1.1]$. The LLWM states are
$1/\kappa_{\mathrm{wm}}=y^{\mathrm{wm}}\,\partial u^{\mathrm{wm}}/\partial y^{\mathrm{wm}}$
and $B_{\mathrm{wm}}=u^{\mathrm{wm}}-\kappa_{\mathrm{wm}}^{-1}\ln(h_{\mathrm{wm}}u_{\tau,\mathrm{wm}}/\nu)$,
evaluated at $h_{\mathrm{wm}}$ (Methods p. 13). Agents are spaced every four
grid points in $x$ and $z$. During training $h_{\mathrm{wm}}$ is drawn at
random in $[0.075\delta,0.15\delta]$ at each step, the initial wall stress
is perturbed by up to $\pm20\%$, and the grid is $0.05\delta$ in all
directions. Training uses V-RACER with Remember-and-Forget Experience Replay
in the smarties library (Eqs. 4–8). The policy network has two hidden
layers of 128 softsign units with skip connections, a batch of 512, a replay
memory of $10^6$, Adam with learning rate $10^{-5}$, and $10^7$
policy-gradient steps.

# Printed slips

- **Reward sign (Eq. 9, Methods p. 13):** printed $r_n=\big(|\tau_w-\tau^m_w(t_n)|-|\tau_w-\tau^m_w(t_{n-1})|\big)/\tau_w+\mathbb 1(\cdot)$. As printed, the first term is positive when the error grows from $t_{n-1}$ to $t_n$, while the text (pp. 4, 13) calls the reward "proportional to the improvement in the prediction".
- **$\beta$ update (Eq. 8, p. 12):** the "otherwise" branch prints $\beta\leftarrow\beta+(1-\eta)\beta$, i.e. $(2-\eta)\beta$, while Eq. 7 weights by $\beta$ and $1-\beta$. Whether $\eta$ here is the Adam learning rate of p. 13 is not said.
- **Eq. 7 (p. 12):** indices switch to $t$ ($\rho_t$, $a_t$, $s_t$) from the $n$ of Eqs. 4–6, and $g^D=\nabla_wD_{KL}(\pi_w(\cdot|s_t))\|\mathcal P(\cdot|\mu_t,\sigma_t)$ has a misplaced parenthesis.
- **Error metric (p. 6):** the text gives the LLWM "prediction error in the friction velocity is less than 5%", while the Fig. 3 caption reads "Error in time-averaged wall-shear stress".
- **Failing VWM cases (p. 6):** the text names only $Re_\tau=2\times10^4$ and $5\times10^4$ as outside the trained $h^+_{\mathrm{wm}}$ range. Table 1 also lists $Re_\tau=10^5$ and $10^6$ at $h^+_{\mathrm{wm}}=10^4$ and $10^5$, and the Fig. 4 caption omits "the two largest Reynolds number cases for VWM".
- **VWM states (Discussion p. 10):** "the state space comprises the streamwise velocity and its wall-normal derivatives", omitting the height listed as the third state on pp. 4 and 13.
- **Matching-height symbol (p. 8):** "sampling location $h_{wm}$", elsewhere $h_m$.
- **Text slips:** "SciMARL has only been used in fluid mechanics only recently" (p. 3, attached to ref 44, Novati et al.'s multi-agent RL for SGS models); "in the range from range from 5200 to $10^6$" (pp. 6, 14); "a reward function only based on the mean wall-shear stress rather." (p. 8, truncated); "initialized as in with small outer weights" (p. 12, missing citation); "recylcing" (p. 14).
- **Reference years as printed:** ref 44 "Nat. Mach. Intell. 3, 87–96 (2020)"; ref 54 (Kingma & Ba) "Proc. 3rd ICLR (2014)".

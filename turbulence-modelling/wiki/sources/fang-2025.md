# Summary

Fang et al. (2025) add a magnetohydrodynamics module to
[Xcompact3d](/software/xcompact3d.md), the open-source Fortran framework
that solves the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations with sixth-order
[compact finite differences](/methods/compact-finite-difference-scheme.md)
on Cartesian meshes (stretching allowed in $y$), a direct FFT-based
Poisson solver on a half-staggered pressure mesh and a 2D pencil
decomposition. Two systems are offered: an induction solver for finite
$Rm$, which advances the induction equation in skew-symmetric form (as is
the momentum convection, to reduce aliasing; see
[kinetic-energy preservation](/properties/kinetic-energy-preservation.md))
and removes the magnetic divergence by solving a Poisson equation for a
"magnetic pressure" and subtracting its gradient, a step the authors
attribute to Dedner et al.'s divergence cleaning; and a
quasi-static potential solver for $Rm\ll1$ with an imposed field, which
solves a Poisson equation for the electric potential. The paper is a
[DNS](/definitions/direct-numerical-simulation.md) code paper: no
turbulence model is involved.

Validation covers the 2D and 3D Orszag–Tang vortices
([decaying MHD turbulence](/results/decaying-mhd-turbulence.md)), where
peak enstrophies and the peak current converge at lower resolution than
the finite-difference and [lattice Boltzmann](/methods/lattice-boltzmann-method.md)
references; laminar Hartmann flow against the analytical profile; and a
[turbulent channel](/benchmarks/turbulent-channel-flow.md) at
$Re_\tau=150$ with a wall-normal field, where the log layer of the
[law of the wall](/definitions/law-of-the-wall.md) is lifted, the
Reynolds stresses fall with the Hartmann number and the flow
relaminarizes at $Ha=8$. On ARCHER2 the potential and induction solvers
cost about 1.5 and 2.6 times the Navier–Stokes solver, and strong scaling
to 32,768 cores is unchanged. Finally the paper proposes an MHD variant of
the [Taylor–Green vortex](/benchmarks/taylor-green-vortex.md): the
classical velocity with a uniform imposed field normal to it and zero
initial induced field. At $Re=100$ kinetic and magnetic energy exchange
almost completely in an oscillation of frequency $1/\pi$; at $Re=7200$
the oscillation decays and the enstrophy peak is delayed to $t\approx32$.
The [isotropic-turbulence](/benchmarks/homogeneous-isotropic-turbulence.md)
scaling is said not to hold in MHD turbulence, which motivates DNS; the
[small-scale dynamo](/results/small-scale-dynamo.md) is named only as a
future application.

# Key claims

Page-local symbols: $N_{\mathrm{St}}=\sigma B_0^2L/(\rho_0U)$ the Stuart
number (interaction parameter); $Ha$ the Hartmann number; $\boldsymbol J$
the current density; $\psi_B$ the "magnetic pressure" of the projection;
$\varphi_e$ the electric potential; $E_k$, $E_m$ the volume-averaged
kinetic and magnetic energies; $\mathcal Z_m=\tfrac12\langle\boldsymbol J\cdot\boldsymbol J\rangle$
the magnetic enstrophy (with $\mathcal Z$ the kinetic one);
$J_{\max}=\max\lvert\boldsymbol J\rvert$. Magnetic fields are
non-dimensional, not in Alfvénic units.

1. The module solves either the induction system (Eqs. 1–4, 6) for relatively high $Rm$ or the electric-potential system (Eqs. 1, 3, 7, 8) for $Rm\ll1$ with $\boldsymbol B=\boldsymbol B_0$; the potential system is cheaper and avoids the stiffness of the induction equation at very low $Rm$ (§2.1). **Stated**
2. The convective terms of the momentum and induction equations are written in skew-symmetric form to reduce aliasing errors (§2.1, Eqs. 1, 2, 5). **Stated**
3. The field is made divergence-free by solving $\nabla^2\psi_B=\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol B^*$ (Eq. 12) and projecting $\boldsymbol B=\boldsymbol B^*-\nabla\psi_B$ (Eq. 13), attributed to "the divergence cleaning technique proposed by Dedner et al. [16]" (§2.2). **Stated**
4. With the modified-wavenumber concept the velocity divergence is zero to machine accuracy (§2.3). **Stated**
5. After the magnetic correction step the magnetic divergence of the 2D Orszag–Tang vortex at $t=0.6$ is a random pattern "with noises of order $10^{-13}$"; before correction it is large around current sheets (§3.1, Fig. 4). **Shown**
6. 2D Orszag–Tang: $E_k$ is minimum at $t=1.2$, $E_m$ maximum at $t=1.0$, the total energy decays monotonically, and the energies agree with Orszag & Tang even on $32^2$ (§3.1, Fig. 5a). **Shown**
7. 2D Orszag–Tang: $\mathcal Z_m$ is mesh independent above $128^2$, with its peak near $t=1.0$ (§3.1, Fig. 5b). **Shown**
8. 2D Orszag–Tang global maxima $\mathcal Z/\mathcal Z_m$ (Table 3), Xcompact3d at $t=0.5$: 22.61/15.53 ($32^2$), 22.43/17.71 ($64^2$), 22.84/18.22 ($128^2$), 22.84/18.26 ($256^2$), 22.85/18.26 ($512^2$); at $t=1.0$: 51.48/33.83, 88.26/43.42, 100.93/46.44, 101.06/46.69, 101.06/46.70. Reference labelled "Dellar (2002)": $t=0.5$ 22.74/17.96 ($128^2$), 22.81/18.20 ($256^2$), 22.84/18.24 ($512^2$); $t=1.0$ 98.98/45.13, 100.25/46.30, 100.82/46.59. De Rosis et al. (2021): $t=0.5$ 21.95/16.88, 22.59/17.88, 22.77/18.15, 22.82/18.22 ($1024^2$); $t=1.0$ 76.26/39.62, 93.02/44.53, 98.42/46.09, 100.39/46.49. The authors conclude that Xcompact3d converges at lower resolution than the references (§3.1). **Shown**
9. 3D Orszag–Tang: the energies collapse for all meshes; the kinetic-energy error relative to $256^3$ is below 4% on the coarsest mesh and about $10^{-4}$ at $128^3$ (§3.2, Fig. 6). **Shown**
10. 3D Orszag–Tang: enstrophy errors are an order of magnitude larger than energy errors, from about $10^{-1}$ ($32^3$) to $10^{-3}$ ($128^3$) (§3.2, Fig. 7). **Shown**
11. 3D Orszag–Tang $J_{\max}$: 19.9230, 22.4334, 22.7469, 22.7571 for $32^3$–$256^3$, against 22.7439 from a pseudo-spectral solver at $128^3$; the $64^3$ result is "still superior" to the lattice Boltzmann data of De Rosis et al. at $128^3$ (§3.2, Fig. 8). **Shown**
12. Laminar Hartmann flow (potential solver): at fixed bulk velocity the centreline velocity drops and the skin friction rises with the wall-normal field; the profiles at $Ha=1,5,10$ agree "perfectly" with the analytical solution, Eq. (22) (§3.3, Figs. 10–11). **Shown**
13. Channel without field ($Re_\tau=150$): the mean velocity agrees with the law of the wall, Eckelmann's experiment and Moser et al.; Reynolds-stress differences with Moser et al. ($Re_\tau=178$ as printed) are attributed to the Reynolds-number difference and disappear with inner or outer scaling (§3.4, Fig. 12). **Shown**
14. With a wall-normal field the log layer is lifted, as in drag-reducing flows, and shrinks with increasing $Ha$; Xcompact3d predicts a slightly higher log layer than the THTLab data in wall units but agrees in bulk scaling (§3.4, Fig. 13). **Shown**
15. The Reynolds stresses decrease with $Ha$ and agree with THTLab at $Ha=4$ and 6 (THTLab slightly higher); at $Ha=8$ all Reynolds stresses vanish and the flow relaminarizes onto the laminar profile (§3.4, Figs. 13–15). **Shown**
16. Cost per time step on ARCHER2 at 262,144 nodes per core (Table 7), NS / NS+potential / NS+induction: 0.54/0.92/1.43 s (8 cores, $128^3$), 1.44/2.31/3.0 (64, $256^3$), 2.15/3.23/5.72 (512, $512^3$), 2.41/3.56/6.33 (4096, $1024^3$), averaged over 50 steps (§3.5). **Shown**
17. NS+potential costs about 1.5 times and NS+induction about 2.6 times the NS solver; the induction solver adds three transport equations and one Poisson equation, the potential solver one Poisson equation (§3.5, Fig. 16b; Conclusions). **Shown**
18. Strong scaling to 32,768 cores: excellent up to 1,024 cores for $256^3$ and up to 16,384 cores for $1024^3$; the MHD solvers "do not alter the strong scaling of Xcompact3d" (§3.5, Fig. 17). **Shown**
19. New benchmark: MHD Taylor–Green vortex with the classical velocity, imposed uniform field $\boldsymbol B_0=(0,0,1)$ and zero initial induced field $\boldsymbol b$, $Re=Rm=N_{\mathrm{St}}$ ($Pm=1$), cases 100 ($64^3$) and 7200 ($512^3$) (§4, Eqs. 23–25, Table 8). **Stated** (contribution)
20. At $Re=100$ kinetic and magnetic energy oscillate half a period out of phase with almost all energy exchanged each period, observed frequency $1/\pi$; the total energy decays monotonically but much more slowly than in the hydrodynamic vortex, and the hydrodynamic dissipation peak near $t\approx5$ is absent (§4.1, Fig. 18). **Shown**
21. Field-line stretching converts kinetic into magnetic energy and the Lorentz force converts it back; the imposed field acts "like a converter" (§4.1). **Stated**
22. At $Re=7200$ the energy still oscillates with decreasing amplitude, especially after $t=30$, when the total-energy decay rate increases suddenly; the kinetic and magnetic enstrophy peak near $t=32$, much later than the hydrodynamic vortex at $Re=1600$ ($256^3$), and decay more slowly after it (§4.2, Fig. 22). **Shown**
23. At $t=50$ the $Re=7200$ fields are anisotropic and elongated in $z$, with symmetric vorticity and current patterns in $x$–$y$ planes, which the authors take as evidence that numerical errors are not amplified (§4.2, Fig. 23). **Shown**
24. $Q$-criterion structures are $z$-aligned tubes and magnetic structures (second invariant of the magnetic-gradient tensor) are feather-like sheets, unlike the randomly oriented worms of the hydrodynamic vortex (§4.2, Figs. 24–25). **Shown**
25. The Kolmogorov scaling of hydrodynamic isotropic turbulence does not hold for MHD turbulence, so conventional turbulence models mispredict under-resolved MHD motion (§1, refs [1–4]). **Stated**

The equations in registry notation (non-dimensional, $p$ kinematic):
$\partial_t\boldsymbol u=-\tfrac12[(\boldsymbol u\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol u+\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol u\otimes\boldsymbol u)]-\nabla p+Re^{-1}\nabla^2\boldsymbol u+N_{\mathrm{St}}\,\boldsymbol J\times\boldsymbol B$
(Eq. 1), the skew-symmetric induction equation with $Rm^{-1}\nabla^2\boldsymbol B$
(Eq. 2), $\boldsymbol J=Rm^{-1}\nabla\times\boldsymbol B$ (Eq. 6); quasi-static
form $\nabla^2\varphi_e=\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol u\times\boldsymbol B_0)$,
$\boldsymbol J=-\nabla\varphi_e+\boldsymbol u\times\boldsymbol B_0$ (Eqs. 7–8).
The induction loop advances $\boldsymbol B^*$, projects the velocity with
the pressure Poisson equation, then projects $\boldsymbol B^*$ with
Eqs. (12)–(13) and adds the Lorentz force. The sixth-order compact
staggered derivative (Eq. 15) and midpoint interpolation (Eq. 16) feed the
spectral Poisson solvers on the half-staggered mesh; time integration is
third-order Runge–Kutta in all benchmarks.

# Printed slips

- **§3.1:** "reference data from previous DNS of Orszag and Tang [5]", while [5] is printed as Orszag (1969), "Numerical methods for the simulation of turbulence"; Orszag & Tang (1979) is [22].
- **§3.1, Table 3:** "the DNS of Dellar [16]", "Dellar (2002) [16]", while [16] is printed as Dedner et al. (2002); no Dellar paper is in the list.
- **§1:** "the classical Kolmogorov K41 scaling [1]", while [1] is printed as Kolmogorov's 1962 refinement paper.
- **§3.4:** parameters chosen to compare with "Nishimura and Kasagi [29]", while [29] is printed as Kasagi & Nishimura (1997), a combined forced and natural convection channel paper; the MHD data of Figs. 13–14 are THTLab [7].
- **Eq. (21):** $Ha=B_0L\sqrt{\sigma\rho_0/\nu}=\sqrt{Re\,N}$. *(Compiler check, not in the sources: from the printed $Re=UL/\nu$ and $N=\sigma B_0^2L/(\rho_0U)$, $\sqrt{Re\,N}=B_0L\sqrt{\sigma/(\rho_0\nu)}$, with $\rho_0$ in the denominator.)*
- **§3.3:** the friction velocity is printed $u_\tau=\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{Re}\frac{\partial u_x}{\partial y}}$, with both $\nu$ and $1/Re$; the bulk velocity is printed $u_b=\frac12\int_0^2u_x\,dx$ for an integral across the channel in $y$.
- **§4:** the alternate-insulating field is printed $\boldsymbol B^A=B_0(\cos2x\sin2y\sin2z,\ \cos2x\sin2y\sin2z,\ 0)$ with identical first two components. *(Compiler check, not in the sources: as printed $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol B^A=-2\sin2x\sin2y\sin2z+2\cos2x\cos2y\sin2z\ne0$.)*
- **§4:** Vahala et al.'s field, introduced as "the usual initial magnetic field from the OTV flow", is printed $B_x=-2\sin(2y/L)\sin(z/L)$, $B_y=2\sin(x/L)+\sin(z/L)$, while the paper's own 3D Orszag–Tang field, Eq. (20), has $-2\sin y+\sin z$ and $2\sin2x+\sin z$.
- **§3.1:** "magnetic energy, $E_n$", while Eq. (18) and the figures write $E_m$; meshes "ranging from $32^2$ to $256^2$", while Table 3 also lists $512^2$.
- **§3.2:** "the simulation with $32^2$ mesh nodes" in the 3D case, whose meshes are $32^3$–$256^3$.
- **§4.1:** "the kinetic and magnetic energy and entropy are defined by replacing $\vec B$ with $\vec b$" ("entropy" for enstrophy).
- **Typos:** "with only 8 mesh uniformly distributed", "away from the way" (§3.3), "the bew MHD module" (§3.5), "In-dept study" (§4); Hartmann flow "first analysed by Hartmann and Lazarus" with [27] printed "J. Hartman".
- **References:** [37] prints the author "J.H.F.S. Jeong" and "332 (185) (1997)"; [41] prints Taylor & Green with the year 1997 (Proc. R. Soc. Lond. A 158 (895)).

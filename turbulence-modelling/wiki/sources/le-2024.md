# Summary

Le et al. (2024) review artificial-intelligence methods for supersonic flow
and combustion, framed by the scramjet. The paper contributes no method or
computation; every result is quoted from the cited works. Section 2 is a tutorial on the
algorithms: Kriging, radial-basis-function and support-vector surrogates,
neural networks, unsupervised learning and dynamic mode decomposition,
reinforcement learning (applied to vehicle control, not to turbulence
closure), active subspaces, convolutional networks,
[physics-informed neural networks](/methods/physics-informed-neural-network.md)
and their variants, and swarm and genetic optimisers.

The turbulence-modelling content is §3.1, about four pages. It groups
machine learning for RANS into three uses. The first is uncertainty
analysis and [Bayesian calibration](/methods/bayesian-closure-calibration.md)
of the coefficients of the [Spalart–Allmaras](/methods/spalart-allmaras-model.md),
[$k$-$\omega$](/methods/k-omega-model.md) and [SST](/methods/k-omega-sst-model.md)
models, including calibrations for [shock/boundary-layer interactions](/benchmarks/shock-turbulent-boundary-layer-interaction.md)
and supersonic cavities; it bears on [model-form uncertainty](/definitions/model-form-uncertainty.md).
The second is model correction: [field inversion and machine learning](/methods/field-inversion-machine-learning.md)
with the multiplier $\beta(\boldsymbol x)$ on the SA production,
Reynolds-stress discrepancy learning, and ensemble-based inversion
([ensemble Kalman](/methods/ensemble-kalman-closure-calibration.md)-type
DAFI). The third is replacement of the RANS model by a neural-network map
from flow features to the eddy viscosity, trained on
[NACA 0012](/benchmarks/naca-0012-airfoil.md) cases and tested on the
[RAE 2822](/benchmarks/rae-2822-airfoil.md).

Section 3.3 reviews learned combustion closures: filtered density
functions, scalar dissipation, a [neural-network](/methods/neural-network-sgs-model.md)
closure of the LES stress in a premixed flame, a
[gene-expression programming](/methods/symbolic-regression-closure.md)
subgrid stress, and subgrid flame-surface density. It also covers chemistry
acceleration and [flamelet tabulation](/methods/flamelet-tabulated-chemistry.md).
Section 4 reviews flow-field reconstruction and image
[super-resolution](/methods/super-resolution-sgs-model.md) (not an LES
closure), and §5 design optimisation. The conclusions ask for "an adaptive
unified turbulence model" for internal high-speed flows. The review calls
[DNS](/definitions/direct-numerical-simulation.md) and
[LES](/definitions/large-eddy-simulation.md) unsuitable for engineering and
names [DES](/methods/detached-eddy-simulation.md) and RANS as the
engineering choices. Several attributions are mis-cited (see Printed slips).

# Key claims

1. Turbulence simulation methods are classed as DNS, LES, DES and RANS; "Due to the complexity and high computational costs associated with DNS and LES, they are not suitable to use in engineering applications [122,123]", while DES and RANS are the engineering research hotspot (§3, p. 9). **Stated**
2. Chemistry source-term evaluation accounts for "over 85 % of the total computational cost of numerical simulations [125,126]" of engine combustion (§3, p. 9). **Stated**
3. Machine learning serves RANS modelling in three ways: analysis of turbulence-model uncertainties, optimisation and improvement of turbulence models, and direct substitution for them (§3.1, p. 10, Table 3). **Stated**
4. Developer-recommended coefficients ("nominal values") are calibrated on simple flows, and "There is currently no evidence suggesting that nominal values perform well across all types of flows" (§3.1.1, p. 10). **Stated**
5. Tang et al. (ref. [140]): after Bayesian calibration of SST parameters for compression-corner interactions, near-wall turbulence is enhanced through a larger eddy viscosity and adverse-pressure-gradient predictions improve, but SST "cannot simulate the inherent shock instability" by modifying parameters, and the wall pressure in the separation region "remains too high" (§3.1.1, p. 10, Fig. 8). **Stated**
6. Hosder et al. (ref. [148]) needed $\mathcal O(10^5\sim10^6)$ MCMC samples to calibrate SA and SST; Sargsyan et al. (ref. [149]) note the maximum a posteriori value can be found by standard optimisation without MCMC (§3.1.1, p. 10). **Stated**
7. Yang et al. (ref. [150], the authors' group): polynomial chaos, Bayesian inference and particle-swarm optimisation recalibrate SST coefficients for supersonic cavities and hollow cylinders; the maximum relative wall-pressure error drops "from 29.71 % to 9.00 %" and the average "from 9.86 % to 3.67 %", and the MAP values beat the nominal ones on three criteria (§3.1.1, p. 10). **Stated**
8. SST parameters calibrated on a 24° compression corner and applied to 20°, 16° and 8° corners and to $M=2.9$ with other inflow boundary-layer thicknesses give wall pressure, skin friction and velocity profiles close to experiment, "confirming the turbulence model parameters' applicability within a narrow range of Mach numbers" (§3.1.1, p. 11, Fig. 9). **Stated**
9. Machine learning improves RANS by (i) a corrective multiplier or source term in the model equations, inferred from data and learned from flow features, or (ii) a learned bias added to the RANS Reynolds stress with the equations unchanged (§3.1.2, p. 12). **Stated**
10. Singh et al. (ref. [152]) multiply the SA production by $\beta(\boldsymbol x)$ (Eq. 18), invert it from experimental data by the adjoint method and learn it from flow variables, which "enhances the model's transferability and ensures the convergence of the original model" (§3.1.2, p. 12). **Stated**
11. Physics-informed learning of Reynolds-stress discrepancies (refs. [153, 154]) improves RANS when training and prediction flows align, but "even for similar flow phenomena, such as separated flows, correcting the model in regions with geometric changes can degrade the computational results of the original RANS model" (§3.1.2, p. 12). **Stated**
12. Heo et al. (ref. [155]): FIML-corrected SA reduces the eddy viscosity in the separation region of a supersonic axisymmetric base flow and improves the expansion and the size of the vortex region (§3.1.2, p. 13, Fig. 11). **Stated**
13. Tang et al. (ref. [170]) invert Reynolds-stress discrepancies of SST for shock/boundary-layer interaction with Ströfer's DAFI framework (ref. [171]), overcoming "some limitations of the eddy viscosity assumption" (§3.1.2, p. 13, Fig. 12). **Stated**
14. Neural-network surrogates replacing RANS (refs. [172–176]): a fully connected network trained on six NACA 0012 cases generalises across free-stream conditions and shapes; a radial-basis-function network gives a black-box algebraic eddy-viscosity model coupled to the solver, with accuracy "comparable to that of the SA model but exhibited higher computational efficiency" on three subsonic NACA 0012 cases (§3.1.3, pp. 14–15, Fig. 13). **Stated**
15. Surrogate turbulence models face stringent accuracy, smoothness and stability requirements: boundary-layer scale effects at high Reynolds number create "edge regions" with outliers, and solver coupling can cause instability and residual oscillations (§3.1.3, p. 15). **Stated**
16. Table 3: uncertainty analysis quantifies model error at "Excessive cost"; model improvement "Requires extensive high-quality data for validation; lacks universality"; an AI substitute "Demands substantial high-quality data; the 'black box' characteristic of AI models limits interpretability; potential for overfitting" (p. 15). **Stated**
17. Chemistry acceleration figures quoted from the cited works: 2.35-fold for an exponential integrator with Krylov approximation; one to three orders of magnitude for kernel PCA plus ANN; acceleration 9.13 with a residual network for H$_2$ ignition (11 species, 29 reactions); about two orders of magnitude for a self-organising-map network; acceleration 12 in LES of Sandia flames D, E and F with multiple perceptrons (§3.2–3.3, pp. 15–20). **Stated**
18. Chen et al. (ref. [10]): a deep network predicts the joint filtered density function of mixture fraction and progress variable in MILD combustion that "deviates significantly" from presumed shapes, so presumed FDFs "cannot be suitable" for some configurations (§3.3(1), p. 20). **Stated**
19. Nikolaou et al. (ref. [226]): a neural network models the unclosed LES stress in a turbulent premixed V-flame (six components from normalised density and velocity gradients) with "superior adaptability to traditional subgrid-scale models, such as the Smagorinsky model" (§3.3(4), p. 20). **Stated**
20. Schoepplein et al. (ref. [227]): gene-expression programming optimises the scalar coefficients of basis functions for subgrid stresses in premixed-flame LES (§3.3(4), p. 20). **Stated**
21. Lapeyre et al. (ref. [228]) and Ren et al. (ref. [229]): convolutional networks trained on DNS predict subgrid flame-surface density and wrinkling better than algebraic models, Ren et al. at high Karlovitz number and larger filters (§3.3(5), p. 20). **Stated**
22. Liu et al. (ref. [231]): a PINN reconstructs three-dimensional turbulent premixed flames from synthetic sparse data with three sub-networks (momentum, species, temperature); its effectiveness depends on dataset size, noise and turbulence–flame interaction (§3.3, pp. 20–21, Fig. 17). **Stated**
23. Fukami et al. (ref. [263]): "traditional super-resolution methods based on filtering operations possess low-pass characteristics, which are unsuitable for high-frequency issues such as turbulence" (§4.2, p. 25). **Stated**
24. Liu et al. (ref. [17]): a multi-temporal-path network fed a time series of velocity fields recovers more unresolved detail than a snapshot network (§4.2, p. 25, Table 7). **Stated**
25. Purely data-driven reconstructions of scramjet combustor fields reproduce the wave system but blur the turbulent region: mean linear correlation 0.952 with "still a strong blurring phenomenon in the turbulent area" (ref. [16]); a segmentation method "completely overlooks the turbulent region" (ref. [250]) (§4.1.3, pp. 23–24). **Stated**
26. FRDN (ref. [256]): average PSNR 31.57 dB, SSIM 0.939, "the mean square error measured 0.979, and the correlation coefficient was recorded at 0.0013" (§4.1.3, p. 25). **Stated**
27. PINN computational and memory costs "increasing exponentially with grid resolution" motivate separable PINNs (ref. [97]); self-adaptive PINNs (ref. [96]) reach lower $L_2$ error with fewer training cycles (§2.2.3, p. 7). **Stated**
28. Machine learning for turbulence-model UQ, parameter identification and surrogates focuses "mainly on low Mach number and outflow conditions"; future work should develop "an adaptive unified turbulence model" for shock/boundary-layer interactions, transverse jets and fuel mixing in internal flows of air-breathing high-speed propulsion (§6(1), p. 36). **Stated**
29. Wall-pressure-based reconstruction captures the key wave system but needs improvement in turbulent and interaction regions, and current work overlooks "turbulence model parameters" and chemistry (§6(3), pp. 36–37). **Stated**

In registry symbols, the corrected SA equation of Eqs. (17)–(18) is
$D\tilde\nu/Dt=\beta(\boldsymbol x)P_{\tilde\nu}-D_{\tilde\nu}+T_{\tilde\nu}$, with
production, destruction and transport terms $P_{\tilde\nu}$, $D_{\tilde\nu}$,
$T_{\tilde\nu}$ (page-local) of $\tilde\nu$ and the mean velocity; no SA
constants, functions or feature sets are printed. The filtered combustion
source term is $\tilde{\dot w}=\int\dot w(Z,c)\tilde P(Z,c)\,dZ\,dc$ (Eq. 19).

# Summary

Dang et al. (2022) present [OpenCFD-SCU](/software/opencfd-scu.md), an
open-source finite-difference solver for
[DNS](/definitions/direct-numerical-simulation.md) of compressible
wall-bounded turbulence, ported from the authors' Fortran CPU code
OpenCFD-SC to GPUs in CUDA-C and translated with HIPIFY so that it runs on
NVIDIA and AMD GPUs. It solves the compressible Navier–Stokes equations on
a single-block curvilinear grid with Steger–Warming flux-vector splitting,
optional characteristic reconstruction, a library of upwind schemes
(second-order NND, fifth- and seventh-order [WENO](/methods/weno-scheme.md),
OMP6, the bandwidth-optimized WENO-SYMBO, a seventh-order linear upwind
scheme) and a hybrid scheme that switches between the linear upwind scheme
and seventh- and fifth-order WENO by a modified Jameson pressure sensor, to
keep [numerical dissipation](/properties/numerical-dissipation.md) low away
from shocks. Viscous terms use sixth- or eighth-order central differences,
time integration third-order Runge–Kutta.

Most of the paper describes the parallel and GPU implementation: 3-D MPI
decomposition with host-staged halo exchange, an interior/outer split that
overlaps computation and communication on four streams, coalesced access,
warp shuffle with redundant block-edge fluxes, shared-memory transposes for
the $y$ and $z$ sweeps, MPI-IO. Performance claims include a GPU speed-up of
200–500 times over the CPU code at equal process count, 512 GPUs faster
than 131,712 CPU cores, 98.7% weak-scaling efficiency at 24,576 GPUs with
$280^3$ points each, and an estimated 17.1 PFLOPS. Validation is a Mach
2.9, 24° [compression ramp](/benchmarks/shock-turbulent-boundary-layer-interaction.md)
against the DNS of Martín et al. and the experiment of Bookey et al. (wall
pressure, van Driest [transformed](/definitions/compressible-velocity-transformation.md)
mean velocity of the incoming [boundary layer](/benchmarks/turbulent-boundary-layer.md)),
followed by a 31.2-billion-point wide-span run showing Taylor–Görtler
vortices behind the separation shock. No turbulence model is involved.

# Key claims

1. OpenCFD-SCU accelerates "by a factor of more than 200" over the CPU code with the same algorithm and number of MPI processes; §5 states "the GPU program is 200–500 times faster than the CPU program" (Abstract, §1, §5, Table 5, Fig. 19). **Shown** (Table 5 has no process count common to both codes; Fig. 19 plots the acceleration against the smallest CPU run)
2. "The running speed of OpenCFD-SCU with just 512 GPUs exceed[s] that of CPU software with 130 000 CPUs": 0.1387 s per step on 512 GPUs against 0.39 s on 131,712 CPU processes (Abstract, §5.2, Table 5). **Shown**
3. Weak scaling with $280^3$ points per process from 1 to 24,576 processes: 0.791 s per step on 1 and 0.78 s on 24,576; parallel efficiency 98.7% at 24,576 GPUs, more than 500 billion points (Abstract, §5.3, Fig. 20). **Shown**
4. Strong scaling "maintains 60% parallel efficiency when the number of processes is increased by 16 times" (§5; Table 5, 128 to 2048 processes, 0.5174 to 0.0627 s per step). **Shown** (see Printed slips)
5. Single-GPU double-precision throughput is estimated at 705.4 GFLOPS from manual operation counts (Table 6, 792 ms per step); the count is a lower bound since library functions such as SQRT take several operations; ROCPROF counts 388 vector instructions for the WENO-SYMBO kernel against 262 manually counted operations (§5.4, Table 6). **Shown**
6. At full scale the code reaches 17.1 PFLOPS in double precision, 11.4% of the GPU peak of the nodes, growing linearly with process count (§5, §5.4, Fig. 21). **Shown**
7. CPU and GPU codes agree: on a $250\times240\times90$ flat-plate case run 1000 steps without random disturbance and written in f16.9 format, 56 of 5.4 million output lines differ, only in the sign (§5.1). **Shown**
8. On a $200^3$ single-GPU case the times per step are V100 4.702 s, RTX3090 4.038 s, A100 3.036 s and AMD MI60 0.315 s; the AMD GPU is "nearly ten times faster" than the A100, attributed to AMD-targeted optimization (§5.5, Table 7). **Shown** (attribution **Stated**)
9. The inviscid upwind differencing takes 70% of the run time and the code is memory-bound, so optimization targets global-memory access (§4.3, Fig. 10). **Shown**
10. Coalesced access and shared-memory thread rearrangement make the $y$ and $z$ WENO-SYMBO kernels as fast as the $x$ kernel; total run time drops by 23.4% and the $z$ kernel by 60% on a $280^3$ grid (§4.3.3, Fig. 14). **Shown**
11. Four streams reduce one Runge–Kutta step from 291 ms to 265 ms despite per-kernel slow-down under concurrent load (§4.3.6, Fig. 15). **Shown**
12. Hoisting divisions out of the seventh-order WENO kernel gives a 10% acceleration on a $280^3$ grid; merging the Steger–Warming splitting of three directions into one kernel halves the reads of primitive variables (§4.3.7). **Shown** / **Stated**
13. Kernel performance "is more than doubled" by the optimizations (§4). **Stated**
14. In the hybrid scheme "more than 90% of the positions use the low-dissipation seventh-order upwind scheme"; switching artefacts are "relatively slight" because all three schemes are of at least fifth order, and are "submerged" by local shocks, turbulence or separation (§2.2.3). **Stated**
15. With characteristic reconstruction, the hybrid scheme "can be guaranteed not to diverge, provided the time step is appropriate" (§2.2.3). **Stated** (no proof or test)
16. Upwind differencing after flux-vector splitting is more stable than central or spectral discretization of the convection term and suppresses aliasing error (§2.2). **Stated**
17. For flows at Mach numbers "generally exceeding 6" a dissipative scheme is usually introduced for robustness at strong shocks (§1). **Stated**
18. Validation: Mach 2.9, 24° ramp on $16100\times240\times200$ points with WENO-SYMBO and eighth-order central viscous terms, inflow turbulence by blowing and suction instead of recycling; mean wall pressure and van Driest mean velocity agree with the DNS of Martín et al. and the experiment of Bookey et al. (§5.1, Figs. 16–18). **Shown**
19. Wide-span ramp: $16250\times240\times8000$ = 31.2 billion points, "more than 6000 times the number used by Martín et al."; $\Delta x^+=1.2$, $\Delta y^+=0.41$, $\Delta z^+=1.2$ at the reference station 35 mm upstream of the corner (§6.1). **Shown**
20. With a spanwise width above 29 boundary-layer thicknesses, a series of Taylor–Görtler vortices appears behind the shock, the spanwise scale of the streaks grows sharply in the interaction region, and several small co-rotating vortices sit beneath one large Taylor–Görtler vortex (§6.1, §7, Figs. 23–25). **Shown** (qualitative)
21. MPI-IO with collective per-line reads lets a field and grid of "tens of billions" of points be read "in just ten minutes" (§3.4). **Stated**
22. The largest case tested on a 16 GB GPU is $280^3$ points per GPU (§4.2). **Shown**
23. OpenCFD-SCU "saves up to 90% of the computational cost" (§7). **Stated** (no cost computation shown)
24. Eight of the TOP500 top ten (May 2022) use GPUs (§1, Table 1). **Stated**

# Printed slips

1. GPU count: "98.7% parallel weak scalability on 24 567 GPUs" (§1) and "At 24 567 processes" (§5.4), against 24,576 in the Abstract, §5, §5.3 and §7.
2. "Radeon Instinct M160" (§4.1, §4.2, Tables 3 and 7) against "Radeon Instinct MI60" (§4.1, Table 4).
3. Table 6 prints the total as $5.5726\times10^{12}$ floating-point operations; its rows sum to $5.5726\times10^{11}$, and $5.5726\times10^{11}/0.792\ \mathrm{s}=7.036\times10^{11}$ FLOPS (703.6 GFLOPS) is close to the printed 705.4 GFLOPS, while $10^{12}$ would give ten times more *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
4. WENO-SYMBO operation count: 216 in Table 6, 262 "counted manually" in the §5.4 text.
5. Weak scaling: the printed times (0.791 s on 1 process, 0.78 s on 24,576) give a ratio of 1.014, not the printed 98.7% efficiency *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
6. Strong scaling: the Table 5 times give $0.5174/(16\times0.0627)=0.52$, against the printed "60% parallel efficiency" *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
7. Eq. (12): all three heat-flux terms carry $\partial T/\partial x$, also in $\Theta_y$ and $\Theta_z$, the $y$ and $z$ flux components *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
8. Eq. (13): the off-diagonal viscous stress is printed $\mu(\partial u_j/\partial x_j+\partial u_j/\partial x_i)$; the symmetric strain form has $\partial u_i/\partial x_j$ as its first term *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
9. Eqs. (15)–(16): $C$ appears in Sutherland's law as $C/T_{\mathrm{ref}}$, a temperature-like constant, but the text defines it as "the nondimensionalized speed of sound" with $C^2=T/\mathrm{Ma}^2$.
10. Eq. (8) prints $E=\rho\left(\frac{p}{\gamma-1}+\frac{u^2+v^2+w^2}{2}\right)$, multiplying $p/(\gamma-1)$ by $\rho$; the consistent form is $E=\frac{p}{\gamma-1}+\rho\frac{u^2+v^2+w^2}{2}$ *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
11. Eq. (19) text assigns $\varsigma$ metrics to the $y$ fluxes and $\eta$ metrics to the $z$ fluxes, the reverse of Eqs. (1) and (6).
12. Eq. (27) writes $\tilde k_i=\alpha_i/\sigma_i$ with indexed $\sigma_i$, although $\sigma$ is a scalar (Eq. 22), and Eq. (23) uses $\tilde k_1$ in all three momentum rows.
13. Eq. (38) prints "$u_1=ul_1+vl_2+wl_3$"; the quantity used in Eqs. (34)–(35) is $u_l$.
14. Hybrid thresholds: seventh-order WENO for $\theta_1<\theta\le\theta_2$ and fifth-order WENO "when $\theta_2\le\theta$", so $\theta=\theta_2$ is covered twice; the text also says "The higher the values of $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, the higher is the dissipation of the hybrid scheme", whereas by the stated rule higher thresholds send more points to the low-dissipation upwind scheme *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
15. "The width of the outer area is set to 8 bytes" (§4.3.1), against twice LAP = 8 points (§3.2).
16. "17.1p" without a unit (§5.4); §5 gives "17.1 PFLOPS".
17. The companion "Part 2 (Dang, Li et al. 2022)" is cited in the Abstract and §1 but missing from the reference list.

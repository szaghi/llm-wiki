# Summary

Zhang et al. (2022c) report a [direct numerical simulation](/definitions/direct-numerical-simulation.md)
of a three-dimensional [shock/turbulent boundary-layer interaction](/benchmarks/shock-turbulent-boundary-layer-interaction.md)
generated by a symmetric swept compression ramp at $M_\infty=6$, with
$34^\circ$ compression and $45^\circ$ sweep, unit Reynolds number
$10^4$/mm and a cold isothermal wall ($T_w=294$ K, $T_\infty=79$ K). The
GPU finite-difference solver [OpenCFD-SCU](/software/opencfd-scu.md)
combines Steger–Warming splitting with a hybrid of a sixth-order optimized
scheme and seventh-order [WENO](/methods/weno-scheme.md), switched by the
pressure gradient as [shock sensor](/definitions/shock-sensor.md), on grids
of $2.06\times10^9$ and $5.51\times10^9$ points; wall pressure is
grid-converged, peak friction and heating differ by 7–10%. The incoming
[boundary layer](/benchmarks/turbulent-boundary-layer.md) is tripped from a
laminar profile by a [wall disturbance](/methods/inflow-turbulence-generation.md)
and checked with the van Driest [transformation](/definitions/compressible-velocity-transformation.md)
and density-scaled fluctuations (the scaling discussed on
[Morkovin's hypothesis](/definitions/morkovin-hypothesis.md)).

The mean flow is quasi-conical about a virtual conical origin: the
separation, reattachment and corner lines and the lines of peak wall
pressure, friction and heat flux meet there. No region of negative skin
friction exists in the analysed plane, so separation is located by
critical-point theory; the separation region coincides with a crossflow
region (spanwise faster than streamwise velocity) in which a helical
supersonic crossflow accelerates along the span. Peak friction and heating
behind reattachment are attributed to high-energy-density fluid crossing
the bow-shaped head of the main shock and impinging on the thin
reattached layer; they grow with distance from the origin as the main shock
steepens. Wall-pressure spectra show almost no low-frequency unsteadiness
at the separation shock, unlike unswept ramps, and a dominant mid-frequency
band; the authors ascribe this to the weak separation shock and to the
supersonic crossflow carrying perturbations to the spanwise boundary. The
paper contains no turbulence model.

# Key claims

Page-local symbols: $\alpha_{\mathrm{sw}}=45^\circ$ and $\beta_r=34^\circ$ the sweep
and compression angles (the source's $\alpha$, $\beta$); VCO the virtual
conical origin; $(r_c,\theta_c,\varphi_c)$ spherical coordinates about the
VCO, $\theta_c$ the zenith angle from $+y$ and $\varphi_c$ the azimuth (the
source's $r$, $\theta$, $\varphi$); $\lambda_i$ the angle between the local
flow direction and the ramp (the source's $\lambda$); $f_0\approx3U_\infty/\delta$
the characteristic frequency of the upstream boundary layer; E1 the
upstream station $(-16.7,0,30)$ mm.

1. A DNS of a Mach 6 swept compression ramp interaction ($\alpha_{\mathrm{sw}}=45^\circ$, $\beta_r=34^\circ$) is run with the GPU solver OpenCFD-SCU on about $2.06\times10^9$ (Mesh 1) and $5.51\times10^9$ (Mesh 2) points (Abstract, §II B, Table I). **Shown**
2. OpenCFD-SCU on one GPU runs "hundreds of times" faster than OpenCFD-SC on one CPU core; its weak-scaling efficiency "can reach about 98.7% on 24 576 GPUs"; considering energy efficiency it "reduces the computing cost by more than 90%" (§II A, citing ref. 43). **Stated**
3. Numerics: eighth-order central differences for the viscous terms; characteristic-based Steger–Warming splitting with a hybrid of OMP6 (low pressure gradient) and WENO7 (high pressure gradient), the pressure gradient being "the shock recognizer"; third-order TVD Runge–Kutta (§II A). **Stated**
4. Wall pressure on the two meshes is "virtually identical"; skin friction and heat flux are "basically the same near the corner", the peaks of Mesh 1 being "7%–10% lower" than those of Mesh 2 at almost the same positions (§II C, Fig. 4). **Shown**
5. Corner-region resolution: Mesh 1 $\Delta x^+=2.92$, $\Delta y^+=0.44$ at the wall and 5.28 at the boundary-layer edge, $\Delta z^+=2.19$; Mesh 2 1.94, 0.35, 3.53, 1.46 (Table I). **Stated**
6. Inflow boundary layer at E1: $\delta=8.61$ mm, $\delta^*=3.33$ mm, $\theta=0.36$ mm, $H=9.30$ (Table II); the van Driest profile follows the linear and log laws with intercept 5.7, higher than incompressible, attributed to the wall temperature (§II C, Eq. 2, Fig. 6a). **Shown**
7. Density-scaled velocity fluctuations at E1 are "basically consistent" with other simulations and experiments (refs. 34–38) and show near-wall anisotropy (§II C, Fig. 6b). **Shown**
8. No region of negative streamwise velocity ($C_f<0$) exists near the wall in the plane $z=30$ mm; the two-dimensional definition of separation by $C_f<0$ "is unsuitable for 3D interactions", and critical-point theory (converging and diverging wall streamlines) is used instead (§III A, Figs. 4, 7). **Shown**
9. The extensions of the separation, reattachment and corner lines, and of the lines of peak wall pressure, skin friction and heat flux, meet approximately at the VCO $(-15.8,0,-15.8)$ mm: the mean flow is quasi-conically symmetric (§III A–B, Figs. 7, 13). **Shown**
10. Separation and reattachment lie in the planes $\varphi_c=32.4^\circ$ and $51.2^\circ$; near the VCO they are bow-shaped, farther away straight, hence "quasi-conical" (§III A). **Shown**
11. The crossflow region ($w>u$, flow deflected by more than $45^\circ$) spans $\varphi_c=32.4^\circ$ to $49^\circ$, from the separation line to slightly before reattachment, so "the crossflow region and the separation region correspond"; its size grows linearly along the span (§III A, Figs. 8, 9a). **Shown**
12. In the separation region the fluid moves toward the spanwise boundary as a helical vortex, so the separated streamlines are not closed, unlike the cylindrical vortex of two-dimensional interactions (§III A, Fig. 8). **Shown**
13. Along the crossflow centerline a transition region ($r_c<40$ mm) and a self-similar region ($r_c>40$ mm) are distinguished; the crossflow is supersonic and accelerates along the span like flow in a supersonic nozzle, its density rising by entrainment (§III A, Fig. 10b). **Shown** (interpretation **Stated**)
14. The main shock is three-dimensional: farther from the VCO it starts further downstream and is steeper, because the separation region grows along the span and the reattachment shock strengthens; its head is bow-shaped (§III A, Fig. 9b). **Shown**
15. Peak friction and heat flux arise from high-energy-density fluid above the separation bubble that crosses the bow-shaped head of the main shock and impinges on the ramp behind reattachment, where the boundary layer is thin; the analysis is "qualitative rather than quantitative" because the peaks are not fully grid-converged (§III B, Figs. 11–15). **Shown** (qualitative)
16. A strong shear layer between the streamwise high-energy-density fluid and the spanwise crossflow raises the turbulent kinetic energy there (§III B, Fig. 14e,f). **Shown**
17. Peak wall pressure, friction and heat flux increase with distance from the VCO, ascribed to the spanwise growth of the main-shock inclination; along streamlines the density before reattachment differs little, so the reattachment and main shocks set the spanwise difference (§III B, Figs. 16–17). **Shown**
18. Whether the spanwise growth of the wall loads and of the crossflow acceleration has an upper limit is left open; it needs a wider spanwise domain (§III A–B). **Stated**
19. Wall pressure is sampled in the planes $z=15$, 30, 45 mm over about $1500\,\delta/U_\infty$ at intervals of about $0.094\,\delta/U_\infty$ (Mesh 1); frequencies are normalized by $f_0\approx3U_\infty/\delta$ (§III C). **Stated**
20. Narrow-band spectral peaks come from the upstream blowing-and-suction disturbance, as also seen by Priebe and Martin (ref. 42), and do not affect the low-frequency analysis (§III C, Figs. 18–20). **Stated**
21. The low-frequency energy at the separation point is very low; compared with the unswept Mach 5 ramp (ref. 3), the low-frequency energy of the three-dimensional interaction "can be negligible", consistent with Erengil and Dolling (ref. 14) (§III C, Figs. 18–19). **Shown**
22. Two reasons for the missing low-frequency unsteadiness: the separation shock is much weaker than the reattachment and main shocks, and the supersonic crossflow carries sound-speed perturbations along $r_c$ to the spanwise boundary, so the intrinsic instability of the separated flow cannot act persistently on the separation shock (§III C, Abstract, §IV). **Stated**
23. With periodic spanwise boundaries and no mean spanwise velocity, as in two-dimensional interaction simulations, perturbations accumulate in the separation region; in three dimensions the separated flow is not closed (§III C). **Stated**
24. A mid-frequency band, about one order of magnitude below $f_0$, dominates the interaction region; it is related to shear and convective fluctuations between the outer flow and the crossflow (after ref. 19); its band widens and its characteristic frequency falls along the span, and the authors predict a further fall with a wider domain (§III C, Figs. 18, 20). **Shown** (prediction **Stated**)
25. Behind reattachment the high-frequency characteristic frequency is higher than before separation because the reattached boundary layer is thinner (§III C). **Stated**

The free stream has $\gamma=1.4$, $Pr=0.70$ and recovery temperature
$T_r=T_\infty[1+r_T(\gamma-1)M_\infty^2/2]=585$ K with recovery factor
$r_T=0.89$, so the wall is cold; $C_f=\tau_w/(\tfrac12\rho_\infty U_\infty^2)$
and $C_h=q_w/[\rho_\infty U_\infty(h_{aw}-h_w)]$ with $h_w$, $h_{aw}$ the
wall and adiabatic-wall enthalpies. The van Driest velocity is
$u^+_{\mathrm{VD}}=\int_0^{u^+}\sqrt{\langle\rho\rangle/\rho_w}\,\mathrm du^+$
(Eq. 2) and the density-scaled fluctuations are
$\sqrt{\langle\rho\rangle/\rho_w}\,\langle u_i'^2\rangle^{1/2}/u_\tau$ and
$(\langle\rho\rangle/\rho_w)R_{ij}/u_\tau^2$ (Eq. 3).

# Printed slips

- **Abstract against §II:** the abstract says "the computational grid number exceeds 5 × 10^6", §II B–C give $5.51\times10^9$ (Mesh 2) and $2.06\times10^9$ (Mesh 1).
- **Azimuth definition (§III A, Fig. 7 caption):** "$\varphi$ is the azimuth between the projection line of the line VCO-P in the x–y plane and the positive z axis".
- **Fig. 17(b):** the text describes "the change in density with the azimuth $\varphi$ on the three streamlines", while the horizontal axis of the figure is labelled "$\theta$(deg.)".
- **Trip forcing:** called a "multi-frequency sine wave disturbance" in §II B and an "upstream blowing and suction disturbance" in §III C.
- **§I:** refs. 4–5 are attributed to "Pirozzoli and Grasso"; ref. 4 has three authors (Pirozzoli, Grasso & Gatski).

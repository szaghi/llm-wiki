# Summary

Flad & Gassner (2017) examine [implicit LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md) with
the [discontinuous Galerkin spectral element method](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin-spectral-element-method.md)
(DGSEM) on Legendre–Gauss–Lobatto nodes. In that practice no subgrid model is
used, polynomial de-aliasing (over-integration with the 3/2 rule) gives
stability, and the interface Riemann solver (Roe, Lax–Friedrichs) is the only
source of subgrid dissipation. On [decaying homogeneous isotropic turbulence](/benchmarks/homogeneous-isotropic-turbulence.md)
($Re_\lambda$ decaying from about 162 to 97, initialised from a filtered
$512^3$ pseudo-spectral DNS), this works at $144^3$ DOF ($18^3$ elements,
$N=7$), where about 43% of the dissipation is resolved. It fails at $48^3$
DOF ($6^3$ elements, $N=7$), where about 0.9% is resolved: energy piles up at
mid-range wavenumbers and then drops off sharply. Changing the Riemann solver,
or adding a [Smagorinsky](/methods/smagorinsky-model.md) or small–small VMS
model on top, does not help. The authors call this an "applicability
plateau": roughly 40% of the dissipation must be resolved for DG implicit LES
to work. Their explanation is that interface [numerical dissipation](/properties/numerical-dissipation.md)
acts only near the cutoff and lacks the low-wavenumber plateau of the
[spectral eddy viscosity](/methods/spectral-eddy-viscosity.md).

The contribution is a "split form DG LES". The summation-by-parts property of
the DGSEM permits a flux-differencing volume term with Pirozzoli's two-point
flux, used also at the interfaces. The resulting scheme is
[kinetic-energy preserving](/properties/kinetic-energy-preservation.md), adds no
artificial kinetic-energy dissipation, and stays stable in the $48^3$ case,
where it shows a $k^2$ equipartition spectrum. On this baseline, which
separates stability from model dissipation, an explicit Smagorinsky model
($C_s=0.12$) already beats DG implicit LES. Three add-ons that damp the
smallest scales all improve the spectrum: the low-Mach L2Roe flux, a BR2
penalty, and a modal "plateau–cusp" eddy viscosity. The modal eddy viscosity
fits the DNS best, but only L2Roe damps the pressure–dilatation oscillations,
and the authors rank it best overall. In a [turbulent channel](/benchmarks/turbulent-channel-flow.md)
at $Re_\tau=590$ the split form with the [dynamic procedure](/methods/dynamic-procedure.md)
gives better Reynolds stresses than DG implicit LES. All evidence is numerical.

# Key claims

1. Element-based high-order methods with interface Riemann solvers have very low dispersion errors over a broad range of scales. Their dissipation is near zero for well-resolved scales and very high near the cutoff, so the interface dissipation acts like a high-frequency filter (Abstract; §1; §3.1). **Stated**
2. Kirby & Karniadakis showed that cutting the top 1/3 of modes every step stabilises incompressible flow (quadratic flux), and the top 1/2 for compressible flow treated as cubic. Because the compressible nonlinearity is rational, this fails in some cases (§1). **Stated**
3. DG implicit LES (Roe flux, polynomial de-aliasing with $12^3$ quadrature points per element) at $144^3$ DOF ($18^3$ elements, $N=7$) agrees excellently with the DNS of decaying isotropic turbulence (§1, Fig. 1a). **Shown**
4. For the $144^3$ DOF setup, about 62.5% of the total dissipation at $t_0$ is resolved up to the Nyquist wavenumber $k=72$, and about 43.2% up to the 3-points-per-wavelength cutoff $k_c=48$ (§1). **Shown** (computed from the DNS spectrum)
5. For $48^3$ DOF ($6^3$ elements, $N=7$), $k_c=48/3=16$, comparable to a $32^3$ spectral simulation. Only about 0.9% of the total dissipation is resolved, and DG implicit LES fails, with energy pile-up at mid-range scales and a strong drop-off (§1, Fig. 1b). **Shown**
6. Changing the interface flux (Roe, Lax–Friedrichs, low-dissipation Roe) changes the coarse implicit-LES result but does not substantially improve it (§1, Fig. 2). **Shown**
7. DG implicit LES without polynomial de-aliasing is unstable at $N=7$ for this case (§1). **Stated** (no figure)
8. Adding a Smagorinsky or small–small VMS Smagorinsky model to de-aliased Roe DG implicit LES gives no significant improvement "independent of the SGS model constants used" (§1, Fig. 3). **Shown**
9. Roughly 40% of the total dissipation must be resolved for de-aliased upwind DG implicit LES to give good results. The authors call this an "applicability plateau" and say no remedy is known in the literature (§1). **Shown** (from two resolutions only)
10. With Pirozzoli's two-point flux (Eq. 13) in the volume term and as the interface flux, the split-form DGSEM is kinetic-energy preserving across the domain, which removes the aliasing error in kinetic-energy preservation from the advective terms (§2.1). **Stated** (citing Gassner, Winters & Kopriva)
11. The dissipation-free split-form DGSEM ($6^3$ elements, $N=7$, central fluxes, BR1) is stable for the severely under-resolved case and yields an approximate $k^2$ equipartition spectrum near the cutoff. The authors state that, to their knowledge, this is the first such result with a DG scheme (§3, Fig. 4). **Shown**
12. The polynomial resolution limit is $\pi$ points per wavelength. For the dissipation-free scheme it matches where the kinetic energy decreases again: $k=15.28$ for 48 DOF and $k=45.84$ for 144 DOF per direction (§3). **Shown**
13. Split-form DG implicit LES (Pirozzoli volume flux with Roe interface dissipation) behaves like de-aliased DG implicit LES: excellent at $144^3$, failing at $48^3$ DOF. With classic Roe dissipation the two de-aliasing strategies give almost identical spectra, so the failure comes from the Riemann-solver dissipation, not from the de-aliasing (§3.1, Figs. 5, 6 left). **Shown**
14. Proposed explanation: interface dissipation is concentrated at the highest wavenumbers and lacks the low-wavenumber plateau of Kraichnan's spectral eddy viscosity, which matters when the cutoff lies in the inertial range (§3.1). **Stated**
15. With the L2Roe low-dissipation flux, split-form implicit LES is slightly more accurate at mid-range scales than polynomial de-aliasing and behaves like the dissipation-free case at small scales (§3.1, Fig. 6 right). **Shown**
16. The dissipation-free split-form DG with Smagorinsky ($C_s=0.12$) gives "for the first time a successful coarse resolution LES DG result". It is better than DG implicit LES in both the kinetic-energy and the dissipation-rate spectra, with a slight energy underestimate at medium wavenumbers attributed to undamped small-scale fluctuations (§4, Fig. 7). **Shown**
17. Adding explicit subgrid models to standard DG implicit LES is hard because the artificial dissipation interacts with the eddy viscosity. A dissipation-free robust baseline strictly separates stability from model dissipation (§1, §4). **Stated**
18. Three "cusp" strategies all improve the kinetic-energy spectrum over plain Smagorinsky: (i) the L2Roe interface flux, (ii) BR2 with penalty $\eta_{BR}=2$, (iii) a plateau–cusp modal eddy viscosity. Strategy (iii) fits the DNS best when its parameters are tuned to the baseline (§5, Fig. 8). **Shown**
19. Strategy (i) reproduces the decay of the dissipation rate and of the kinetic energy (integrated up to $k=16$) of the filtered DNS much better than standard DG implicit LES, which fails to reproduce the energy decay (§5, Fig. 9). **Shown**
20. Neither the kinetic-energy-preserving split form nor the Smagorinsky model damps pressure or density fluctuations, so the pressure–dilatation oscillates strongly. Strategy (i) removes these oscillations, (ii) reduces them somewhat, and (iii) leaves them unchanged (§5.1, Fig. 10). **Shown**
21. Best overall: the split-form baseline with L2Roe, which damps density and pressure fluctuations without affecting the momentum dissipation, plus the standard Smagorinsky model (§5.1, §6.1). **Shown**
22. The split-form LES approach reduces computational cost by at least a factor of 3 in the authors' in-house DG framework (§6.1). **Stated** (no timings, hardware or breakdown given)
23. Channel flow at $Re_\tau=590$ ($8^3$ elements, $N=7$): split-form DG LES with the dynamic Smagorinsky model (Germano, Lilly) gives better Reynolds-stress profiles than DG implicit LES with polynomial de-aliasing and Roe flux. Both are called "acceptable" (§6.2, Fig. 11). **Shown**

# Printed slips

- **Filter width (App. A).** "The filter width $\Delta$ is computed as the local cell volume divided be $(N+1)^3$". As printed this is a volume, not a length.
- **Smagorinsky constant.** Eqs. A.1 and B.1 print $-2C_S^2\Delta^2$. Eqs. C.5 and D.1 print $-2C_S\Delta^2$ (unsquared), with "$C_S=0.13^2$" and "$C_{Cusp}=0.33^2$" in App. C. The Fig. 7 caption gives "$C_s=0.12$" and the Fig. 3 legend "CS=0.17". The meaning of $C_S$ changes between appendices.
- **Turnover time (§1).** "$T=\bar v(t_{start})/L_{int}\approx1.3$": a velocity over a length is an inverse time.
- **Eq. 10.** "$(F^a_1)_{mjk}=F^a_1(\hat U_{ijk})$": the index $m$ on the left becomes $i$ on the right.
- **Eq. 13** uses $u,v,w$ and $h$, while Eq. 2 uses $v_1,v_2,v_3$ and $h$ is never defined.
- **Eqs. C.2, C.3** have unbalanced closing parentheses.
- **Strategy lists.** §6.1 lists the three strategies as "a kinetic energy consistent split-DG scheme, the low dissipation Roe-type Riemann solver of [35], and Smagorinsky's SGS model". This does not match the three cusp strategies of §5: (i) L2Roe, (ii) BR2 with $\eta_{BR}=2$, (iii) the cusp eddy viscosity.
- **Germano identity (App. D).** Eq. D.3 is called "the Germano identity" but defines the resolved stress $L_{ij}$ (printed "$Lij$").
- **Fig. 11 caption** ends with a dangling "and no model LES". Its legend names an L2Roe interface flux ("RoeL2") for the split-form channel run, which the text does not mention.
- **Typos:** "Priozzoli" (§1), "unwinding type" (§2.1), "snape" (§5), "we extend change the BR1 approach" (§5), "Plataeu-Cusp" (App. C title), "grater" (App. B).
- **Dynamic coefficient, sign and width ratio (App. D)** *(compiler check, not in the sources)*. The printed procedure is $C_S\Delta^2=-\tfrac12\langle L_{ij}M_{ij}\rangle/\langle M_{ij}M_{ij}\rangle$, with the test-filter ratio entering $M_{ij}$ unsquared as $(\tilde\Delta/\Delta)$. In registry notation, insert the model of Eq. D.1, $\tau^d_{ij}=-2C\Delta^2\lvert\bar S\rvert\bar S^d_{ij}$, at both filter levels of $L_{ij}=T_{ij}-\hat\tau_{ij}$. This gives $L^d_{ij}=2C\Delta^2M_{ij}$ with $M_{ij}=\widehat{\lvert\bar S\rvert\bar S^d_{ij}}-(\hat\Delta/\Delta)^2\lvert\hat{\bar S}\rvert\hat{\bar S}^d_{ij}$. The least-squares coefficient is then $C\Delta^2=+\tfrac12\langle L_{ij}M_{ij}\rangle/\langle M_{ij}M_{ij}\rangle$, with the width ratio squared. The printed sign and exponent do not match the printed $L_{ij}$, $M_{ij}$ and Eq. D.1.
- **Plateau term of Eq. C.3** *(compiler check, not in the sources)*. The constant 9.21 of Eq. C.3 equals $0.267\times34.5$, the cusp coefficient of Eq. C.2. The plateau term "1+" of Eq. C.2 is therefore absent from Eq. C.3, although the model is called "plateau–cusp".

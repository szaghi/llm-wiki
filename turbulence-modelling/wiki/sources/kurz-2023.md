# Summary

Kurz et al. (2023) argue that supervised learning of LES closures is
ill-posed for implicitly filtered LES, where the underresolved
discretization is the filter: for discontinuous Galerkin it is an
element-local $L_2$ projection coupled across elements by numerical
fluxes, a non-linear filter of unknown form, so exact closure terms cannot
be computed from DNS to serve as targets
([discretization-consistent closure](/definitions/discretization-consistent-closure.md),
[a priori and a posteriori testing](/definitions/a-priori-and-a-posteriori-testing.md)).
A [reinforcement-learning closure](/methods/reinforcement-learning-closure.md)
avoids this, because the policy is trained by interacting with the running
LES, a form of [model-consistent training](/methods/model-consistent-training.md)
that needs no differentiation of the solver.

With their framework [Relexi](/software/relexi.md) (TensorFlow, TF-Agents,
SmartSim) and the PPO algorithm, the authors train a small
three-dimensional convolutional policy that reads the momentum field of one
element and returns that element's coefficient of the
[Smagorinsky model](/methods/smagorinsky-model.md), $C_s\in[0,0.5]$,
updated every $\Delta t_{\mathrm{RL}}=0.1$. The LES solver is
[FLEXI](/software/flexi.md), a
[DG spectral element method](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin-spectral-element-method.md)
with [kinetic-energy-preserving](/properties/kinetic-energy-preservation.md)
split-form fluxes. The flow is forced
[homogeneous isotropic turbulence](/benchmarks/homogeneous-isotropic-turbulence.md)
at $Re_\lambda\approx180$, $M=0.1$; the reward measures the error of the
instantaneous LES spectrum against the time-averaged DNS spectrum. The
authors present the purely element-local input as an improvement over
Novati et al. (2021), whose policy also read global statistics.

On four resolutions (24 to 48 degrees of freedom per direction, $N=5$ or
7) the policies are stable over four times the training horizon. They beat
[implicit LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md), which piles up energy at high
wavenumbers, and static Smagorinsky, which is over-dissipative near the
resolution limit ([numerical dissipation](/properties/numerical-dissipation.md)
being the model of the former). They match the
[dynamic procedure](/methods/dynamic-procedure.md) and beat it on the
coarsest grid, where, by the authors' explanation, the test filter is
outside the scale-similar range. The policies transfer between two
resolutions and to $Re_\lambda\approx240$. The predicted coefficients are
non-negative by design, unlike the clipped dynamic model, which allows
[backscatter](/definitions/energy-backscatter.md). The work is a proof of
concept in [large-eddy simulation](/definitions/large-eddy-simulation.md),
and the trained models are not proposed for use. The
[neural-network SGS model](/methods/neural-network-sgs-model.md) page
places it among learned subgrid models.

# Key claims

Page-local: DOF is the number of degrees of freedom per direction (elements
per direction times $N+1$); $\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}$, $\Delta t_{\mathrm{RL}}$
as in the registry (§11); $n_{ppw}$ points per wavelength; $\alpha_r$ the
reward scaling factor (the source's $\alpha$).

1. For implicitly filtered LES the filter form is unknown, so exact closure terms cannot be computed even from the full DNS solution; supervised learning there is "from our perspective, ill-posed, since the training targets are generally unknown" (§1, §3.3). **Stated**
2. The induced filter of DG is an element-local $L_2$ projection, but the coupling of elements through numerical fluxes makes the DG operator a complex filter with typically non-linear kernel (§1). **Stated**
3. Training supervised models on targets from an approximating explicit filter leads to "severe inconsistencies between the training and actual simulations" and can cause inaccurate or unstable simulations (§1, citing [8]). **Stated**
4. Reinforcement learning incorporates the implicit filter into training by design, since the policy learns by interacting with the LES (Abstract, §1, §3.5). **Stated**
5. The policy acts on element-local inputs only; this "improves on" Novati et al. [17], who also fed the global dissipation rate and spectrum to the policy (§1, §3.4). **Stated**
6. The return starts negative for the random initial policy and converges to a plateau just below the maximum 50 (50 rewards per episode, each in $[-1,1]$) (§4.1, Fig. 5). **Shown**
7. Using 32 instead of 16 episodes per policy update sped up training consistently (§4.1). **Shown**
8. The 48 DOF case needed fewer iterations and showed less return variance than the 24 DOF case; the authors offer two reasons (smaller model influence, more elements hence more samples per state) and note the speed-up could be stochastic, being non-monotone in resolution (§4.1). **Shown**
9. Training took one to five days on HAWK (HLRS), with up to 1024 CPU cores and one GPU node (§4.1). **Shown**
10. A posteriori tests from an unseen state run to $t=20$ (four times the training horizon of 5): the policies are long-term stable (§4.2, Fig. 6). **Shown**
11. Implicit LES shows an energy pile-up at high wavenumbers; static Smagorinsky with $C_s=0.17$ is over-dissipative near the resolution limit; the policy "clearly outperforms both models for all considered configurations", matching the target spectrum "almost perfectly up to and even beyond" the resolution limit of about 4 points per wavelength (§4.2, Fig. 6). **Shown**
12. The dynamic Smagorinsky model reproduces the DNS spectra with accuracy similar to the policy, except at 24 DOF, where the policy is significantly better; the authors' "likely explanation" is that the test filter is not in the scale-similar range there (§4.2). **Shown** (explanation **Stated**)
13. Velocity-fluctuation PDFs: static Smagorinsky narrowest, implicit LES broadest, the policy closest to DNS; at 36 DOF the policy gives an unsymmetric distribution whose origin is "still subject of on-going investigations" (§4.2, Fig. 6). **Shown**
14. Most predicted $C_s$ are near zero with a decaying tail; at 24 DOF the agent uses almost the whole range up to 0.5, at 48 DOF it stays below 0.3 (§4.2, Fig. 6). **Shown**
15. On one CPU core (48 DOF), evaluating the unoptimized policy took about 10% more time than the dynamic procedure (§4.2). **Shown**
16. Policies reading the five velocity-gradient invariants (Pope) train slower and less stably and end worse than momentum-input policies, especially at 36 DOF; doubling the iterations left "the gap … quite substantial" (§4.3, Fig. 7). **Shown**
17. The authors attribute claim 16 to input distributions: velocity fluctuations are near $\mathcal N(0,1)$ in this flow, while the invariants span orders of magnitude, DG gradients are discontinuous across faces, and the projection of DNS on the coarse basis creates large gradients in the initial states (§4.3). **Stated**
18. The 48 DOF policy run at 36 DOF and vice versa give stable, accurate results with comparable spectral errors, beating static Smagorinsky and implicit LES and matching the dynamic model (§4.4, Fig. 8). **Shown**
19. The 36 DOF policy predicts up to $C_s\approx0.4$, the 48 DOF policy at most 0.3, on either resolution; the authors conclude that the distribution is a property of the policy trained on a given discretization, which induces its own implicit filter ("discretization-adapted turbulent models for implicit LES") (§4.4). **Shown** (interpretation **Stated**)
20. Policies trained at $Re_\lambda\approx180$ stay long-term stable at $Re_\lambda\approx240$; at 32–48 DOF they beat implicit LES and static Smagorinsky and are similar to the dynamic model, both showing some energy build-up near the cutoff; at 24 DOF the policy still outperforms the dynamic model (§4.5, Fig. 9). **Shown**
21. Removing the action limits ($C_s$ unbounded) gave similar accuracy for selected configurations but needed many more training iterations (§3.4, footnote 3). **Shown** (results not displayed)
22. With about 4 points per wavelength as the resolution capability of these DG schemes (citing [35]), the reward's maximum wavenumber $k_{\max}$ is set so that $2.6\le n_{ppw}\le3$, forcing optimization over all represented wavenumbers (§3.5). **Stated**
23. The authors "do not propose to use any of the models prescribed here in practice"; the work is a proof of concept (§5). **Stated**
24. The unclipped elementwise dynamic model occasionally produced huge $C_s^2$ that "deteriorated the solution quite drastically"; with $\mu_t\in[-100\mu,1000\mu]$ the clipping range had very limited influence (Appendix A). **Shown** (results not displayed)

Setup (§3.4–3.5, Table 2, Appendix A): $\mu_t=\bar\rho(C_s\Delta)^2|\bar S|$ (Eq. 19; $\Delta$ for DG not defined in the paper); action noise Gaussian with fixed standard deviation 0.02; reward $r=2\exp\!\big(-\frac{1}{\alpha_rk_{\max}}\sum_{k=1}^{k_{\max}}\big(\frac{\langle E_{\mathrm{DNS}}\rangle(k)-E_{\mathrm{LES}}(k)}{\langle E_{\mathrm{DNS}}\rangle(k)}\big)^2\big)-1$ with $\alpha_r=0.4$ (Eq. 21); episodes of length 5 from filtered DNS states at $t=3$–6, test from $t=8$; PPO with clip 0.2, $\gamma_{\mathrm{RL}}=0.995$, Adam, learning rate $10^{-4}$; the dynamic model is elementwise Germano–Lilly with a modal test filter to degree 2 ($N=5$) or 3 ($N=7$) and element averaging.

# Printed slips

- **Reward argument:** Table 1 and §2 write $\mathcal R(s_t,s_{t+1})$, Eq. (21) writes $\mathcal R(s)$.
- **Velocity symbol:** $\lambda^i_{\nabla v}$ (§3.4, Figs. 4, 7) against $\lambda^i_{\nabla u}$ (§4.3); Appendix A writes $u$ where the main text writes $v$.
- **§4.4:** "the distribution of the models' predictions are almost identical for both cases and thus do not appear to change depending on the LES resolution", followed by "The predictions of the 36 DOF model exhibit a much wider tail" than those of the 48 DOF model; consistent only if "both cases" means each model across the two resolutions.
- **Episode length:** "$\Delta t_{end}=5$" (§3.5) against "$t_{end}=5$" (Fig. 5 caption).
- **Typos:** "incorporation if physical invariances" (§5); "RL model sill outperforms" and "the RL models is able" (§4.5); "causes the environment to change is state" (§2.1).

# Summary

Beck & Kurz (2023) treat the closure of
[implicitly filtered LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md), where the grid and
the discrete operator induce the filter, so that $\Delta\approx h$ and the
filter shape is unknown and possibly nonlinear. On the linear advection and
inviscid Burgers equations they split the unclosed terms into a commutation
error $C_1$, the classical subfilter term $C_2$ and a
discretization-induced term $C_3$ (the "computational subgrid stresses").
For $\Delta\approx h$, $C_3$ can exceed $C_2$, and it cannot be determined
[a priori](/definitions/a-priori-and-a-posteriori-testing.md). A closure
must therefore be [discretization-consistent](/definitions/discretization-consistent-closure.md)
and [trained in the loop](/methods/model-consistent-training.md) with the
actual LES operator.

They pose the adaptation of model parameters as a Markov decision process
and solve it with [reinforcement learning](/methods/reinforcement-learning-closure.md)
(PPO, beta-distributed policy, a convolutional network of about 3300
parameters). The Relexi framework couples the agent to the
[DGSEM](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin-spectral-element-method.md) code
[FLEXI](/software/flexi.md), which runs in
[split form](/properties/kinetic-energy-preservation.md) for nonlinear
stability (anti-aliasing). The
reward measures the error of the LES energy spectrum against DNS. Three
models act per element: RL-Const, an elementwise constant
[Smagorinsky](/methods/smagorinsky-model.md) $C_s$; RL-Quad, a $C_s$ that
varies quadratically inside the element; and RL-Blend, the blending factor
$\alpha$ of the [subcell finite-volume shock-capturing](/methods/subcell-finite-volume-shock-capturing.md)
scheme of Hennemann et al., reinterpreted as an implicit SGS model.

On forced [isotropic turbulence](/benchmarks/homogeneous-isotropic-turbulence.md)
at $Re_\lambda\approx180$ with 24 or 36 degrees of freedom per direction
and $N=3,5,8$, the RL models match or beat the best baseline: no-model LES,
static Smagorinsky, or the [dynamic](/methods/dynamic-procedure.md) model.
The exception is $N=3$, where no-model LES is best. RL-Quad learns an eddy
viscosity peaked at the element centre and vanishing at the faces, which the
authors read as compensating the face-concentrated
[numerical dissipation](/properties/numerical-dissipation.md) of DG.

# Key claims

1. In implicitly filtered LES the discretization induces the filter, and $\Delta\approx h$; in explicit LES the two are chosen independently (§I). **Stated**
2. For linear advection the filtered equation contains a commutation error $C_1[\bar\cdot;\partial_x]\,(u)=\partial_x\bar u-\overline{\partial_xu}$ that does not vanish when $\Delta$ is nonuniform or anisotropic, already for linear equations (§I, Eq. 2, citing [4–10]). **Derived**
3. For inviscid Burgers the filtered equation contains $C_1$ and the classical unclosed term $C_2=\tfrac12\partial_x(\bar u\bar u-\overline{u^2})$, i.e. $-\tfrac12\partial_x\tau$ with $\tau=\overline{uu}-\bar u\bar u$; a discrete derivative $\delta_x$ adds $C_3[\partial_x;\delta_x]=\tfrac12(\delta_x-\partial_x)(\bar u\bar u)$ (Eq. 4), or, writing $\delta_xf=\partial_x\mathcal F_h(f)$ with a numerically induced filter $\mathcal F_h$, $C_3^*=\tfrac12\partial_x(\mathcal F_h(\bar u\bar u)-\bar u\bar u)$ (Eq. 5) (§I). **Derived**
4. For consistent schemes and smooth, band-limited fields $C_3$ and $C_3^*$ converge at the design order as $h\to0$, but not for grid-filtered turbulence; for $\Delta\approx h$ the computational subgrid stresses $C_3$ or forces $C_3^*$ can be much larger than $C_2$ (§I, citing Geurts & van der Bos [2]). **Stated**
5. High-order schemes are nonlinear in under-resolved settings (upwinding, non-oscillatory reconstruction, limiters), so $C_3^{(*)}$ depends on the current solution and cannot be determined a priori; the closure must be optimized together with the LES operator, and a segregated optimization on independently generated data "is not constructive" (§I, citing [11, 17]). **Stated**
6. Ghosal & Moin found the $C_1$ due to grid-spacing change comparable to the $C_3$ of a second-order finite-difference scheme, with $C_1$ purely (anti-)dissipative and the discretization error purely dispersive (§I, [7]). **Stated**
7. The closure optimization is a Markov decision process solved by PPO; the reward (Eq. 18) uses the normalized squared error of the energy spectrum up to $k_{\max}$, with $k_{\max}=9$ (24 DOF) and $11$ (36 DOF) and scaling $0.4$ (§IV B). **Stated** (method definition)
8. A beta-distribution policy instead of a Gaussian "has shown to accelerate the training" (§IV B, citing [70]). **Stated**
9. Training: episodes of 5 time units (about 7 large-eddy turnovers), an action every $\Delta t_{RL}=0.1$ (50 per episode), 16 parallel episodes and 5 epochs per iteration, Adam with learning rate $10^{-4}$, 2000 iterations per configuration; at most 0.8 CPU-hours per LES (§IV B). **Stated**
10. Evaluation rewards exceed training rewards, attributed to a possibly more favourable unseen initial state and, mainly, to greedy evaluation against stochastic sampling during training (§V A; Fig. 7). **Shown**
11. RL-Quad collects more reward than RL-Const for $N=3$ and $N=8$ (36 DOF), with a gap that grows with $N$ (confirmed for $N=5$); RL-Const at $N=8$ does not converge within 2000 iterations (§V A; Figs. 7–8). **Shown**
12. $N=8$ cases reach higher rewards than $N=3$, attributed to better scale resolution and a smaller $C_3$ ($\delta_x\approx\partial_x$) (§V A). **Shown** (interpretation **Stated**)
13. The untrained policy gives a near-Gaussian $C_s$ distribution with mean about 0.17 and too much eddy viscosity; after training the distribution is skewed toward lower $C_s$ (§V A; Fig. 7). **Shown**
14. The reward of no-model LES decreases with $N$, where numerical dissipation concentrates at element faces; no-model LES is the best baseline at $N=3$, ahead of the dynamic model; static and dynamic Smagorinsky rewards rise with $N$ (§V A; Fig. 8). **Shown**
15. The RL models outperform the dynamic Smagorinsky model for $N=5$ and $N=8$; for $N=3$ no-model LES is best and the RL models do not reach it; RL gives "arguably the best overall performance for the range of cases investigated" (§V A; Fig. 8). **Shown**
16. No baseline is universally best; performance depends on the pairing with the discretization, which supports treating closure and discretization jointly (§V A). **Shown**
17. At $N=5$, no-model LES is under-dissipative (energy pile-up at the cutoff) and static Smagorinsky ($C_s=0.17$) over-damps high wavenumbers; RL-Const and RL-Quad agree with DNS at 24 and 36 DOF; the dynamic model is close at 36 DOF but deviates at 24 DOF, plausibly because the test filter leaves the similarity range (§V B; Fig. 9). **Shown** (explanation **Stated**)
18. The RL policies find a suitable $C_s$ dynamically from the resolved field alone, with no scale-invariance assumption and no test filter (§V B). **Shown**
19. The RL action distributions are strongly positively skewed (little or no eddy viscosity most of the time); RL-Const has a higher mean $C_s$ than RL-Quad and a wider distribution at 24 DOF (§V B; Fig. 9). **Shown**
20. For $N=3$, where no-model LES is accurate, the agent's skewed $C_s$ distribution mimics $C_s=0$; for $N=5$ it is less skewed with a higher mean (§V B; Fig. 10). **Shown**
21. The time-averaged RL-Quad $C_s$ (36 DOF, $N=5$) is a parabola of the same orientation in every element, maximal at the centre and near zero at the faces, inverting the DG numerical dissipation that enters through the face fluxes; the authors read this as accounting for $C_3^*$ as well as $C_2$, a hypothesis they flag as needing investigation (§V B; Fig. 11). **Shown**
22. The hybrid DG/FV subcell scheme of Hennemann et al., a shock-capturing scheme, had "not been interpreted as a possible implicit SGS model alternative yet" (§V C). **Stated**
23. Without an explicit model, pure DG ($\alpha=0$) is under-dissipative, pure FV ($\alpha=1$) over-dissipative, and the RL-optimized blending matches the DNS spectrum (36 DOF, $N=5$) (§V C; Fig. 12). **Shown**
24. Finite-volume subcell dissipation is concentrated at the element centre, where the subcells (sized by the LGL weights $w_i$) are largest, the inverse of DG dissipation at the faces, so blending can homogenize dissipation (§V C; Fig. 4). **Stated**
25. RL-Blend reaches rewards comparable to the explicit RL closures; at 24 DOF the agent raises the FV share, for $N=3$ it lowers it and for $N=5$ it raises the mean $\alpha$ (§V C; Figs. 8, 13, 14). **Shown**
26. RL-Quad and RL-Blend give very similar, accurate spectra for $N=3$ and $N=5$ despite different modelling ideas (§V C; Fig. 14). **Shown**
27. On a sinusoidally deformed mesh (Eq. A1, amplitude $\pi/10$, geometry degree 4) all LES lose accuracy at low wavenumbers; the dynamic model and RL-Quad (not retrained) perform comparably and better than RL-Const, which loses energy at $k\in[2,6]$ (App. A; Fig. 16). **Shown**
28. The trained models are offered not as ready-to-use closures but as a way of developing discretization-specific models; generalizability, interpretability and uncertainty remain open, and transfer learning and meta-RL are proposed to reduce training cost (§VI). **Stated**

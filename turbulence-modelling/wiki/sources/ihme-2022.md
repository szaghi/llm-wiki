# Summary

Ihme et al. (2022) review machine learning for combustion science
("combustion machine learning"). Section 1 argues that combustion is
data-rich: a survey of more than 200 reacting-flow
[DNS](/definitions/direct-numerical-simulation.md) studies estimates more
than 10 PB of structured data. It contrasts physics-based, data-driven and
"knowledge-guided data-driven" discovery, the authors preferring that term
to "physics-informed" for combustion. Section 2 gives the
statistical-learning background (distributions, Bayes' theorem, maximum
likelihood, losses, generalisation error, bias–variance, epistemic against
aleatoric uncertainty as in [model-form uncertainty](/definitions/model-form-uncertainty.md),
dataset shift). Section 3 surveys supervised, unsupervised,
semi-supervised and reinforcement learning with three worked tutorials
(ignition-regime classification, k-means and PCA on an LES snapshot,
Q-learning control of a stirred reactor; the RL is control, not a
[turbulence closure](/methods/reinforcement-learning-closure.md)), and a
taxonomy of knowledge-guided ML: constraints in the loss
([PINNs](/methods/physics-informed-neural-network.md)), the data, the
architecture (the [tensor-basis neural network](/methods/tensor-basis-neural-network.md))
or the output.

The turbulence content is §4.1.4, framed by the filtered reacting-flow
equations of [LES](/definitions/large-eddy-simulation.md). Neural networks
for the filtered density function and for the subgrid flame surface density
beat algebraic closures a priori; gene expression programming and sparse
[symbolic regression](/methods/symbolic-regression-closure.md) of the
subgrid stress in flames rediscover the
[Clark gradient model](/methods/gradient-model.md); a
[neural-network](/methods/neural-network-sgs-model.md) closure of the
filtered scalar dissipation degrades with filter width; and the
[super-resolution](/methods/super-resolution-sgs-model.md) PIESRGAN with a
continuity loss is tested a priori and a posteriori in spray-flame LES.
The verdict: supervised closures often outperform physics-based ones
[a priori, with caution needed a posteriori](/definitions/a-priori-and-a-posteriori-testing.md),
where accumulated errors can make them unstable. §4.1.3 reviews neural
networks as replacements for [tabulated chemistry](/methods/flamelet-tabulated-chemistry.md).
Sections 4.2–4.3 (propulsion, fire, explosions) and 5 (benchmarks,
interpretability, uncertainty quantification, out-of-distribution
prediction, cost) stay on this page. Evidence is that of the cited works,
except the tutorials.

# Key claims

Page-local symbols: $\mathcal D=\{(\boldsymbol\zeta,y)_i\}$ the learning
set (the source's $\mathscr L$), $\lambda_{\mathrm{reg}}$ the
regularisation weight, $g$ a constraint residual, $N_\psi$ the dimension
of a combustion manifold, $\Delta/l_{\mathrm f}$ the filter width over the
laminar flame thickness, $c$ the progress variable, $Z$ the mixture
fraction.

1. More than 200 reacting-flow DNS studies (refs [18–239]) give, assuming $\mathcal O(10^6)$ timesteps and $\mathcal O(10^2)$ stored snapshots per DNS, "more than 10 PB of structured data"; mean mesh size grew by more than three orders of magnitude in 20 years, and 3-D reacting DNS carry upward of $\mathcal O(30)$ species (§1.1, Fig. 1). **Shown** (literature survey)
2. Experimental diagnostics exceed 400 GB/s; an aircraft gas turbine is estimated to produce 2 GB to 2 TB per transatlantic flight; satellites supply more than 100 GB/day for wildfire monitoring (§1.1). **Stated**
3. Purely data-driven approaches "are not expected to be truly predictive" (no governing laws, too little data, poor generalisation); knowledge-guided approaches are the remedy (§1.3, Figs. 3–4). **Stated**
4. Polynomial fits of order 1, 3 and 5 to ten noisy CH$_4$/air flame-speed points show underfitting, a good fit and overfitting, illustrating the bias–variance decomposition (Eq. 34) (§2.5, Fig. 7). **Shown**
5. Learning-set splits: 80:20 training to testing, or 80:10:10 and 60:20:20 with validation; the tutorial uses 60:20:20 (§2.4, §3.2.6). **Stated**
6. Ignition-regime tutorial (n-dodecane/air, 20 bar): logistic regression reaches 0.89 test accuracy; with 60% training data all nonlinear classifiers reach about 96%, the feedforward network best; a random forest beats the network below 5% of the data (§3.2.6, Figs. 19–20a). **Shown**
7. In the same tutorial SVM accuracy can decrease with more data, its hyperparameters having been tuned at about 20% of the data; random-forest boundaries are axis-aligned artefacts of recursive partitioning (§3.2.6–3.2.7). **Shown**
8. Inductive biases by model class: axis-aligned trees, margin-sensitive SVMs, weak bias of fully connected layers, translation equivariance and locality of CNNs (not rotation- or reflection-invariant), temporal invariance of RNNs; L1 favours sparsity, L2 small parameters, dropout reduces co-adaptation (§3.2.7). **Stated**
9. Rules of thumb: about a thousand images per class for a CNN classifier; augmentation, transfer learning and domain constraints reduce this to hundreds, tens or fewer (§3.2.7). **Stated**
10. k-means on seven thermochemical features of a jet-in-hot-coflow LES snapshot: 3 clusters separate reactants, products and oxidiser, 5 isolate the reaction zone; after PCA to two components 6 clusters are needed (§3.3.3, Fig. 21). **Shown**
11. Q-learning control of a well-stirred reactor converges to the target band on the unstable middle branch of the S-curve after about 30 episodes (§3.4.3, Fig. 24, Table 1). **Shown**
12. Deep RL faces data inefficiency (upward of $\mathcal O(10^5)$ samples), unpredictable behaviour outside training conditions and lack of interpretability (§3.4.2, §5). **Stated**
13. Knowledge-guided constraints are in practice enforced weakly, $\arg\min_{\boldsymbol\theta}\sum\mathcal L+\lambda_{\mathrm{reg}}g$ (Eqs. 64–66), PINNs being the flow-physics example; the tensor-basis neural network was "one of the first architectures tailored for fluid modeling", with Galilean invariance built in (§3.5.1). **Stated**
14. Structured tables of combustion manifolds need memory $\mathcal O(10^{N_\psi})$, limiting them to $N_\psi\lesssim4$; neural-network regression is the alternative (§4.1.3). **Stated**
15. Networks first replaced reaction chemistry in transported-PDF simulations (Christo et al.); networks found by pattern search, with dense connectivity beyond neighbouring layers, are more accurate and have a significantly higher accuracy-to-memory ratio ("knowledge density") than conventional tabulation (§4.1.3, Fig. 29). **Stated**
16. A priori, deep networks beat algebraic models of the joint filtered density function of $Z$ and $c$ at large filter widths (Chen et al.), and a U-Net using only the filtered progress variable beats physics-based closures of the subgrid flame surface density (Lapeyre et al.) (§4.1.4). **Stated**
17. Findings on generative models are mixed: for joint $Z$–$c$ PDFs a fully connected network beat a variational autoencoder and a random forest (Henry de Frahan et al. [570]); a Wasserstein GAN beat a CNN for small scales of scalar transport (Bode et al. [571]); the authors call for a common benchmark dataset (§4.1.4). **Stated**
18. Gene expression programming of the subgrid stress and subgrid energy as tensor-basis and invariant expansions (Eq. 74), on premixed-flame DNS, rediscovers tensor-diffusivity forms performing like Clark's model (Schoepplein et al.); sparse symbolic regression with random-forest preselection, on transcritical non-premixed flames, also yields a Clark-like model (Chung et al.) (§4.1.4, Fig. 30). **Stated**
19. A feedforward network for the filtered scalar dissipation rate of the progress variable reproduces small-scale features at small filter ratios, with discrepancies growing as $\Delta/l_{\mathrm f}$ increases (Yellapantula et al.) (§4.1.4, Fig. 31). **Stated**
20. ML subgrid models trained on filtered DNS and tested a priori "can be unstable due to the accumulation of small errors over a large number of timesteps when evaluated a posteriori" [559]; knowledge-guided ML is one remedy, e.g. PIESRGAN with a continuity-enforcing loss, used a priori and a posteriori in n-dodecane spray-flame LES (Bode et al.) (§4.1.4, Fig. 32). **Stated**
21. Supervised learning "often outperform[s] physics-based closure models in a priori studies; however, caution is warranted for extrapolating these findings to a posteriori applications" (§4.1.4). **Stated**
22. Cost reduction: k-means cell clustering in engine simulations cut cost by 50–75% (Perini); random-forest assignment of combustion submodels in a rocket-combustor LES by 20% (Chung et al.); network selection of the stiff-chemistry ODE solver gave speedups above three (Lapointe et al.) (§4.1.4). **Stated**
23. Random-forest submodel assignment is interpretable, and its errors "are limited by the predictive capability of the lowest-performing submodel" (§4.1.4). **Stated**
24. For out-of-distribution prediction, turbulence-modelling studies [279,570] "indicate improvements in generalizability, when using generative learning methods instead of supervised learning"; the Wasserstein metric or the Mahalanobis distance can gauge whether a trained model applies to new samples (§5.4). **Stated**
25. Gaussian-process inference cost grows cubically with the number of observations; Bayesian-network uncertainty depends strongly on the inference algorithm and training can be orders of magnitude longer; Gaussian processes currently offer accurate means, reliable uncertainty and robustness out of distribution (§5.3). **Stated**
26. Most combustion-ML applications so far are a priori tests and idealised problems (§6). **Stated**

The closure framing (Eq. 72), in registry notation for the filter: with
$\boldsymbol V$ the reduced state, $\partial_t\bar{\boldsymbol V}+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol F(\bar{\boldsymbol V})-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol Q(\bar{\boldsymbol V},\nabla\bar{\boldsymbol V})=\overline{\boldsymbol S(\boldsymbol V)}+\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol F(\bar{\boldsymbol V})-\overline{\boldsymbol F(\boldsymbol V)})-\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol Q(\bar{\boldsymbol V},\nabla\bar{\boldsymbol V})-\overline{\boldsymbol Q(\boldsymbol V,\nabla\boldsymbol V)})$:
turbulence–chemistry interaction, subgrid convective flux and subgrid
diffusive flux. The GEP model forms (Eq. 74) are, with the source's stress
$\tau^{SGS}_{ij}$ the compressible registry $\tau_{ij}$ (full, positive trace),
$\tau_{ij}=\bar\rho\sum_mG^m(I_1,I_2,\dots)\,T^{(m)}_{ij}$ and
$\tau_{kk}=\bar\rho\sum_mC_mI_m$.

# Printed slips

- **Eq. (36):** supervised learning is said to proceed "by minimizing an objective function", but Eq. (36) prints $\arg\max_{\theta\in\mathscr P}E(Y,f(X,\theta))$ with $E$ the error measure.
- **§3.4.3 against Table 1b:** the text reads "the largest Q for the state $T^{n+1}>T^*_{min}$"; the Table 1b row is $T^{n+1}>T^*_{max}$.
- **§4.1.4 against §5.4 on ref [570]:** §4.1.4 reports that the variational autoencoder of [570] did not beat the fully connected network; §5.4 cites [279,570] as indicating better generalisability with generative methods than with supervised learning.
- **§4.1.4:** "ERSGAN [580]", the reference being titled "ESRGAN".
- **Eq. (74) text:** "$I^\alpha$ the invariants" against the subscripted $I_\alpha$ of the equation.
- Bracket typos: "[568], 569]", "[581], 582]" (§4.1.4), "[702], 703]", "[719], 720]" (§4.3.1); Fig. 19 caption "n-docecane".
- Reference list: [244] prints "Michel et al V." inside the author list; [404] prints given names as surnames; [599] "Veláazquez"; [301] and [384] "Repr..".

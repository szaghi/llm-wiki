# Summary

Tang et al. (2023) build explicit algebraic Reynolds-stress closures for
incompressible RANS by [symbolic regression](/methods/symbolic-regression-closure.md).
They use the deep symbolic regression (DSR) method of Petersen et al.: an
LSTM network emits an expression tree token by token. It is trained with a
risk-seeking policy gradient, a [reinforcement-learning](/methods/reinforcement-learning-closure.md)
algorithm that maximizes the reward of the best fraction of sampled
expressions rather than the expected reward. Here reinforcement learning
trains an expression generator offline; no agent acts inside a running
solver. The target is the non-linear part of the anisotropy. The linear
part is kept as the [$k$-$\varepsilon$](/methods/k-epsilon-model.md)
[eddy-viscosity term](/definitions/boussinesq-hypothesis.md) and treated
implicitly. The non-linear part is expanded on Pope's two-dimensional
[integrity basis](/methods/tensor-basis-neural-network.md), three tensors
whose coefficients are functions of two invariants, so Galilean and
rotational invariance hold by construction. DSR returns the three
coefficients as explicit expressions, and a factor $\beta/10$ ($\beta=7$),
"following the suggestion by Shih", is meant to ensure realizability.

The training case is the parameterized [periodic hill](/benchmarks/periodic-hill.md)
with steepness 0.8 (DNS of Xiao et al., $Re=5600$). The closure is
evaluated a priori on its anisotropy, then frozen in OpenFOAM and
re-converged with the linear part ("loose coupling"); the training is
therefore not [model-consistent](/methods/model-consistent-training.md).
It is tested on hills of steepness 0.5 and 1.0 and on a
[backward-facing step](/benchmarks/backward-facing-step.md) at $Re=5000$.
Against the $k$-$\varepsilon$ baseline, the learned closure gives
anisotropy components with the correct sign, barycentric-map anisotropy
closer to DNS, a larger separation bubble and better velocity profiles; for
the steepest hill the improvement is not distinguishable in the
recirculation region. A second reward produces a much more complex model
whose velocity fields are "strikingly close" to those of the first. The
evaluation is [a priori, then a posteriori](/definitions/a-priori-and-a-posteriori-testing.md)
on frozen injection, on two-dimensional cases, against $k$-$\varepsilon$
only.

# Key claims

The source's anisotropy is $\boldsymbol b=\boldsymbol\tau/k-\tfrac23\boldsymbol I$
(Eq. 10), twice the registry's $b_{ij}$ with the same sign. Writing
$\mathsf S_{ij}=(k/\varepsilon)\langle S_{ij}\rangle$ and
$\mathsf W_{ij}=(k/\varepsilon)\langle\Omega_{ij}\rangle$ (Eq. 11; registry §11,
as on [Boussinesq hypothesis](/definitions/boussinesq-hypothesis.md)),
the closure (Eqs. 9, 15–16, 19) is
$R_{ij}=\tfrac23k\delta_{ij}-2\nu_t\langle S_{ij}\rangle+2k\,b^\perp_{ij}$,
i.e. $b_{ij}=-C_\mu\mathsf S_{ij}+b^\perp_{ij}$, with
$b^\perp_{ij}=0.35\sum_{m=1}^{3}G^m(I_1,I_2)\,T^{(m)}_{ij}$,
$T^{(1)}=\mathsf S$, $T^{(2)}=\mathsf S\mathsf W-\mathsf W\mathsf S$,
$T^{(3)}=\mathsf S^2-\tfrac13\operatorname{tr}(\mathsf S^2)\,\mathsf I$,
$I_1=\operatorname{tr}(\mathsf S^2)$, $I_2=\operatorname{tr}(\mathsf W^2)$.
The source writes the linear part as $-2C_\mu\boldsymbol S$ and the
prefactor as $\beta/10=0.7$ ($\beta=7$); $-C_\mu$ and 0.35 are those
factors halved with the anisotropy, and $b^\perp_{ij}$ here is half the
source's $\boldsymbol b^\perp$ *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.

1. DSR combines an LSTM generator with reinforcement learning; unlike gene-expression programming it ensures "all samples adhere to all constraints, without rejecting samples post hoc", and the authors call it deterministic (§I, §II C 1). **Stated**
2. The risk-seeking policy gradient "aims to increase the reward of the top $\varepsilon$ fraction of samples from the distribution, without regard for samples below that threshold" (§II C 1; App. B, Eq. B1). **Stated** (after Petersen et al.)
3. The Reynolds stress is closed as $R_{ij}=\tfrac23k\delta_{ij}-2\nu_t\langle S_{ij}\rangle+2k\,b^\perp_{ij}$ (Eq. 9, printed $+k\boldsymbol b^\perp$ with the doubled anisotropy); only $b^\perp_{ij}$ is learned, the linear part being "taken as classical LEVM and solved implicitly" (§II C 2). **Derived**
4. For statistically two-dimensional flows the coefficients depend on at most two invariants and the basis reduces to three tensors (Eqs. 13–14, after Pope 1975) (§II C 2). **Stated**
5. Splitting the Reynolds stress into an implicit linear part and a non-linear part before injection is "necessary" to avoid ill-conditioning; injecting the full learned stress magnifies small errors during the RANS iterations (§II C 2, citing refs 46, 47, 19). **Stated**
6. The closure is coupled loosely: it is called once, $b^\perp_{ij}$ is frozen, and the linear part is updated until the residuals reconverge (§II D; §II E, step 5). **Stated**
7. Training hyperparameters: LSTM of three hidden layers of 64 units; 20 epochs; batch 640; learning rate 0.001; entropy coefficient 0.005; risk factor 0.05; expression length 4–32; operators $+,-,\times,\div$ (Table II). **Stated**
8. At most three constant placeholders are allowed per expression, because BFGS constant optimization "can be prohibitively expensive" (§III B). **Stated**
9. Training takes about 100 h on a 20-core workstation (Intel Xeon E5-2650) (§III B). **Shown** (reported timing)
10. Non-linear operators such as sin and tan "could slightly improve the accuracy" but give "extremely complex" expressions at higher cost, so only the four arithmetic operators are used (§III B). **Stated**
11. Learned model 1 (reward Eq. 17): $G^1=0.1893I_1+0.2229I_2+0.1176$; $G^2=-0.1036I_1I_2^3-0.05182I_1^2I_2^2+0.1718I_1^2-0.2333$; $G^3=-2.514I_1I_2^4-3.514I_2^3-0.01105I_2^2-2I_1I_2+2.98I_2$, with $\beta=7$ and the invariants mapped to $[-1,1]$ by $(1-e^{-I})/(1+e^{-I})$ (Eqs. 18–20). **Shown**
12. The coefficient of a higher-order basis tensor has a more complex form, with higher-order invariants, than that of a lower-order one (§III B, Eq. 20). **Shown**
13. The coefficients vary with the invariants, unlike sparse-regression and fast-function-extraction closures that "only contain constant coefficients"; the authors claim "a degree of superiority" and a "more robust closure model" (§III B). **Stated** (no comparison run; this description of sparse regression conflicts with that of Duraisamy (2021), see [tensor-basis neural network](/methods/tensor-basis-neural-network.md), Contradictions)
14. A priori on the training hill, the baseline predicts the shear component reasonably but the diagonal components are "far removed" from DNS, especially the wall-normal and spanwise ones near the bottom wall; the learned model "captures the correct sign" of all components, with the largest gains for the streamwise and wall-normal components in separated regions (§III C, Fig. 7). **Shown**
15. On barycentric maps the $k$-$\varepsilon$ anisotropy lies close to the plane-strain line at all six stations; the learned model is closer to DNS with "a tiny minority of outliers" (§III C, Fig. 8). **Shown**
16. The baseline underestimates the separation bubble on the training hill and the learned model enlarges it; at the upstream hillcrest the baseline's relative error is "nearly double larger" than the learned model's (§III C, Fig. 9). **Shown**
17. Difficulty in computing the streamwise normal and/or shear anisotropy is "perhaps the major contributing factor" in the failure of RANS to predict separation (§III C). **Stated**
18. The linear part and $T^{(1)}$ dominate the shear anisotropy, $T^{(2)}$ contributes most to the streamwise normal component and $T^{(3)}$ to the spanwise one (§III C, Fig. 11). **Shown**
19. On the test hills (steepness 0.5 and 1.0) realizability is "well-maintained" and the anisotropy is "markedly improved" at the three stations; the velocity improvement is "not distinguishable" in the recirculation region for steepness 0.5, ascribed to its more severe separation, but present for 1.0, and the bulk flow improves for both (§III D, Figs. 12–14). **Shown**
20. Error histograms give the learned model a larger share of low streamwise-velocity error than the baseline, although it "could produce higher errors in part of the computational domain" (§III D, Fig. 15). **Shown**
21. On the backward-facing step at $Re=5000$ the learned velocity profiles are "much closer" to DNS and experiment in the near-step region shown, with training on one periodic hill only (§III D, Fig. 16). **Shown**
22. Model 2 (reward Eq. 21, $-\log(1+\mathrm{MSE})$) contains higher-order invariants, e.g. $0.5544I_1^2I_2^6$, and more products; model 1 has fewer terms (§III E, Eq. 22). **Shown**
23. Both models satisfy realizability, model 2 without outliers at the stations of Fig. 18(c), and model 2 has "no distinct advantages" over model 1 for the anisotropy (§III E, Figs. 17–18). **Shown**
24. The two models give "strikingly close" velocity fields despite different forms, ascribed to the mean velocity being a first-order statistic and the Reynolds stress a "less converged" second-order one (citing Thompson et al.) (§III E, Fig. 19). **Shown** (closeness) / **Stated** (explanation)
25. The convergence cost with the learned model is "basically the same" as for $k$-$\varepsilon$; each case converges in hundreds of seconds on a multi-core workstation, with 20000 iterations to residuals of about $10^{-10}$ (§III A, Table I). **Stated**
26. The algebraic form can be implemented in existing RANS solvers "without the need to deploy a deep learning environment" (§I, §IV). **Stated**

The baseline (Eqs. 4–8) is the $k$-$\varepsilon$ model with
$\nu_t=C_\mu k^2/\varepsilon$ and $C_\mu=0.09$, $C_{\varepsilon1}=1.44$,
$C_{\varepsilon2}=1.92$, $\sigma_k=1.0$, $\sigma_\varepsilon=1.3$,
"standard values due to Launder and Sharma".

# Printed slips

1. §III C lists the barycentric stations as "x/h = 1.5, x/h = 1.0, x/h = 1.5, x/h = 2.0, x/h = 4.0 and x/h = 7.5"; the Fig. 8 caption and panels give $x/h=0.5$ for the first.
2. The captions of Figs. 17 and 18 read "learned model 2 (Eqs. 20) … compared with … model 1 (Eqs. 22)"; the text defines model 1 by Eq. (20) and model 2 by Eq. (22).
3. §III D cites "reported DNS data50 and experimental data51", while ref. 50 is Jovic & Driver's measurements and ref. 51 Le, Moin & Kim's DNS.
4. Fig. 15 caption: "The relative error is calculated by $U_p-U_{DNS}$", a difference, under an axis labelled "Relative error in streamwise velocity".
5. Eq. (16) writes $\boldsymbol b^\perp=\sum G^m\boldsymbol T^m$ and Eq. (19) $\boldsymbol b^\perp=\frac{\beta}{10}\sum G^m\boldsymbol T^m$ with the same symbols, so the coefficients of Eq. (20) differ from those of Eq. (16) by the factor $\beta/10=0.7$ *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
6. §II C 1 says "The token with the highest probability is then sampled", while Algorithm 1 samples expressions from the distribution $p(\cdot\mid\theta)$.
7. Eq. (B1) prints $J(\theta;\varepsilon)=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim p(\tau|\theta)}[R(\tau)\ge R_\varepsilon(\theta)]$, the expectation of an inequality, while the text describes maximizing the reward of the top $\varepsilon$ fraction (a conditional expectation).
8. §II E step 4 writes the mapping "$g:\omega_i(\boldsymbol S,\boldsymbol R)\mapsto\boldsymbol b^\perp$" with $\omega_i$ undefined; Fig. 11 labels the terms $k\lambda_iT^i$ and "$-k\boldsymbol b^\parallel$", unlike the text's $G^m\boldsymbol T^m$ and $\boldsymbol b^\parallel=-2C_\mu\boldsymbol S$.
9. Typos: "validated a prior" (§III C), "rates of stain" (§II C 2), "both tow fields" (§III C). Ref. 52 (the DEAP library) is cited for "the BFGS algorithm".
10. How the training target $\hat{\boldsymbol b}^\perp$ is computed from DNS is not printed beyond "obtain the non-linear part" (§II E, step 3; a gap, not a slip).

# Summary

Hoy & Bermejo-Moreno (2022) simulate the Mach 3 oblique shock–turbulent
boundary-layer interaction over a thin flexible steel panel of the DLR
experiments of Daub, Willems & Gülhan (2016): $M_\infty=3$, unit Reynolds
number $49.4\times10^6$ m$^{-1}$, incoming thickness $\delta_0=4$ mm, and a
30° wedge rotating to 17.5° in about 15 ms. The paper is peripheral to
closure modelling: its subject is fluid–structure interaction, and the
turbulence modelling enters as the flow solver.

That solver is a finite-volume compressible
[wall-modelled LES](/methods/wall-modelled-les.md) code in arbitrary
Lagrangian–Eulerian form, with the [Vreman](/methods/vreman-model.md)
subgrid model, the Kawai–Larsson
[equilibrium wall-stress model](/methods/equilibrium-wall-model.md)
(matching height $h_{\mathrm{wm}}=0.1\delta_0$, four cells below it against
[log-layer mismatch](/definitions/log-layer-mismatch.md)), a hybrid
ENO/central scheme switched by a shock sensor (no formula printed, a
[numerical-dissipation](/properties/numerical-dissipation.md) device) and
digital-filter inflow turbulence. It is loosely coupled to a geometrically
nonlinear finite-element panel model with mass-proportional damping fitted
to the measured panel displacement, and to a spring-analogy mesh
deformation. The paper argues that
[LES](/definitions/large-eddy-simulation.md) with a wall model reaches the
experimental Reynolds number, span and duration that DNS and wall-resolved
LES cannot, and that RANS cannot predict strong separation; it cites a
[hybrid RANS/LES](/definitions/hybrid-rans-les.md) study of the same
experiment.

A full-span run (about 200 million cells) shows three-dimensional panel
deflection near the free edges but a nearly two-dimensional wall pressure,
which justifies spanwise-periodic runs (about 20 million cells). Damping
brings the panel-deflection histories closer to the experiment than earlier
undamped simulations. Against a rigid wall, the flexible panel lowers the
pressure upstream of the interaction and the peak pressure, moves separation
about $3\delta_0$ upstream, lengthens the separation by about 60%, shifts the
spectra to lower frequencies and lengthens the recovery of the
[turbulent boundary layer](/benchmarks/turbulent-boundary-layer.md) by about
30%. Inviscid and one-way-coupled models capture the upstream pressure drop
but not the separation shift or the lower peak pressure.

# Key claims

1. WMLES coupled with a finite-element solid solver with structural damping allows simulation at the experimental Reynolds number, over the full panel span and for the experimental duration (§1, Impact Statement). **Stated**
2. RANS cannot accurately predict strong separation and the associated low-frequency dynamics of shock–boundary-layer interactions, and DNS and wall-resolved LES are too expensive beyond moderate Reynolds numbers for the long integration times needed (§1, citing Sadagopan et al. 2021). **Stated**
3. WMLES models the inner region of the boundary layer up to about 10% of its thickness (§1, citing Bose & Park 2018; Larsson et al. 2016). **Stated**
4. Bermejo-Moreno et al. (2014) showed WMLES capable of capturing non-equilibrium, separated interactions over adiabatic rigid walls (§1). **Stated**
5. Four uniform cells below the matching height ($\Delta y=0.25h_{\mathrm{wm}}$, $h_{\mathrm{wm}}=0.1\delta_0$) reduce the numerical errors passed from the LES to the wall model and avoid the log-layer mismatch (§3, citing Kawai & Larsson 2012). **Stated**
6. The coupled response has three phases: transient (wedge rotation, largest deflection, bubble formation after a lag of about 1 ms), transition (panel kinetic energy decays over about 8 cycles of the primary mode) and long-term (limit-cycle oscillation; the panel never comes to rest) (§4, Fig. 2). **Shown**
7. In the long-term phase the vibration magnitude scales roughly with the inverse of the damping ratio (§4). **Stated**
8. The full-span run reproduces the measured deflection at the three probes ($x=295$, 375, 445 mm) with reasonable accuracy, best at the centre probe; the front and rear probes are over- and under-predicted near the first two peaks (§4.1, Fig. 3b). **Shown**
9. Mass-proportional damping approximates the damping of the primary mode reasonably (against the undamped simulations of Pasquariello et al. 2015), but higher-frequency vibrations are less damped and take longer to decay (§4.1). **Stated** (comparison **Shown**, Fig. 3b)
10. Three-dimensional effects are largest in the panel deflection near the free edges ($\lvert z'\rvert>15$); skin friction is mainly two-dimensional except near the edges ($\lvert z'\rvert>20$) downstream of impingement, and wall pressure is nearly two-dimensional, so the interaction is treated as statistically two-dimensional (§4.1, Fig. 4). **Shown**
11. Downstream of reattachment ($15\lesssim x'\lesssim40$) the mean skin friction shows streaks, attributed to large shear-layer structures and Görtler-like vortices of spanwise wavelength about $2\delta_0$ found by Pasquariello et al. (2017) (§4.1). **Shown**/**Stated**
12. A longer wedge strengthens the interaction (earlier onset, higher pressure peak); a thicker incoming boundary layer moves the initial pressure rise upstream and lowers the maximum wall pressure (§4.2, Fig. 5). **Shown**
13. The best match to the measured wall pressure is a 6 mm wedge extension ($\xi=1.069$) and an incoming boundary layer 20% thicker than 4 mm ($\eta=1.2$) (§4.2). **Shown**
14. Panel flexibility lowers the wall pressure upstream of impingement, consistently with supersonic flow over a diverging surface, and local piston theory estimates this drop accurately, as in Sullivan & Bodony (2019) (§4.3, Fig. 7). **Shown**
15. Separation lies about $3\delta_0$ further upstream on the flexible panel ($x'\approx-9.4$) than on the rigid wall ($x'\approx-6.4$), and the peak wall pressure is higher on the rigid wall (§4.3, Fig. 8a,b). **Shown**
16. Flexibility increases the separation length by about 60%, $L_{\mathrm{sep}}/\delta_0\approx7.8$ (rigid) against 12.6 (flexible) (§4.3). **Shown**
17. Low-pass-filtered (cut-off $u_\infty/L_{\mathrm{sep}}$) RMS wall-pressure fluctuations peak at separation and at reattachment; on the flexible panel the first peak is further upstream and lower, the second lower and further downstream (§4.3, Fig. 8c,d). **Shown**
18. The panel's fundamental frequency is $f_n\approx230$ Hz, $St_n=f_n\delta_0/U_\infty=0.0017$; the deflection spectrum partly overlaps the low-frequency flow motions (§4.3, Fig. 9). **Shown**
19. Upstream of the interaction the band $St>0.5$ carries over 80% of the wall-pressure power; at the separation-shock onset the band $St<0.05$ reaches about 70% at its peak, for both walls (§4.3, Fig. 10). **Shown**
20. Boundary-layer recovery (to 80% of the upstream high-frequency power share) takes $61\delta_0$ from separation on the flexible panel against about $47\delta_0$ on the rigid wall, about 30% longer (§4.3). **Shown**
21. The separation-bubble volume $V_b/(\delta_0^2Z_d)$ has near-Gaussian distributions with means 9.4 (flexible) and 4.3 (rigid) and standard deviations 0.75 and 0.37, a mean-to-deviation ratio of about 12 in both (§4.3, Fig. 11a). **Shown**
22. Flexibility induces a small negative correlation between bubble volume and streamwise centroid, absent on the rigid wall, and lowers the dominant bubble frequencies, partly collapsed by $St_{\mathrm{sep}}=fL_{\mathrm{sep}}/u_\infty$ (§4.3, Fig. 11). **Shown**
23. Flow-reversal regions are mostly triangular and confined to the boundary layer; flexibility flattens them slightly when scaled by $L_{\mathrm{sep}}$ (§4.3, Fig. 12). **Shown**
24. Two-way inviscid coupling overpredicts the peak wall pressure (no separation) and shifts the pressure load and the deflection downstream, but predicts the upstream pressure drop reasonably (§4.4, Figs. 13–16). **Shown**
25. One-way coupling with a local piston-theory correction predicts the upstream pressure drop and the downstream shift, but not the lower peak pressure nor the upstream move of separation; it overpredicts the peak pressure and gives a smaller bubble, though it improves much on one-way coupling with the rigid-wall WMLES pressure (§4.4, Figs. 14–16). **Shown**
26. As separation grows, piston-theory models may struggle in the separated region, and corrections for the separation growth and the lower peak pressure are needed (§4.4, §5). **Stated**
27. Both the one-way piston-theory and the two-way inviscid models give reasonable long-term mean deflection profiles but slightly overpredict the maximum deflection and its streamwise location (§5). **Shown**

# Printed slips

- **Reference station.** §2: "At the reference station located at x = 210 mm"; Table 1 caption "at the reference location (x0 = 200 mm)"; later in §2 "reference station (located at x = 200 mm)".
- **Wedge-length factor.** §4.2 and the Fig. 5(b) caption give $\xi=1.069$ for the 6 mm extension; the Fig. 5(a) legend lists $\xi=1.024$, 1.048, 1.071, 1.095 for 2 mm steps, whose third value is the 6 mm case.
- **Figure panel.** §4.3: "Figure 10(b) compares the streamwise-averaged PSD of wall pressure …", which by the Fig. 10 caption is panel (d); the text later cites "(figure 10d)" correctly.
- **Band order.** §4.3 assigns the high-, medium- and low-frequency bands to Figs. 10(a), (b), (c) "respectively", then lists the same bands in the reverse order in the next sentence.
- **$St_n$.** §4.3 prints $St_n=f_n\delta_0/U_\infty=0.0017$ with $f_n\approx230$ Hz; with Table 1 ($\delta_0=4$ mm, $u_\infty=595$ m s$^{-1}$), $230\times0.004/595=1.55\times10^{-3}$, and with the adjusted thickness $1.2\delta_0=4.8$ mm, $1.86\times10^{-3}$ *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
- **Midspan origin.** §4.1 and Fig. 6 write "midspan plane (z = 0 mm)"; elsewhere the centreline is $z=Z_d/2$, consistent with $z'=(z-Z_d/2)/\delta_0$.
- **Undefined and reused symbols.** The damping ratio $\zeta$ is used in §4 without definition; $\eta$ is both the boundary-layer thickness factor (§4.2) and the band power fraction (Fig. 10).

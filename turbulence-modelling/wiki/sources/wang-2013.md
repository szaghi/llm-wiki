# Summary

Wang et al. (2013) report the 1st International Workshop on High-Order CFD
Methods (Nashville, 7–8 January 2012), organized by the CFD Algorithm
Discussion Group of the AIAA Fluid Dynamics Technical Committee with the
European ADIGMA project. The paper says explicitly that it does not review
high-order methods: it measures them against second-order production
methods "as fairly as possible", on the basis of error against cost rather
than error on a given mesh. It rebuts the beliefs that high-order methods
are expensive and not needed for engineering accuracy, reports a survey
(high order means third order and above), gives method-independent
definitions of the residual (finite volume, DG, finite difference) and of
the $L_2$ error, and defines a non-dimensional cost, the work unit, from the
TauBench benchmark.

It specifies fifteen cases in three difficulty classes, from the inviscid
bump channel, Ringleb flow, the [NACA 0012](/benchmarks/naca-0012-airfoil.md)
and vortex transport, through the RANS [RAE 2822](/benchmarks/rae-2822-airfoil.md)
case 9 (with [Spalart–Allmaras](/methods/spalart-allmaras-model.md),
[Wilcox $k$-$\omega$](/methods/k-omega-model.md) or EARSM, an
[explicit algebraic Reynolds-stress model](/methods/explicit-algebraic-reynolds-stress-model.md))
and a laminar delta wing, to the 30P-30N airfoil, the DPW III wing, the
[SD7003](/benchmarks/sd7003-airfoil.md) [implicit LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md) of a
[laminar separation bubble](/definitions/laminar-separation-bubble.md), and
the [Taylor–Green vortex](/benchmarks/taylor-green-vortex.md) at $Re=1600$
as a [DNS](/definitions/direct-numerical-simulation.md), with its
dissipation diagnostics and a $512^3$ pseudo-spectral reference. Results
are shown for the bump, NACA 0012, vortex transport, RAE 2822, the delta
wing and the Taylor–Green vortex, from DG, [flux reconstruction](/methods/flux-reconstruction.md)
(CPR), [DGSEM](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin-spectral-element-method.md),
reconstructed finite volumes and central and
[DRP](/methods/low-dissipation-low-dispersion-scheme.md) finite differences.

For smooth problems $N=3$ beats $N=1$ and the second-order DLR TAU finite
volume code in error against work units; for non-smooth problems
(trailing-edge and leading-edge singularities, shocks) high-order rates are
not reached and the evidence cannot rank high against low order;
output-based and solution-based [hp-adaptation](/methods/mesh-adaptation.md)
is very effective; high-order RANS is less robust than low-order RANS in
reaching steady state, which the authors attribute to non-smooth turbulence
models. The pacing items are curved meshing, hp-adaptation, scalable
low-memory time integration and parameter-free
[shock capturing](/methods/artificial-viscosity-shock-capturing.md). Work
units are declared uncertain to about 30%.

# Key claims

The polynomial degree is $N$ (the source's $p$; $N=1$ and $N=3$ are
formally second and fourth order).

1. In the aerospace community "high order" means third order or higher, the unanimous answer of the survey (§1, §2 Q1). **Stated**
2. Efficiency must be compared at equal error, not on the same mesh; on that basis "high-order methods are not necessarily expensive" (§1, Belief 1). **Stated**
3. Halving the mesh size and time step in 3-D multiplies the cost by about 16; reducing the error by a factor of 4 takes 256 times more degrees of freedom with a first-order method and 16 times with a second-order one (§1, Belief 2). **Derived**
4. A 5% velocity error may become a 20% or larger skin-friction error; for helicopter flows a 5% drag error may require the tip-vortex strength within 5% over four to eight revolutions (§1). **Stated**
5. Survey answers: high-order methods are "routinely chosen for LES and DNS of turbulent flows"; the turbulence-model equations should be discretized at high order, and "Defects in turbulence models may be exposed in this exercise"; no completely satisfactory high-order limiter is known (§2, Q2, Q4, Q5). **Stated**
6. The residual is defined method-independently for finite volumes (Eq. 4), DG (Eq. 7) and nodal finite differences (Eq. 9), with the $L_2$ norm of Eq. (5); the density residual is monitored, but it is machine zero for the flat plate in uniform free stream, so the energy residual is recommended in future (§3.1). **Stated** (definition)
7. One work unit is the CPU time of TauBench for ten steps with 250 000 degrees of freedom; parallel CPU time is multiplied by the number of processors (§3.2). **Stated**
8. Reference force coefficients must be hp-independent to 0.01 counts ($10^{-6}$ in $C_L$ and $C_D$), and the far-field location must change them by less than 0.01 counts (§3.2, §4.8). **Stated**
9. Fifteen cases are defined in three categories, C1 (easy) to C3 (difficult) (§4). **Stated**
10. Bump channel (C1.1): $N=3$ is "across the board, more efficient in DOFs" than $N=1$; $N=1$ is similar to the TAU second-order reference and $N=3$ from nearly all participants outperforms it in degrees of freedom and work units; ideal asymptotic rates are reached (§5.1, Fig. 15). **Shown**
11. The spread across participants in error against $h$ is smaller at $N=3$ than at $N=1$; the spread in work units is large for both, "indicating that implementation and coding vary significantly" (§5.1–5.2). **Shown**
12. Inviscid subcritical NACA 0012: $N=3$ is more efficient than $N=1$ in degrees of freedom and work units even at moderate error; asymptotic high-order rates are not reached under uniform refinement because of the trailing-edge singularity; MIT's fixed-DOF output-based mesh optimization gives the highest rates, at least for drag (§5.2, Figs. 16–17). **Shown**
13. The NACA 0012 results are "qualitative in nature": participants made their own reference solutions without common standards, and the scatter of those references is similar to the errors under refinement (§5.2). **Stated**
14. Transonic NACA 0012: the gain of $N=2$ over $N=1$ is smaller than for smooth problems, but $N=2$ "does not fare worse" in degrees of freedom or work units; adaptive refinement generally beats uniform refinement (§5.2, Figs. 18–19). **Shown**
15. Viscous NACA 0012 ($Re=5000$): $N=1$ is comparable with TAU and $N=3$ generally better, especially at high accuracy; the residual criterion was relaxed from ten to eight orders of magnitude (§5.2, Figs. 20–21). **Shown**
16. Vortex transport (C1.6, DG submissions only, against second-order STAR-CD): high order in both space and time is needed for optimal convergence rates, and $N=3$ "significantly outperform[s]" $N=1$ (§5.3, Fig. 22). **Shown**
17. RAE 2822 RANS (C2.2; three groups, all DG; SA, EARSM, Wilcox $k$-$\omega$): "a large spread among the groups and virtually no improvement from high order under uniform mesh refinement", attributed partly to poor wake alignment of the initial mesh and partly to the turbulence model; adaptive mesh optimization is "markedly superior" (§5.4, Fig. 23). **Shown**
18. Laminar delta wing (C2.4): high order shows some gain in degrees of freedom but is comparable with low order in work units, because the leading-edge singularity precludes high-order rates; UM and DLR (both BR2 on a tensor-product space) are "almost identical" in accuracy against degrees of freedom, "a perfect cross-code verification", but differ greatly in work units because of the solver strategy (§5.5, Fig. 24). **Shown**
19. Taylor–Green vortex (C3.5): the transition at $Re=1600$ "is sufficiently robust such that it is not initiated by truncation errors", so a detailed temporal comparison is possible (§5.6). **Stated**
20. The reference is a dealiased incompressible pseudo-spectral solution, grid-converged on $512^3$ (low-storage three-step Runge–Kutta, $\Delta t=10^{-3}$); adequate resolution is already reached at $256^3$, the baseline requirement, which "seems to correspond to the Nyquist criterion" (§4.15, §5.6). **Stated** (convergence study not displayed)
21. The high-order finite-difference schemes (4th, 8th, 12th order) and the finite-volume scheme do not reach their projected orders for any error measure; the DG-like schemes "seem to obtain the correct order of convergence" for error types 1 and 3; type 2 "seems to evolve suboptimally for all schemes" (§5.6, Figs. 25–27). **Shown**
22. The 12th-order central finite-difference error is comparable with that of the formally 4th-order DRP scheme, and the standard 4th-order error is much higher, which "seems to indicate that the dispersion error already dominates", consistent with DG being "dissipative but [having] particularly good dispersive accuracy" (§5.6). **Shown** (interpretation **Stated**)
23. Unstructured methods reach the same effort-based convergence rate as the most precise finite-difference methods, shifted toward higher cost and precision: "For sufficiently high convergence requirements, unstructured methods are competitive with FDMs" (§5.6). **Shown**
24. In Beck's DGSEM study at constant equivalent resolution, spatial convergence is spectral at constant cost; this holds for explicit time integration only and cannot be generalized to implicit integration (§5.6). **Shown** (explicit Runge–Kutta)
25. The third error (measured minus theoretical dissipation) needs no reference solution and would be zero for any truly kinetic-energy-preserving method such as skew-symmetric central differences, which in practice add dissipation through dealiasing filters (§5.6). **Stated**
26. Work-unit costs of the same case on different computers differ by up to 30%, so no efficiency conclusion should be drawn from cost differences below 30% (§6). **Stated** (from the workshop data)
27. For smooth solutions and geometries, high-order methods beat the second-order finite-volume TAU code in error against cost, for steady and unsteady 2-D cases; a similar 3-D conclusion is "expected" (§6, conclusion 1). **Shown** (2-D) / **Stated** (3-D)
28. For non-smooth problems high order cannot reach high-order accuracy, and the evidence is insufficient to rank it against low order; high order "might require more computer memory" (§6, conclusion 2). **Stated**
29. For RANS, high-order methods are still less robust than low-order ones in converging to steady state; this is "believed" to be related to non-smoothness in turbulence models, ad hoc fixes are used, and "smooth turbulence models are needed" (§6, conclusion 4). **Stated**
30. Line-implicit solvers handle the stiffness of highly anisotropic near-wall meshes, but "the memory requirement scales as $p^6$" ($N^6$) (§6, pacing item 3). **Stated**

# Printed slips

- **Ringleb flow (§4.2):** "the variable $q$ varies between $q_0=0.5$ and
  $k$", then "Again, $q_0=0.4$, $k_{\min}=0.7$, $k_{\max}=1.5$": two values
  of $q_0$.
- **Tandem airfoils, case B (§4.7):** "Let
  $s(\mathbf r)=(\mathbf r-(\delta_2,0))\cdot(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$ with
  $\delta_1=0.5$": the formula uses $\delta_2$, the value is assigned to
  $\delta_1$, which case A sets to 0.05.
- **Radial expansion wave (§4.5):** the domain is printed
  "$[4,4]\times[4,4]$", with a third factor $[4,4]$ in parentheses for
  3-D, degenerate intervals; the "Overview"
  field contains only "Boundary conditions.".
- **Vortex transport (§4.6):** the free-stream pressure is printed
  "$P_\infty=105$ N/m2".
- **Viscous NACA 0012 (§4.3):** "$\alpha=1$" without a degree sign, where
  the other conditions print degrees.
- **Section reference (§1):** the benchmark cases are said to be presented
  "in Section 3"; they are in Section 4 (Section 3 holds the error and cost
  definitions).
- **Order symbol (§1):** "proportional to the mesh size $h$ to the power K,
  that is, $e\propto h^k$".
- **TGV error count (§5.6):** "Two errors with respect to the reference
  results were defined", followed by a third error, which is not relative
  to the reference.
- **DRP attribution (§5.6):** the fourth-order DRP scheme is attributed to
  "Bogey and Bailly", who are not in the reference list, and "DRP methods
  [35]" cites Kim and Lee, "Optimized compact finite difference schemes
  with maximum resolution"; the reference titled
  "Dispersion-relation-preserving" is [6], Tam and Webb.
- **Spelling:** "Boieng Company" (affiliation 6), "University of
  Stuttgard" (Table VII; the text has "Stuttgart"), "mean cord length"
  (§4.10).

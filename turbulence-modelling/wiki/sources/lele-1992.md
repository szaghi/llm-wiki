# Summary

Lele (1992) constructs families of
[compact finite-difference schemes](/methods/compact-finite-difference-scheme.md):
implicit central relations coupling the derivative at up to five nodes to
function values at up to seven, for first, second, third and fourth
derivatives on uniform grids, for derivatives at mid-cell points, for
mid-point interpolation and for low-pass filtering. Matching Taylor
coefficients gives a hierarchy from fourth to tenth order whose
maximal-order members are the Padé approximants. The emphasis is on
resolution rather than formal order: each central scheme has a real modified
wavenumber, the resolving efficiency is the fraction of the wavenumber range
resolved within a tolerance, and imposing exact wavenumbers at chosen points
instead of maximal order gives formally fourth-order "spectral-like" schemes
that resolve about 80–90% of the range; later spectrally optimized
stencils are collected on
[low-dissipation low-dispersion schemes](/methods/low-dissipation-low-dispersion-scheme.md).

For non-periodic problems the paper gives one-sided explicit and compact
boundary closures (with complex modified wavenumbers), a conservative
boundary formulation that turns the scheme into a discrete conservation law
with implied quadrature weights, numerical tests with random-phase
multi-mode functions, and a semi-discrete eigenvalue analysis of the whole
operator, related to [eigensolution analysis](/methods/eigensolution-analysis.md),
which finds no unstable eigenvalues. The compact low-pass filters of the
appendix, designed through their transfer function, are proposed against
the aliasing errors of nonlinear problems on non-periodic domains (see [explicit modal filtering](/methods/explicit-modal-filtering.md)).
The central schemes have no dissipative error in the interior; the paper
insists they be applied only where the shortest scales are set physically,
since they carry no built-in [numerical dissipation](/properties/numerical-dissipation.md),
and demonstrates them on a compressible spatially developing mixing layer and
the viscous structure of a Mach 1.5 normal shock.

# Key claims

Page-local symbols: $\phi_i$ a grid function on a uniform mesh of spacing $h$
and $N_x$ points, primes its discrete derivatives; $\alpha_{\mathrm c}$,
$\beta_{\mathrm c}$ the left-side coefficients and $a_{\mathrm c}$,
$b_{\mathrm c}$, $c_{\mathrm c}$ the right-side coefficients of the
differences over $2h$, $4h$, $6h$ (the source's $\alpha,\beta,a,b,c$); $kh$
the scaled wavenumber and $k'h$, $k''h^2$ the modified wavenumbers of the
first and second derivative (the source's $w$, $w'$, $w''$); $e_1$, $e_2$
their resolving efficiencies at tolerance $\epsilon_{\mathrm r}$;
$c_{\mathrm{ph}}$ the numerical phase speed; $\sigma$ a
semi-discrete eigenvalue; $s_{\mathrm i}$, $s_{\mathrm r}$ the
imaginary- and real-axis extents of a Runge–Kutta stability region;
$a$ the advection speed; $\alpha_{\mathrm b}$ and $a_{\mathrm b},b_{\mathrm b},c_{\mathrm b},d_{\mathrm b}$ the
left-side coefficient and one-sided weights of a boundary closure; $Re_{\mathrm s}$ the shock Reynolds number.

1. The relation $\beta_{\mathrm c}\phi'_{i-2}+\alpha_{\mathrm c}\phi'_{i-1}+\phi'_i+\alpha_{\mathrm c}\phi'_{i+1}+\beta_{\mathrm c}\phi'_{i+2}=c_{\mathrm c}\frac{\phi_{i+3}-\phi_{i-3}}{6h}+b_{\mathrm c}\frac{\phi_{i+2}-\phi_{i-2}}{4h}+a_{\mathrm c}\frac{\phi_{i+1}-\phi_{i-1}}{2h}$ (Eq. 2.1) with the second- and fourth-order constraints (2.1.1)–(2.1.2) is a three-parameter family of fourth-order schemes, tridiagonal for $\beta_{\mathrm c}=0$; sixth order leaves two parameters, eighth order one, and one tenth-order scheme exists (§2.1, Table I). **Derived**
2. For $\beta_{\mathrm c}=c_{\mathrm c}=0$ the fourth-order family is $a_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac23(\alpha_{\mathrm c}+2)$, $b_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac13(4\alpha_{\mathrm c}-1)$; $\alpha_{\mathrm c}\to0$ is the explicit fourth-order difference, $\alpha_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac14$ the classical Padé scheme, $\alpha_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac13$ the sixth-order scheme with $a_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac{14}9$, $b_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac19$ (Eqs. 2.1.6–2.1.7). **Derived**
3. The eighth-order tridiagonal member is $\alpha_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac38$ in (2.1.8), "the tridiagonal scheme … with the highest formal accuracy"; the tenth-order pentadiagonal scheme has $\alpha_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac12$, $\beta_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac1{20}$, $a_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac{17}{12}$, $b_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac{101}{150}$, $c_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac1{100}$, truncation error $\tfrac{144}{11!}h^{10}\phi^{(11)}$ (Eqs. 2.1.8, 2.1.14). **Derived**
4. The maximal-order schemes for a given stencil are the Padé approximants tabulated by Kopal, whose coefficients of $\delta^4$ and $\delta^6$ in $D^{(0)}_2$ and $D^{(0)}_3$ "are in error, they should read as $\tfrac1{180}$ and $\tfrac1{1512}$" (verified with MACSYMA by K. Shariff) (§2.1, footnote 1). **Stated**
5. The second-derivative relation (2.2) gives the fourth-order tridiagonal family $a_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac43(1-\alpha_{\mathrm c})$, $b_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac13(10\alpha_{\mathrm c}-1)$ (Padé at $\alpha_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac1{10}$), the sixth-order member $\alpha_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac2{11}$, $a_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac{12}{11}$, $b_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac3{11}$, and one tenth-order scheme (Eqs. 2.2.6–2.2.11). **Derived**
6. A central scheme has the real modified wavenumber $k'h=\dfrac{a_{\mathrm c}\sin kh+(b_{\mathrm c}/2)\sin2kh+(c_{\mathrm c}/3)\sin3kh}{1+2\alpha_{\mathrm c}\cos kh+2\beta_{\mathrm c}\cos2kh}$ (Eq. 3.1.4) and no dissipative error in the interior. **Derived**
7. First-derivative $e_1$ at $\epsilon_{\mathrm r}=0.1/0.01/0.001$ (Table III): second-order central 0.25/0.08/0.02; fourth-order central 0.44/0.23/0.13; sixth-order central 0.54/0.35/0.23; Padé 0.59/0.35/0.20; sixth-order tridiagonal 0.70/0.50/0.35; eighth-order tridiagonal 0.75/0.58/0.44; eighth-order pentadiagonal 0.77/0.61/0.48; tenth-order pentadiagonal 0.81/0.68/0.56; spectral-like (3.1.6) 0.90/0.83/0.79. **Shown** (computed)
8. A formally fourth-order spectral-like pentadiagonal first derivative, from $k'h=kh$ at $kh=2.2$, 2.3, 2.4: $\alpha_{\mathrm c}=0.5771439$, $\beta_{\mathrm c}=0.0896406$, $a_{\mathrm c}=1.3025166$, $b_{\mathrm c}=0.9935500$, $c_{\mathrm c}=0.03750245$; "no attempt was made to optimize" the three points (Eqs. 3.1.5–3.1.6). **Derived**
9. Within the fourth-order tridiagonal family a member labelled (d) resolves better than the sixth-order $\alpha_{\mathrm c}=\tfrac13$ member at the two smaller tolerances (Table IV: 0.59/0.52 against 0.50/0.35); improvements of the dispersive error over $(\pi/2,\pi)$ are "not very sensitive" to the formal truncation error, which the author anticipates "to be a general feature" (§3.1, Fig. 2); the value of $\alpha_{\mathrm c}$ for (d) is printed differently in text and caption (Printed slips). **Shown**
10. The phase speed is $c_{\mathrm{ph}}=k'h/kh$; in 2D it is anisotropic, $c_{\mathrm{ph}}(kh,\theta)=[\cos\theta\,k'h(kh\cos\theta)+\sin\theta\,k'h(kh\sin\theta)]/kh$, with least error along ±45°; for the spectral-like scheme only the shortest 20% of waves feel it (§3.1, Eqs. 3.1.7–3.1.9, Figs. 3–4). **Shown**
11. Better resolution entails possibly larger aliasing errors in nonlinear problems, expected to be akin to spectral methods and removable by dealiasing or spectral-like filtering; "more research is needed" (§3.1). **Stated**
12. Second-derivative $e_2$ (Table V) ranges from 0.35/0.11/0.03 (second-order central) to 1.00/0.89/0.84 (spectral-like 3.1.12); the largest error, near $kh=\pi$, depends weakly on formal order (§3.1, Fig. 5). **Shown**
13. A formally fourth-order spectral-like pentadiagonal second derivative: $\alpha_{\mathrm c}=0.50209266$, $\beta_{\mathrm c}=0.05569169$, $a_{\mathrm c}=0.21564935$, $b_{\mathrm c}=1.7233220$, $c_{\mathrm c}=0.17659730$, from $k''h^2=(kh)^2$ at $kh=2$, 2.4, 2.6 (Eqs. 3.1.12–3.1.13). **Derived**
14. Boundary closures $\phi'_1+\alpha_{\mathrm b}\phi'_2=h^{-1}(a_{\mathrm b}\phi_1+b_{\mathrm b}\phi_2+c_{\mathrm b}\phi_3+d_{\mathrm b}\phi_4)$ of second, third and fourth order (Eqs. 4.1.1–4.1.4): even orders have dispersive, third order dissipative leading error; the second-order compact $\alpha_{\mathrm b}=1$, $d_{\mathrm b}=0$ closure is non-dissipative but singular at $kh=\pi$; the third-order compact $\alpha_{\mathrm b}=2$ closure has small dispersive error and $\tfrac19$ of the explicit third-order truncation error (§4.1, Figs. 6–7). **Derived** / **Shown**
15. A second-order compact closure with virtually no dissipative error follows from a zero imaginary part at $kh=\pi$, i.e. $d_{\mathrm b}=-\tfrac12$; "the desired characteristics … are better achieved by directly optimizing the scheme (in Fourier space) rather than by seeking the scheme with the lowest possible truncation error" (§4.1, p. 26). **Shown**
16. Many compact closures have an imaginary modified wavenumber of opposite sign to explicit one-sided ones (at $\pi$: $4(2d_{\mathrm b}+1)/(1-\alpha_{\mathrm b})$ for (4.1.2)); it is "erroneous" to infer instability from this — stability needs the eigenvalues of the complete scheme (§4.1). **Derived**
17. A conservative formulation, in which columns 2 to $N_x-1$ of the right-side matrix sum to zero, leaves only boundary fluxes and implies quadrature weights near the boundary; it needs at least 10 nodes with $c_{\mathrm c}=0$ and 14 with $c_{\mathrm c}\ne0$ (§4.2, Eqs. 4.2.1–4.2.18). **Derived**
18. The conservative formulation carries over to a mapped non-uniform mesh with $dx/ds$ in the weights (§4.2, Eq. 4.2.14). **Derived**
19. The third-order compact second-derivative closure $\phi''_1+11\phi''_2=h^{-2}(13\phi_1-27\phi_2+15\phi_3-\phi_4)$ has truncation error $\tfrac1{12}h^3\phi^{(5)}$, 10 times smaller than the explicit third-order form (§4.3, Eqs. 4.3.1–4.3.3). **Derived**
20. With random-phase multi-mode functions on 129 points (sixth-order tridiagonal interior, conservative form, third-order compact closure), the dominant error is always at the boundary and "even in these cases the interior is virtually error-free" (§4.4, Fig. 8). **Shown**
21. For the tridiagonal family with conservative or non-conservative compact closures or explicit closures, all semi-discrete eigenvalues of advection on $N_x$ up to 200 satisfy the stability constraint; the least negative real part approaches the imaginary axis as $N_x^{-3}$, as Warming and Beam predicted for a second-order interior (§4.5, Figs. 9–10). **Shown**
22. Exception: the Padé interior with the $\alpha_{\mathrm b}=4$, $d_{\mathrm b}=-\tfrac12$ closure has an eigenvalue moving to the origin slower than $N_x^{-1}$ (a generalized GKS zero eigenvalue); no unstable eigenvalues occur (§4.5). **Shown**
23. With a fixed interior and $N_x$, raising the boundary order shifts the spectrum to more negative eigenvalues — "a caution against associating the eigenvalue spectrum with the overall performance (accuracy)" (§4.5, Fig. 10). **Shown**
24. Explicit time-step limits: $a\Delta t/h\les_{\mathrm i}/\max k'h$ and $\nu\Delta t/h^2\les_{\mathrm r}/\max k''h^2$, with $s_{\mathrm i}=0,\sqrt3,2.85$ and $s_{\mathrm r}=2,2.5,2.9$ for second- to fourth-order Runge–Kutta, and the printed lists of maxima for the schemes of Figs. 1 and 5 (§4.6, Eqs. 4.6.1–4.6.5). **Derived**
25. The schemes have no built-in artificial dissipation and must be used where the shortest scales are "determined physically and not numerically", ruling out direct use with discontinuities; viscous terms should use the second-derivative operator, which damps $2h$ waves that repeated first derivatives leave undamped (§5). **Stated**
26. Adapted Thompson non-reflecting boundary conditions perform well; conditions suited to low-order dissipative schemes "may not perform well" (§5). **Stated**
27. Compressible mixing layer (Mach 2.0 and 1.2 streams, convective Mach 0.4, $601\times161$ points): roll-up and pairing are captured and scalar gradients in braids resolved with 7–8 points (§5, Fig. 11). **Shown**
28. Mach 1.5 normal shock with 7–8 points across: smooth profiles; for weak shocks the structure "agrees perfectly" with the analytical solution; $Re_{\mathrm s}\approx6$–7 against the weak-shock value 6.222; maximum-gradient thicknesses underestimate the visual extent by about 3 (§5, Figs. 12–13). **Shown**
29. The schemes are not monotone: a step gives $2h$ oscillations damped exponentially away from it, against the algebraic $x^{-1}$ decay of spectral Gibbs oscillations (§5). **Stated**
30. Cell-centred compact schemes resolve better near $kh=\pi$ than collocated ones; on a cell-centred mesh the pentadiagonal schemes are "virtually indistinguishable" from exact differentiation (App. B, Figs. 16–17). **Shown**
31. Compact interpolation and filters are designed through their transfer function $\hat G(kh)$, with $\hat G(\pi)=0$ for filters; tridiagonal compact filters are "much better low pass filters" than explicit ones, and the filtering is confined to shorter waves as $\alpha_{\mathrm{cf}}$ approaches 0.5 (App. C, Figs. 18–20). **Shown**

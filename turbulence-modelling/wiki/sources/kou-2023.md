# Summary

Kou et al. (2023) compare two interface penalties that stabilize high-order
discontinuous Galerkin (DG) schemes run as
[implicit LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md) of under-resolved turbulence.
The **solution jump penalty** penalizes the jump of the solution across
element faces (an enlarged interior-penalty parameter, attributed to Ferrer);
the **gradient jump penalty** penalizes the jump of the solution gradient
(the gradient-jump penalty of Moura et al., from continuous interior
penalty). Both add symmetric positive semi-definite face terms, independent
of the time discretization, and act as localized
[numerical dissipation](/properties/numerical-dissipation.md). The paper is
a comparison and parameter study, not a new scheme; the methods are gathered
on [jump-penalty stabilization](/methods/jump-penalty-stabilization.md).

The authors write both penalties for 1D advection–diffusion with a modal
Legendre DG scheme and for the 3D compressible Navier–Stokes equations in
[HORSES3D](/software/horses3d.md), a
[DG spectral element](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin-spectral-element-method.md)
solver, where the penalty is scaled by $1/Re$ and a selection matrix
chooses the penalized equations. A temporal eigensolution (von Neumann)
analysis of linear advection with upwind and central fluxes for $N=2,3,4$
selects representative penalty values: a positive solution penalty and,
with an upwind flux, a small negative gradient penalty improve dispersion at
medium–high wavenumbers and move dissipation to high wavenumbers. In forced
Burgers turbulence both penalties extend the resolved wavenumber range. On
the [Taylor–Green vortex](/benchmarks/taylor-green-vortex.md) ($Re=1600$,
$M=0.1$, $32^3$ elements, $N=3$) the unpenalized scheme diverges, both
penalties remove a spurious second peak of the dissipation rate once they
are large enough (the gradient penalty is then positive), and both
outperform the [Smagorinsky](/methods/smagorinsky-model.md) and
[Vreman](/methods/vreman-model.md) models adapted to DG, whose constants are
not stated. On a 2D compressible Kelvin–Helmholtz instability only the
solution penalty completes the run. [Spectral vanishing viscosity](/methods/spectral-vanishing-viscosity.md)
is mentioned as the alternative stabilization, and the paper frames the whole
study as [LES](/definitions/large-eddy-simulation.md) with an implicit
subgrid model. All evidence is numerical; nothing is proved.

# Key claims

Page-local symbols, as on [jump-penalty stabilization](/methods/jump-penalty-stabilization.md):
$\tau_s$ and $\tau_g$ are the solution- and gradient-jump penalty parameters
(the source's symbols, unrelated to the SGS stress $\tau_{ij}$),
$\sigma_{\mathrm{IP}}$ the three-dimensional penalty coefficient, $N_e$ the
number of 1D elements; $\lambda_{\mathrm{up}}$ is the upwinding parameter of
the numerical flux ($0$ central, $1$ upwind).

1. The jump penalty stabilization "acts as an implicit subgrid model and stabilizes under-resolved turbulent simulations"; the authors treat it as implicit LES while comparing it with explicit SGS models (Abstract, §1). **Stated**
2. For scalar linear advection–diffusion, the jump penalty combines with the upwinding term: positive penalties increase the damping of upwinding, negative ones decrease it, and large negative values make the scheme unstable ("effectively adding anti-diffusion") (§2.1, end). **Stated**
3. The global penalty matrices with entries $\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{IP}}}{Re}\int_\Gamma[\![\phi_j]\!][\![\phi_i]\!]$ and $\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{IP}}h^2}{Re}\int_\Gamma[\![\nabla\phi_j]\!][\![\nabla\phi_i]\!]\cdot\boldsymbol n$ are "symmetric and positive semi-definite and therefore provide dissipation of energy to the numerical scheme" (§2.2, end). **Stated** (no proof; refers to [2])
4. With upwind flux and $N=3$, a negative $\tau_g$ gives less dissipation at medium wavenumbers, a positive $\tau_g$ is too dissipative there; a positive $\tau_s$ reduces dissipation at medium wavenumbers and increases it at high wavenumbers, more so as it grows (§3, Figs. 1–2). **Shown**
5. For the gradient penalty with upwind flux, $\tau_g<-1\times10^{-3}$ makes the dissipation positive (instability), so $\tau_g$ must be limited (§3, Fig. 3). **Shown**
6. A positive $\tau_s$ induces no numerical instability; from the dissipation curve the optimal $\tau_s$ "should be as large as possible" (§3, Fig. 4). **Shown**
7. As the solution penalty grows, the DG eigenvalues split into a set converging to the continuous Galerkin (CG) eigenvalues and a set of large real part (pure dissipation), recovering [53] (§3). **Shown** (recovers a cited result)
8. Representative penalties with upwind flux: $\tau_g=-3\times10^{-3}$, $-1\times10^{-3}$, $-4\times10^{-4}$ for $N=2,3,4$; $\tau_s=0.25$ for all three degrees (Table 1). **Shown**
9. Representative penalties with central flux: $\tau_g=1.2\times10^{-2}$, $4\times10^{-3}$, $2\times10^{-3}$ for $N=2,3,4$; $\tau_s=0.75$ for all (Table C.2); $\tau_s$ is 0.5 higher than with upwind flux and $\tau_g$ becomes positive (App. C.1). **Shown**
10. With central flux, $\tau_s=0.5$ gives dispersion–dissipation curves identical to standard DG with upwind flux ($\lambda_{\mathrm{up}}=1$), the known equivalence [54] (§3, App. C.1, Fig. C.18). **Shown**
11. A solution penalty below $-0.5$ produces anti-diffusion in the von Neumann analysis, associated with divergence in simulations (§3). **Shown**
12. Forced Burgers turbulence ($N_e=400$, $N=3$, 1600 DOFs, Roe flux): both penalties extend the resolved wavenumber range; too large penalties keep excess energy at high wavenumbers and lose the $-2$ slope; optimal $\tau_g=-8\times10^{-4}$, $\tau_s=1.5$ (§4.1, Figs. 6–8). **Shown**
13. In the Burgers test the gradient jump penalty shows "slightly better behaviour than the solution jump, as it maintains the slope −2 for higher wavenumbers" (§4.1, Fig. 8). **Shown**
14. Burgers at $N=2$ and $N=4$ ($N_e=400$): both penalties still improve standard DG, and their effect shrinks from $N=2$ to $N=4$ "since the continuity across the element interface is better maintained at high polynomial orders"; optimal $\tau_g=-2\times10^{-3}$, $\tau_s=1.5$ at $N=2$ and $\tau_g=-3\times10^{-4}$, $\tau_s=1.5$ at $N=4$ (App. D.1, Figs. D.23–D.24). **Shown**
15. Refined Burgers ($N_e=819$, $N=4$): optimal $\tau_g=-4\times10^{-4}$, $\tau_s=1.5$; with central flux standard DG blows up while the jump penalties stabilize it; gradient–central ($\tau_g=0.005$) does not maintain the $-2$ slope well, and the other three combinations give similar spectra (App. D.2, Fig. D.27). **Shown**
16. Taylor–Green vortex ($Re=1600$, $M=0.1$, $32^3$ elements, $N=3$): classic DG without jump penalty diverges, which the authors relate, following Gassner et al. [61], to aliasing errors of under-resolved simulation (§4.2). **Shown** (divergence) / **Stated** (cause)
17. Taylor–Green vortex, solution jump: for low penalties the kinetic-energy dissipation rate has two peaks, at $t/t_c=9$ and $11$, against one DNS peak at $t/t_c=9$; for $\tau_s>1.9$ the second peak disappears but the first exceeds the DNS value (§4.2, Fig. 10a). **Shown**
18. Taylor–Green vortex, gradient jump: for $\tau_g>6.3\times10^{-4}$ the second peak disappears; the largest penalty recovers the peak magnitude at $t/t_c=9$ but oscillates later (§4.2, Fig. 10b). **Shown**
19. The penalty values of the Taylor–Green vortex are larger than in the Burgers case because of the $1/Re$ scaling of §2.2; very large penalties may need implicit treatment (§4.2). **Stated**
20. Taylor–Green spectra at $t/t_c=8$: the solution penalty value has no significant effect, while large gradient penalties make energy decay significantly faster (§4.2, Fig. 11). **Shown**
21. Both jump penalties are "clearly superior" to the Smagorinsky [8] and Vreman [9] models adapted to DG on the Taylor–Green vortex: the classic models give a lower maximum dissipation peak, a higher dissipation rate before $t/t_c=8$, and dissipate significantly at mid wavenumbers; the solution jump captures higher wavenumbers (§4.2, Fig. 12). **Shown** (model constants not stated)
22. Kelvin–Helmholtz instability ($Re=10^4$, $M=0.5$, $64^2$ elements, $N=3$): unpenalized DG diverges at $t/t_c\simeq4$; with $\tau_s=0.01$ the run completes and matches the reference ($128^2$ elements, $N=5$) until $t/t_c=5$, deviating slightly afterwards; kinetic energy and enstrophy follow a similar trend (§4.3, Figs. 13–15). **Shown**
23. The Kelvin–Helmholtz runs with the gradient penalty diverged for all values tried ($\tau_g=-10^{-8}$, $-10^{-7}$, $10^{-6}$, $5\times10^{-6}$, $10^{-5}$, $5\times10^{-5}$) (§4.3). **Shown** (reported, no figure)
24. DG schemes "with and without entropy stable formulations [62] diverge for this case" (the Kelvin–Helmholtz instability) (§4.3). **Stated** (cited)
25. 1D advection–diffusion ($N_e=120$, $N=3$, $a=1,2,4$, Péclet numbers 400, 80, 40): the gradient penalty gives optimum errors for small negative values, diverges for large negative values and stagnates for large positive ones; the solution penalty diverges for large negative values and plateaus at low error for large positive ones, with no local optimum near zero (App. B, Fig. B.16a,b). **Shown**
26. Inviscid case: the $L^2$ difference between DG with solution penalty and CG with the same DOFs ($N_e=160$, $N=3$) decays as $\tau_s^{-0.5}$, in agreement with [53] (App. B, Fig. B.16d). **Shown**
27. With central flux the physical mode has a negative wave speed for $kh/(N+1)>1.57$, but a spurious mode carries the energy beyond about $1.1$ (after Asthana and Jameson [67]); the gradient penalty modifies that spurious mode, not the physical one, adding upwinding and dissipation (App. C.1, Fig. C.17). **Shown**
28. The optimal penalties of both kinds on the Taylor–Green vortex are "the highest tested", which "facilitates the estimation of this parameter but can lead to CFL-type restrictions" (§5). **Stated** (from Fig. 10)
29. Jump penalty stabilization requires no change to the original numerical method, and is symmetric and decoupled from the time discretization (citing [3]) (§1). **Stated**

# Printed slips

- **Eq. (25) and the text after it:** "Again, σ scales with the viscosity Re, σ = τ_s Re" is printed for the **gradient** penalty, which elsewhere is parametrized by $\tau_g$. *(Compiler check, not in the sources: with $\sigma_{\mathrm{IP}}=\tau_sRe$ the coefficient $\sigma_{\mathrm{IP}}/Re$ of Eqs. (23) and (25) equals the penalty parameter itself, so the printed $1/Re$ factor cancels; §4.2 nevertheless attributes the larger Taylor–Green penalties to "the scaling of the penalization parameter introduced in Section 2.2".)*
- **Eq. (27):** the forcing carries the prefactor $\frac h2$, while the text below says "$\frac{\Delta x}{2}$ comes from the Jacobian of the element".
- **App. D.2, Figs. D.25–D.27 captions:** "N = 819 … P = 4, resulting in 4096 DOFs". *(Compiler check, not in the sources: $819\times(4+1)=4095$; the paper's other DOF counts, 1600, 1200 and 2000, equal elements times $P+1$.)*
- **(A.14) and (A.16):** printed $\mathbf L=\frac1h\phi(-1)[(a+\lambda|a|)\phi^T(1)]$ but $\mathbf R=-\frac1h\phi(+1)[\frac12(a-\lambda|a|)\phi^T(-1)]$; the factor $\frac12$ appears inside the bracket of $\mathbf R$ and not of $\mathbf L$, so the two printed operators are not mirror images of each other.
- **Eq. (5):** the viscous boundary term is printed $(\mu g)^*|_{-1}^{+1}$ without $\phi_i$, while the advective one is $(au)^*\phi_i|_{-1}^{+1}$.
- **§1 outline and §4 opening:** "two turbulent cases are discussed, including Burgers turbulence and Taylor-Green vortex" and "Two test cases are considered" omit the third case, the Kelvin–Helmholtz instability of §4.3.
- **§4.1 against App. D.2:** the $-2$ slope is that of "unforced Burgers turbulence" in §4.1 and "the slope of the forced Burgers turbulence (−2)" in App. D.2.
- **§5:** "This case includes impressibility effects" (compressibility).
- **App. A:** "$k$ is the wave frequency" ($k$ is the wavenumber); **App. C.1:** "$k/(P+1)>1.1$" lacks the $h$ of "$kh/(P+1)<1.1$" in the same paragraph.
- **Fig. 10 caption:** the DNS dissipation rate is cited as [5], the Wang et al. (2013) review.
- **§4.1:** "Several penalty parameters have been tested … and the representative penalty parameter that better resolves the turbulent kinetic energy" has no main verb.
- **References:** [10] prints "G.E. Karniadakis, G. Karniadakis, S. Sherwin" (an author duplicated).

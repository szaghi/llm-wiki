# Summary

Kurz et al. (2025) present [GALÆXI](/software/galaexi.md), a GPU port of
[FLEXI](/software/flexi.md) for the compressible Navier–Stokes equations on
unstructured, curved hexahedral meshes. It is written in Fortran 2008 with CUDA
Fortran device code and keeps FLEXI's data structures and user interface, so
existing FLEXI setups run unchanged. The discretization is the
[DGSEM](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin-spectral-element-method.md) with
[split-form kinetic-energy-preserving](/properties/kinetic-energy-preservation.md)
fluxes (Pirozzoli's flux) against aliasing, which the paper places among the
remedies for under-resolution instability
([numerical dissipation](/properties/numerical-dissipation.md)). It uses BR1
lifting, low-storage Runge–Kutta schemes and the convex DG/finite-volume
[subcell blending](/methods/subcell-finite-volume-shock-capturing.md) of
Hennemann et al.

The paper contains no turbulence model. Its LES run without an explicit
subgrid model, and the paper does not use the term
[implicit LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md). It cites Beck & Kurz (2023) for the claim
that the DG/FV blending can act as a turbulence model if tuned
([discretization-consistent closure](/definitions/discretization-consistent-closure.md)).

The paper describes three levels of parallelism:
- MPI on a space-filling-curve partition, with CUDA-aware communication;
- three prioritized CUDA streams that compute communicated data first and hide
  latency;
- kernels with one thread per degree of freedom, a race-free "gather" surface
  integral, and a shared-memory volume integral with one block per element.

Scaling on up to 1024 A100 GPUs is near-perfect above about $10^6$ degrees of
freedom per GPU. The design order is recovered on manufactured solutions. On
the [Taylor–Green vortex](/benchmarks/taylor-green-vortex.md) at
$M_0=0.1$ and $1.25$, GALÆXI and FLEXI give identical results that converge to
the references. A wall-resolved LES of a mid-span cascade of
[NASA Rotor 37](/benchmarks/nasa-rotor-37.md) confines the shock capturing to
the leading-edge shock. Measured per convective time, it uses 147 kWh on
128 GPUs against 339 kWh for FLEXI on 32 768 CPU cores. The introduction lists
other high-order solvers ([Nek5000 and NekRS](/software/nek5000.md),
[Nektar++](/software/nektar-plus-plus.md), [HORSES3D](/software/horses3d.md),
[STREAmS](/software/streams.md)), and PyFR for
[flux reconstruction](/methods/flux-reconstruction.md).

# Key claims

Page-local symbols: $\alpha$ is the DG/FV blending factor of the registry. PID
is the performance index, the wall time for one rank to advance one degree of
freedom by one Runge–Kutta stage (a rank is a CPU core or a whole GPU), and
EPID $=P_{\mathrm{rank}}\times$PID its energy-normalized form.
$\varepsilon_s$, $\varepsilon_d$ (registry §11; the source's $\varepsilon_S$, $\varepsilon_D$) are the solenoidal and dilatational
dissipation rates of the Taylor–Green vortex.

1. GALÆXI implements the DGSEM with finite-volume subcell shock capturing for the compressible Navier–Stokes equations on unstructured, curved hexahedral meshes on GPUs. Its user interface is compatible with FLEXI, so it runs existing FLEXI setups "without modifications" (§1, p. 2). **Stated**
2. The combination of high-order accuracy, "provable stability near shocks" and an efficient GPU implementation of the hybrid DG/FV discretization is claimed as a unique feature that sets GALÆXI apart from existing solvers (§1, p. 2). **Stated**
3. All routines called during time stepping run on the accelerator without host–device transfers. Initialization and infrequent analysis routines stay on the CPU (§3, p. 6). **Stated**
4. The volume integral scales as $O(N^4)$, and all other operations as $O(N^3)$ or $O(N^2)$ (Table 1, §2.2). **Derived**
5. Compute-time breakdown on HAWK-AI ($N=7$, split form, $8.9\times10^5$ degrees of freedom on one GPU; Fig. 5): VolInt 39.0%, ConsToPrim 14.5%, FillFlux 10.1%, Misc 7.7%, lifting VolInt 7.2%, lifting SurfInt 5.8%, lifting FillFlux 4.8%, lifting ProlongToFace 3.7%, ApplyJac 2.5%, ProlongToFace 2.4%, SurfInt 2.4%. The volume–surface routines take "only around 15 %", so the overhead of the unstructured mesh is "negligible" (§3.3, p. 11). **Shown**
6. Three prioritized CUDA streams (low: element interior; mid: inner faces; top: MPI faces) let the GPU compute communicated quantities first. Communication of the face states is hidden behind ConsToPrim, and that of the MPI fluxes behind VolInt, ConsToPrim and FillFlux (§3.2, Fig. 4). **Stated** (design); the effect is **Shown** in claim 12
7. The GPU surface-integral kernel assigns one thread per volume degree of freedom, loops over the six faces and flips the flux sign on the replica side. Each entry of the time derivative has a single writer, so there are no race conditions without explicit synchronization (§3.3, Alg. 4). **Stated**
8. The GPU volume integral assigns one thread block per element and computes all fluxes into shared memory before applying the derivative matrix, so each flux is computed once and never written to main memory (App. A). **Stated**
9. GPU memory per degree of freedom falls with $N$, from 1.457 KiB ($N=1$) to 0.787 KiB ($N=12$), because the surface-to-volume ratio $6(N+1)^2/(N+1)^3$ falls. With $N=7$, 48 million degrees of freedom fit on one 40 GiB device (§4.3, Table 2). **Shown**
10. PID converges for all GPU counts above about $10^6$ degrees of freedom per GPU, so GALÆXI "scales almost perfectly beyond the threshold of $10^6$ DOF per GPU". Below it the PID rises, from kernel-launch overhead and tail effects even on one GPU and from communication latency with more GPUs. The code is "kernel-bound" at high load and "low-load-bound" at low load (§4.4, Fig. 6). **Shown**
11. Unlike GALÆXI, CPU FLEXI also loses performance at high loads (cache exhaustion, memory bandwidth), with a narrow optimum of 3000–10 000 degrees of freedom per rank [21, 50]. For GALÆXI a larger load only improves performance, and GPU memory is the only limit (§4.4, p. 13). **Stated** (CPU part, cited) / **Shown** (GPU part)
12. Weak scaling, relative to one 4-GPU node, is almost perfect up to 1024 GPUs for loads above $10^6$ degrees of freedom per GPU (Fig. 7, loads $2.6\times10^5$ to $3.4\times10^7$). Strong scaling (problem sizes $3.4\times10^7$, $5.4\times10^8$, $2.1\times10^9$) is almost perfect up to 1024 GPUs while the load exceeds $10^6$ per rank. Without stream scheduling performance drops significantly at low loads, and the figure annotates "up to 92% increase" from the streams (§4.4, Fig. 8). **Shown**
13. The strong-scaling limit "matches" NekRS, which reaches its limit at about 2 to 4 million degrees of freedom per rank (Fischer et al. [15]) (§4.4, p. 13). **Stated** (comparison with a cited work)
14. On the DragonFly+ network of JUWELS Booster, communication cost rises significantly when the nodes span more switch groups of 192 GPUs (§4.4, p. 13). **Stated**
15. Manufactured solutions: the design order is reached for all $N\in[2,9]$, with standard DG on Gauss nodes and split-form DG on Gauss–Lobatto nodes, on meshes from 1 to $64^3$ elements at $t=1$ (§5.1, Fig. 9). **Shown**
16. Taylor–Green vortex at $Re=1600$, $M_0=0.1$ (version II initialization, $N=7$, $64^3$ to $512^3$ degrees of freedom): GALÆXI and FLEXI give the same results "up to machine precision". $\varepsilon_s$ converges with resolution to DeBonis's reference and matches it "almost perfectly" at $512^3$ (§5.2, Fig. 11 left). **Shown**
17. Taylor–Green vortex at $Re=1600$, $M_0=1.25$ (version II, Sutherland's law, subcell blending with the same maximum $\alpha$ at all resolutions, $N=7$, $64^3$ to $512^3$): GALÆXI and FLEXI give identical $\varepsilon_s(t)$, which converges to Chapelier et al. and is "almost identical" at $512^3$ (§5.2, Fig. 11 right). **Shown**
18. In the Rotor 37 cascade LES the finite-volume shock capturing is active only near the shock, with $\alpha$ between 0.1 and 0.7 in the leading-edge zoom, so high order is kept in smooth regions (§6.3, Fig. 13). **Shown**
19. GALÆXI's PID in the Rotor 37 case is about 35% higher than in §4. The authors attribute this to sponge zones, boundary conditions, the shock indicator, the finite-volume shock capturing and load imbalance (§6.3). **Shown**
20. Energy comparison over 8 convective times (Table 3). GPU: 128 ranks, $2.03\times10^6$ degrees of freedom per rank, 448 W per rank, PID $4.58\times10^{-9}$ s, EPID $2.05\times10^{-6}$ J, 9209 s and 147 kWh per convective time. CPU: 32 768 ranks, $7.93\times10^3$ per rank, 4.94 W, PID $1.02\times10^{-6}$ s, EPID $5.06\times10^{-6}$ J, 7538 s and 339 kWh. Savings: 59.5% (EPID) and 56.8% (energy) (§6.3). **Shown**
21. Power was measured rack-wise by facility monitoring. It includes the network switches, and it includes cooling for the GPU system but not for the CPU system. The authors call the saving "a rough estimate" and "a conservative lower bound" (§6.3). **Stated**
22. The authors equate the energy saving with a similar reduction of carbon emissions, "more than 55 %" (§6.3, §7). **Stated**
23. The DG/FV blending "has also been demonstrated to yield a sensible turbulence model if tuned correctly" [46] (§2.3). **Stated** (citing Beck & Kurz 2023)
24. Evaluating two-point split fluxes "increases the computational cost considerably" (§2.2). **Stated**

# Printed slips

- **Eq. (15):** the Lagrange polynomial is printed as $\ell_i^N(x)=\prod_{i=0,\,i\neq j}^N\frac{x_i-x}{x_i-x_j}$. The product index is the polynomial's own label, and as printed it cannot satisfy the Kronecker property of Eq. (16).
- **Fig. 9 caption:** "split-flux DG scheme on LGL nodes (left) and the standard DG scheme on GL nodes (right)". The panel titles print the standard scheme on the left and the split-flux scheme on the right.
- **Fig. 11 caption:** "DeBonis [57] (left) and Chapelier et al. [58] (left)". The compressible reference belongs to the right panel.
- **App. A, SurfInt:** "discussed at length in Section 2.2". The GPU implementation is discussed in Section 3.3 (Algs. 3–4).
- **§4.4:** the mesh starts at $4\times4\times2=32$ elements and is "successively doubled in each spatial direction until the finest resolution of $256^3=16.8\times10^6$ elements". *(compiler check, not in the sources)* Doubling every direction from $4\times4\times2$ reaches $256\times256\times128$. The quoted endpoints, 16 384 and $8.6\times10^9$ degrees of freedom, match 32 and $256^3$ elements at $N=7$.
- **Eqs. (29)–(30)** normalize by $\mu_0$, which is never defined (the text defines $\mu_{ref}$). §5.2 names a "viscous dissipation rate $\varepsilon_T$" that is never defined.
- **Eq. (24)** sets the velocity vector equal to a scalar expression.
- **Typos:** "interconeccted" (§4.1, twice), "Ifiniband", "rage of 3000 to 10 000", "Futhermore", "an perfect gas".

# Summary

Gao et al. (2025b) build a wall-stress
[wall-modelled LES](/methods/wall-modelled-les.md) into a compressible
high-order [flux reconstruction](/methods/flux-reconstruction.md) (FR/CPR)
solver with the [Vreman model](/methods/vreman-model.md) (constant $c_v=0.07$ multiplying $\sqrt{B_\beta/(\alpha_{ij}\alpha_{ij})}$, the filter width inside $\beta_{ij}$) and
low-dissipation low-dispersion Runge–Kutta time marching
([LDDRK](/methods/low-dissipation-low-dispersion-scheme.md), Hu et al.
1996). Two algebraic wall laws are used. The
[equilibrium](/methods/equilibrium-wall-model.md) composite
[law of the wall](/definitions/law-of-the-wall.md) of Allmaras, Johnson &
Spalart (2012) is inverted without iteration by a five-branch piecewise fit
$y^+=F^{-1}(Re_y)$. The generalized wall function of Shih, Povinelli & Liu
(2003), which splits the near-wall velocity into a wall-stress part and a
tangential-pressure-gradient part with the velocity scale $u_p$ (an
[adverse-pressure-gradient wall law](/definitions/adverse-pressure-gradient-wall-law.md)),
is applied by a non-iterative two-step correction; the abstract states
that the wall model "accounts for non-equilibrium effects induced by
favorable and adverse pressure gradients" (see
[non-equilibrium wall models](/methods/nonequilibrium-wall-model.md)).

Three implementation choices depart from earlier DG/FR WMLES (Frère et al.
2017; Shi et al. 2020; Wang 2022): the model input is the first solution
point of the wall-adjacent element, which keeps the compact stencil; the
wall is no-slip rather than the
[slip condition](/methods/slip-wall-model.md) earlier authors favoured;
and the inviscid wall flux is a convex blend of a weak-prescribed flux
(accurate, unstable) and a weak-Riemann HLLC flux (stable, too steep a
velocity profile), with weight $C_2\in[1/12,1/5]$ on the Riemann part.
The paper cites Shi et al. (2020) for an even polynomial degree in the
wall element being preferable, and itself reports results that improve
from $N=2$ to $N=4$ on the hill (Figs. 8–10, 13).

Validation: [channel flow](/benchmarks/turbulent-channel-flow.md) at
$Re_\tau=2000$, $5200$, $10\,000$ on $48\times24\times24$ elements
($N=2,3,4$), with mean velocity on the log law and no first-cell
oscillation at $Re_\tau=5200$. The
[periodic hill](/benchmarks/periodic-hill.md) is run at $Re_h=10\,595$
on two meshes "significantly coarser than those employed by other
researchers" and at $Re_h=37\,000$, against PIV,
LES and DNS $C_f$. $L_2$ errors fall with $N$ and with mesh refinement.
Cost on one core rises 6% (equilibrium law) and 12.5% (generalized law)
over no wall model. The introduction cites Choi & Moin (2012) for the
[grid-point scaling](/results/les-grid-point-requirements.md). Evidence is
numerical; which $C_2$ each case used is not stated.

# Key claims

Page-local symbols: $Re_y=Uy/\nu$ the wall-distance Reynolds number of
the input velocity $U$ and $F$ the map $y^+\mapsto Re_y=y^+u^+$;
$u_c=u_\tau+u_p$ the hybrid velocity scale and $y^+_p=u_py/\nu$; $f_1$,
$f_2$ the Shih wall-stress and pressure-gradient profile functions;
$F^c_{\mathrm{PRE}}$, $F^c_{\mathrm{RM}}$ the weak-prescribed and
weak-Riemann inviscid wall fluxes, $C_1+C_2=1$ their weights.

1. LES grid count for high-Re wall turbulence scales as $Re^{13/7}$; wall modelling reduces it to $Re$ (citing Choi & Moin 2012). §I. **Stated**
2. Frère et al. (2017) found the location of the wall-model input critical and recommended the bottom face of the second off-wall cell; Shi et al. (2020) corroborated it and found strong sensitivity to the polynomial degree of the wall-adjacent element, "quadratic polynomials in the first off-wall element yields noticeably better results than cubic polynomials". §I. **Stated**
3. Even after extensive averaging the velocity profiles of those studies oscillate in the first wall-adjacent cell, harmful for aeroacoustic and wall-pressure applications; on unstructured meshes a second-cell input breaks the compact DG/FR stencil and degrades parallel efficiency. §I, §II C3. **Stated**
4. The wall model takes its input at the solution point closest to each wall flux point, inside the wall-adjacent element. §II C3, Fig. 1. **Stated** (design; contribution)
5. The inverse $y^+=F^{-1}(Re_y)$ of the Allmaras et al. law is fitted piecewise (five branches, Eq. (A1), Table II), so $u_\tau$ follows without root finding of Eq. (17). §II C1, Eq. (19). **Derived** (fit)
6. The Shih et al. wall function, Eq. (28), reduces to the equilibrium law at zero pressure gradient and remains valid at zero wall stress, because $u_c$ is always non-zero and positive. §II C2. **Stated**
7. A two-step strategy avoids iterating on $u_\tau/u_c$: equilibrium $u_\tau$ first, then the pressure-gradient correction (steps 3–8, Eqs. (30)–(32)). §II C2–C3. **Derived** (contribution)
8. A no-slip wall condition is imposed, "unlike other investigators" who stressed the slip condition (Shi et al. found slip works better). §II D. **Stated** (contribution)
9. The weak-prescribed inviscid wall flux, Eq. (33), gives a good mean velocity at $Re_\tau=10\,000$ but "tends to destabilize the computation, producing grid-level oscillations at the wall"; "it is only stable for simple case such as the channel flow". §II D1, Fig. 2(a). **Shown** (velocity) / **Stated** (stability)
10. The weak-Riemann wall flux, Eqs. (34)–(36), is stable but produces an excessively steep mean velocity profile together with the wall-model viscous flux. §II D2, Fig. 2(b). **Shown**
11. The hybrid flux $F^c=C_1F^c_{\mathrm{PRE}}+C_2F^c_{\mathrm{RM}}$ keeps the stability of the Riemann flux and the accuracy of the prescribed one; $C_2$ should be the smallest stable value, smaller on regular grids, larger on poor grids; "a range of $\frac1{12}\sim\frac15$ is effective". §II D3, Eq. (37). **Stated** (empirical; contribution)
12. A highly dissipative Riemann solver (Rusanov) is unsuitable at the wall because the extrapolated tangential velocity at the wall flux point is non-zero; HLLC is used in all cases. §II D3. **Stated**
13. Channel at $Re_\tau=2000$ and $10\,000$: mean velocity with $N=2,3,4$ "closely follow the log law"; at $Re_\tau=10\,000$ the first solution point lies at $y^+\approx120$. §III A, Fig. 3. **Shown**
14. Reynolds stresses ($N=3$) deviate from DNS near the wall, attributed to the coarse mesh of 12 cells (48 solution points) per half-width; $N=2$ and $N=4$ are similar and not shown. §III A, Fig. 4. **Shown**
15. At $Re_\tau=5200$ the mean velocity lacks the oscillatory first-element behaviour of the Frère et al. and Shi et al. data; Reynolds stress is comparable to the references "at almost equivalent resolution". §III A, Fig. 5. **Shown**
16. Periodic hill, $Re_h=10\,595$, Mesh 1: the hill-top mean velocity shows a minor discrepancy at $N=2$, improved at $N=3$, excellent at $N=4$; $R_{11}$ is over-predicted in the upper-wall boundary layer at $N=2$, improving to $N=4$. §III B1, Figs. 8–9. **Shown**
17. $C_f$ agrees better with the DNS of Krank et al. as $N$ rises from 2 to 4; separation and reattachment positions agree with the reference. §III B1, Fig. 10(a). **Shown**
18. Mesh 2 is more accurate than Mesh 1; $L_2$ errors of mean velocity and Reynolds stress at the 10 PIV stations decrease with $N$ and with refinement. §III B1, Figs. 12–13. **Shown**
19. $Re_h=37\,000$, $N=3$ on Mesh 1 ("128 × 48 × 60 points", against $512\times256\times256$ of the reference LES): mean velocity agrees with experiment and reference LES except a slight deviation of the mean wall-normal velocity in the recirculation; shear-layer intensities are over-estimated, as in the reference LES. §III B2, Figs. 14–15. **Shown**
20. Cost of 100 steps on one core (Intel Xeon Silver 4210), Mesh 1 (6144 elements, 1024 wall elements): 135.6 s without wall model, 143.8 s (+6%) with the standard law, 152.7 s (+12.5%) with the generalized law, attributed to the absence of iterations. §III B. **Shown**
21. The hill meshes are "significantly coarser than those employed by other researchers"; the near-wall elements at the crest do not resolve the boundary layer. §III B, Table I, Fig. 11. **Stated** / **Shown**

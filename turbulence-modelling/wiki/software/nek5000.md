# Nek5000 and NekRS

**Nek5000** is the open-source spectral element code of Argonne National
Laboratory for the incompressible Navier–Stokes and energy equations, and
**NekRS** its GPU-oriented successor. NekRS is written in C++ with kernels
in the OCCA kernel language, compiled just in time to CUDA, HIP or OpenCL;
it keeps Nek5000's user interface and features such as conjugate heat
transfer, uses Nek5000's gather–scatter library gslib for MPI communication,
and forks its kernels from libParanumal ([Fischer et al. 2022](/sources/fischer-2022.md),
§1, §5). [Gillyns et al. (2022)](/sources/gillyns-2022.md) implement an
algebraic wall model in Nek5000, and Domingo & Vervisch (2023, §1) list
Nek5000 among the spectral element codes used for
[DNS](/definitions/direct-numerical-simulation.md).

## The spectral element method

Both codes use the continuous Galerkin spectral element method (SEM) of
Patera: $N_e$ curvilinear hexahedra (page-local), each carrying tensor-product
Lagrange polynomials of degree $N$ on Gauss–Lobatto–Legendre (GLL) nodes,
about $n\approx N_eN^3$ unknowns (Fischer et al. 2022, §2.3; Gillyns et al.
2022, §2). The velocity is continuous across element faces and its gradient
is not (Gillyns et al. 2022, §2); in the
[DGSEM](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin-spectral-element-method.md) the
solution itself is discontinuous across faces.

As written by Fischer et al. (2022, §2.3):

- Derivatives are tensor contractions with the one-dimensional
  differentiation matrix $D_{ij}$, and the GLL quadrature gives a diagonal
  mass matrix; the local stiffness matrix is applied in factored form
  $D^{\mathsf T}GD$ with six diagonal geometric factors, matrix-free, so the
  work is $O(nN)$ in small dense matrix–matrix products and the storage
  $O(n)$.
- Continuity is imposed by a Boolean global-to-local map $Q$; the
  gather–scatter $QQ^{\mathsf T}$ has a unit-depth stencil for every $N$,
  so the discretization is "communication minimal".
- GLL quadrature is exact for integrands of degree up to $2N-1$ on affine
  elements, sufficient for the stability of the Poisson operator but not of
  advection, whose integrand has degree $3N$: advection is evaluated on
  $N_q^3$ dealiasing points (page-local $N_q$; $N_q=9$–12 in the runs with
  $N=7$).

**Time stepping and splitting** (Fischer et al. 2022, §2.1–2.2). The
standard integrator is BDF$k$/EXT$k$: implicit backward differences for the
Stokes part and extrapolation of the advection term, typically $k=3$ at
CFL 0.5. The alternative is a characteristics (semi-Lagrangian, OIFS)
formulation in which the advected fields are obtained by solving
$\partial_t\boldsymbol w+\boldsymbol u\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol w=0$ backwards
over the previous steps with dealiased Runge–Kutta substeps, typically at
second order and CFL 2–4. Each step then solves a pressure-Poisson problem
with a boundary term built on the extrapolated vorticity, which controls
divergence errors at boundaries, and one Helmholtz problem per velocity
component. The pressure problem carries the fastest modes and is the
stiffest and most communication-intensive substep.

## NekRS on GPUs

From Fischer et al. (2022, §3):

- **Partitioning** by parallel recursive spectral bisection after a
  recursive coordinate bisection, which cuts its run time a hundredfold
  (about 40 s for 60 million elements on 8,100 Summit cores).
- **Communication.** gslib times pairwise, crystal-router and all-reduce
  exchanges at setup and picks the fastest; NekRS adds device-direct and
  host-staged variants, tuned under overlapped run conditions, and overlaps
  communication with the evaluation of interior elements.
- **Kernels.** A three-dimensional thread block is limited by the 1,024
  threads per block to $N_q\le10$; a two-dimensional block that loops over
  the third direction holding it in registers reaches $N_q=32$ and cuts
  shared-memory loads by nearly three.
- **Pressure preconditioning.** $p$-multigrid with Chebyshev-accelerated
  Jacobi or Chebyshev-accelerated overlapping Schwarz smoothers (local
  solves by fast diagonalization) and an algebraic-multigrid coarse solve.
  On a 1,568-pebble bed, Chebyshev–Schwarz smoothing is 1.85 times faster
  than plain Schwarz, and 4.85 times with adaptive gather–scatter,
  single-precision smoothing and projection-based initial guesses; pressure
  iterations per step fall from 93 to 16 with characteristics time stepping
  (Fig. 4, Table 1). Characteristics stepping at CFL 4.05 takes a step four
  times larger than BDF3/EXT3 at CFL 1.06 for twice the time per step
  (0.503 s against 0.259 s).
- **Bandwidth.** The kernels sustain 80–90% of the realizable,
  bandwidth-limited peak (§3.4, Fig. 5; §5).

## Performance

- **Against Nek5000.** A spacer-grid LES ($Re_D=14{,}000$, 3.2 million
  elements, $N=7$) runs at 0.14 s per step on 98 Summit nodes (V100), 4.8
  times faster than Nek5000 on 8,192 Mira nodes at comparable efficiency; a
  5×5 rod-bundle DNS ($Re_D=19{,}000$, 8.4 million elements, $N=7$, 2.87
  billion points) is about twice as fast as the estimated Mira strong-scale
  time (§4.1, Table 2). The subgrid model of the LES is not stated.
- **Scaling.** Strong scaling to all 27,648 V100 GPUs of Summit for 17×17
  rod-bundle and full-core reactor geometries up to 175 million elements
  and 60 billion points; 80% parallel efficiency at about 2.5 million points
  per GPU, the points per GPU, not the GPU count, being the leading
  indicator. Weak scaling at 2.1 million points per GPU keeps 82.5% over a
  53-fold increase for the rod bundle but falls to 54% for the full core,
  which the authors correlate with the maximum number of neighbours in the
  gather–scatter graph (§4.2, Table 3). Nek5000's strong-scale limit on Mira
  is 2,000–4,000 points per MPI rank (§3).
- **Devices.** On a turbulent pipe ($Re_D=19{,}000$, $N=7$) an A100 is 1.52
  times faster than a Summit V100, and the MI100 and MI60 deliver 85% and
  60% of the V100; one V100 matches 336 Power9 cores (§4.3, Table 4). A
  44,257-pebble bed (13 million elements, 4.5 billion points) runs at 0.54 s
  per step on 1,788 V100s, 84% of it in the pressure solve.

Fischer et al. report performance only: no flow statistics, validation or
subgrid model are given for the runs labelled LES or DNS.

## Wall-modelled LES in Nek5000

Gillyns et al. (2022) add an algebraic [equilibrium wall model](/methods/equilibrium-wall-model.md)
to Nek5000 and validate it on [channel flow](/benchmarks/turbulent-channel-flow.md)
at $Re_\tau=1000$ and 5200 against the DNS of Lee & Moser, with $N=7$ and
Nek5000's approximate deconvolution subgrid model (relaxation parameter
`filterWeight` = 10), which they describe as based on high-pass filtering,
"not a simple eddy-viscosity SGS model" (§2). Code-specific points:

- the momentum equation must be in stress form so that the wall shear
  stress can be imposed as a traction boundary condition (§2);
- the model samples the velocity at the top node of the wall-adjacent
  element, which keeps it element-local because Nek5000 is parallelized
  element-wise, and filters it in time with an exponential moving average;
  without the filter the model diverges, which the authors attribute to the
  spikes at element boundaries (§3, §5);
- the $C^0$ continuity produces Gibbs spikes of the fluctuation statistics
  at element boundaries on coarse meshes, which vanish as the mesh nears DNS
  resolution; the authors use them as a reference-free under-resolution
  indicator and name them the method's main drawback (§2, §6, §8);
- the mean velocity agrees with DNS inside the under-resolved first element
  down to $y^+\simeq10$ at $Re_\tau=1000$, which the authors attribute,
  without a same-mesh comparison, to the spectral element method (§7, §10).

Details are on [wall-modelled LES](/methods/wall-modelled-les.md).

**Wall-stress modelling with explicit subgrid models.**
[Mukha et al. (2025)](/sources/mukha-2025.md) implement algebraic wall-stress
modelling in Nek5000 and release it as the open-source `nekwmles`. The
arXiv v1 preprint read lists two authors; the published article lists
three. Their configuration is $N=7$ on GLL nodes, the $P_N$–$P_{N-2}$
formulation (pressure two degrees lower), 3/2 over-integration of
convection and BDF3/EXT3 time stepping. Momentum is solved by
preconditioned conjugate gradients, and pressure by GMRES with an additive
overlapping Schwarz preconditioner and the XXT coarse-grid solver
(§II.B). The wall model is Spalding's law (the
[equilibrium wall model](/methods/equilibrium-wall-model.md)). Any
sampling height can be set per wall node, and the velocity there is
spectrally interpolated inside the element that contains the sampling
point (§II.C.1); the runs use $h_{\mathrm{wm}}=0.2\delta$ in the channel
and $0.2\delta_{99}(x)$ in the boundary layer (§III.A, §IV.A). The subgrid closure is explicit: the
[Vreman model](/methods/vreman-model.md) (constant $2.5C_s^2$, $C_s=0.16$)
or the $\sigma$ model ($C_\sigma=1.35$, page-local). Its length scale is
the largest GLL spacing of the element, assigned to the whole element
(§II.D). Code-specific findings (§II–§V):

- **Weak wall-stress condition.** The modelled stress enters the weak
  form as a natural boundary term (Eqs. 7–12). The stress computed from the
  mean velocity gradient at the wall therefore differs from the modelled
  one, by 12.6%, 10.9% and 8.1% on channel meshes of 2, 4 and 8 elements
  per $\delta$ at $Re_\tau\approx8000$. The modelled stress itself is
  within 1% of the DNS value (Table II). The authors call the discrepancy
  "unique to the finite element method".
- **Viscosity-based condition.** This alternative imposes no-slip together
  with the wall eddy viscosity that reproduces the modelled kinematic
  stress, $\nu_t=\lvert\tau_w^{\mathrm{wm}}\rvert/\big(2\sqrt{S_{xy}^2+S_{zy}^2}\big)-\nu$
  at the wall. Here $\tau_w^{\mathrm{wm}}$ (page-local) is the modelled
  kinematic wall stress, and $S_{ij}$ carries the registry's factor
  $\tfrac12$ (Eq. 14 with the source's unhalved strain). The condition fails
  in the spectral element method. The driving-pressure-gradient stress is in
  error by 38%, 26% and 16% (Table IV), which the authors attribute to the
  loss of global momentum conservation. The velocity is continuous across
  elements, but its derivatives jump (§II.B, §III.D).
- **Derivative jumps** across element faces produce spikes in $\nu_t$,
  which the authors call "just numerical artifacts". They name derivative
  continuity "a missing component" of the methodology (§III.C, Fig. 7).
- **Nek5000's high-pass relaxation filter** as implicit subgrid model,
  after Gillyns et al. but on two modes at strength 100 instead of one mode
  at 10, "does not introduce enough damping". Vreman stabilizes best. The
  $\sigma$ model, whose eddy viscosity vanishes as $y^3$ at the wall, gives
  stronger derivative wiggles (§III.C, Figs. 6–8).
- Mean velocity on the channel meshes is within 1% for $y>0.1\delta$, and
  second-order statistics need about 4 elements per $\delta$. On a
  zero-pressure-gradient boundary layer ($Re_\theta$ from 790 to about
  6000) $C_f$ is within 2% of wall-resolved LES on the finest mesh (§III.B,
  §IV.B). Details are on [wall-modelled LES](/methods/wall-modelled-les.md),
  [turbulent channel flow](/benchmarks/turbulent-channel-flow.md) and
  [turbulent boundary layers](/benchmarks/turbulent-boundary-layer.md).

The two Nek5000 WMLES studies differ on two operational points:

- **Time filtering.** Gillyns et al. (2022) need it, because their model
  diverges without it. Of that divergence Mukha et al. (2025, §II.C.1)
  write "We have not encountered this issue in our simulations", and they
  find no systematic a posteriori gain from time-averaging the wall-model
  input (§III.B.3).
- **Sampling height.** Gillyns et al. keep the sampling at the top node of
  the wall-adjacent element. Mukha et al. sample at $0.2\delta$
  (channel) and $0.2\delta_{99}(x)$ (boundary layer), interpolated in
  whichever element holds that point, and report that "no accuracy issues
  have been observed when sampling from the wall-adjacent element" (§V).
  On their finest channel mesh (8 elements per $\delta$) $0.2\delta$ lies
  in the second element if the elements have equal height *(compiler
  check, not in the sources)*.

The configurations differ in subgrid closure (approximate deconvolution
against Vreman), sampling height and Reynolds number, so the two
statements on time filtering are not on the same set-up; the same
difference is recorded as a qualification on the
[equilibrium wall model](/methods/equilibrium-wall-model.md).

## Other GPU ports, as recalled

[Kurz et al. (2025)](/sources/kurz-2025.md), §1, recall a first port of a
"barebones version" of Nek5000 to GPUs with OpenACC in 2015, and full GPU
support in NekRS through OCCA. They also recall Neko, written from scratch
in object-oriented modern Fortran with hardware abstraction layers. They
compare their own GPU DG code [GALÆXI](/software/galaexi.md), which scales
almost perfectly above about $10^6$ degrees of freedom per GPU, with the
NekRS strong-scaling limit of about 2–4 million per rank that they cite to
Fischer et al. (2022). They call the two "similar" (§4.4). The figure
Fischer et al. give for 80% efficiency is about 2.5 million points per GPU
(above).

## Qualifications

- The version of Fischer et al. read is the arXiv v1 preprint (April 2021).
  Its Table 1 header prints $n=2{,}569{,}495{,}663$ for a case of
  524,386 elements at $N=7$, i.e. about 180 million points as the text
  says; the pipe case is printed with 6840 elements in the text and 6480 in
  Table 4; §4.3 calls one V100 248 times faster than one CPU core where Table 4
  gives $19.9/0.0851\approx234$ *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.
- Fischer et al. (2022, §2.3) approximate the gradient cost
  $6N_e(N+1)^4+15N_e(N+1)^3$ by $n(15+6N)$; with $n=N_e(N+1)^3$ it is
  $n(21+6N)$ *(compiler check, not in the sources)*.

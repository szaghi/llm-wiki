# Shock–turbulent boundary layer interaction

A shock–turbulent boundary layer interaction (STBLI) arises when a
[turbulent boundary layer](/benchmarks/turbulent-boundary-layer.md) at
supersonic or hypersonic free-stream Mach number meets a shock, either
generated by the wall itself (a **compression ramp** of angle $\phi_r$,
page-local) or by an external body (an **impinging oblique shock**). A
strong enough interaction separates the layer: in the ramps of
[Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md) the Mach 7, 8° case stays
attached while the 24°, 33° and 34° cases separate, with a separation shock
forming inside the incoming layer ahead of the bubble, a separation shear
layer over it, and reattachment on the ramp (§III C–D, §IV B, Fig. 17). The quantities a simulation must predict are the mean
separation length $L_{\mathrm{sep}}$ (page-local: the distance between the
zero crossings of the mean $C_f$), the wall pressure, skin friction and heat
transfer through the interaction, and the low-frequency unsteadiness of the
separation shock, measured by the Strouhal number $St_L=fL_{\mathrm{sep}}/U_e$
(page-local, $U_e$ the boundary-layer-edge velocity). The case combines the
compressible boundary layer, a separated shear layer and shock motion, and is
expensive because the low-frequency cycle needs long records.

Both sources run a Mach 2.9, 24° ramp: Dang et al. validate against the DNS
of Martín et al. and the experiment of Bookey et al. (§5.1), and Helm &
Martín's R24-M3 DNS is that of Priebe & Martín (their ref. [35]). The
printed incoming conditions are close (edge temperature 108.9 K in Helm &
Martín, Table II; free-stream 108.1 K in Dang et al., Table 8), but neither
paper states that the two set-ups are identical. Helm & Martín's attached
Mach 7 case reproduces, through the DNS of Priebe & Martín, the conditions
of the Bookey et al. experiment (§I).

Three further sources share configurations with these. The Mach 2.91, 24°
ramp of [Tonicello et al. (2022)](/sources/tonicello-2022.md) takes the
conditions of Priebe & Martín (2012) as cited (§2.1) and adds an expansion
corner downstream "to study the effect of strong expansions on the
turbulence". The Mach 7.2, 8° ramp of
[van Noordt et al. (2022)](/sources/vannoordt-2022.md) is compared with the
DNS of Priebe & Martín (§3.4), the reference of Helm & Martín's R8-M7. The
Mach 2.28, 8° impinging shock is the case of
[De Vanna et al. (2022)](/sources/devanna-2022.md) (the IUSTI experiment,
$Re_\tau=700$) and of [Yu et al. (2022)](/sources/yu-2022-e.md) (a DNS at
$Re_\tau=201$), at different Reynolds numbers;
[Yu et al. (2023)](/sources/yu-2023.md) analyse the same DNS database
downstream of the interaction (§2), with a separation length that differs
from the one Yu et al. (2022) print (see Contradictions).

Two further sources continue configurations above.
[Shi & Yan (2023)](/sources/shi-2023.md) run the Mach 2.9, 24° ramp of
the Bookey et al. experiment, with $T_\infty=108.1$ K and $T_w=307$ K as in
Dang et al. and Tonicello et al., and compare with the DNS of Wu & Martín,
Tong et al. and Zhu et al. (§IV.A, Table I); their unit Reynolds number,
$5.966\times10^6$ m$^{-1}$, differs from the 5581.4 per mm printed by Dang
et al. (Table 8). [Gao et al. (2023)](/sources/gao-2023.md) rerun the
Mach 2 impinging shock of Gao et al. (2022) with the rigid wall under the
interaction replaced by an elastic panel, and take the rigid case as the
reference (§5).

## Methods the sources use

**LES with a dynamic mixed model.** [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md),
§II, solve the Favre-filtered compressible equations in curvilinear
coordinates with the one-coefficient
[dynamic mixed model](/methods/scale-similarity-model.md) of Martín, Piomelli
& Candler for the subgrid stress and heat flux (a dynamic eddy-viscosity term
plus a scale-similar term built with a top-hat test filter, the coefficients
and the subgrid Prandtl number computed by the
[dynamic procedure](/methods/dynamic-procedure.md) with spanwise averaging),
Knight et al.'s model for the subgrid turbulent diffusion, and the subgrid
viscous diffusion neglected. Inviscid fluxes use a fourth-order
bandwidth-optimized [WENO scheme](/methods/weno-scheme.md) with absolute and
relative limiters and near-wall filtering of the candidate weights, to reduce
its [numerical dissipation](/properties/numerical-dissipation.md); viscous
terms are fourth-order central, time stepping low-storage third-order
Runge–Kutta. The filter width is the cube root of the product of the
computational grid spacings. Inflow comes from an auxiliary boundary layer
with recycling–rescaling over a long box, interpolated onto the ramp inlet,
so that the incoming state stays fixed over the long records the
low-frequency cycle needs (§II, Fig. 1).

**DNS on GPUs.** [Dang et al. (2022)](/sources/dang-2022.md) run the
Mach 2.9 ramp with [OpenCFD-SCU](/software/opencfd-scu.md): Steger–Warming
flux-vector splitting, the seventh-order bandwidth-optimized WENO-SYMBO of
Martín et al. for convection (the scheme of the reference DNS), eighth-order
central viscous terms, and turbulence generated by blowing and suction on
the upstream flat plate rather than by recycling (§5.1, §6.1). The wide-span
grid has $\Delta x^+=1.2$, $\Delta y^+=0.41$, $\Delta z^+=1.2$ at the
reference station 35 mm upstream of the corner, "more than 6000 times the
number [of points] used by Martín et al." (§6.1).

[Zhang et al. (2022c)](/sources/zhang-2022-g.md), §II, run the
Mach 6 swept ramp with the same GPU solver: Steger–Warming splitting with a
hybrid of the OMP6 scheme in smooth regions and WENO7 where the pressure
gradient flags a shock, eighth-order central viscous terms, third-order TVD
Runge–Kutta, and an upstream boundary layer tripped from a laminar profile
by a multi-frequency disturbance. In the corner region the coarser grid has
$\Delta x^+=2.92$, $\Delta y^+=0.44$ at the wall and 5.28 at the
boundary-layer edge, $\Delta z^+=2.19$, the finer one 1.94, 0.35, 3.53 and
1.46 (Table I). Wall pressure is "virtually identical" on both grids;
peak skin friction and heat flux are 7–10% lower on the coarser one at the
same positions (Fig. 4). Upstream, the van Driest-transformed velocity
follows the linear and log laws with an intercept of 5.7, which the
authors attribute to the wall temperature, and the density-scaled
fluctuations agree with the cited simulations and experiments (Fig. 6).

**Wall-modelled LES.** [Hoy & Bermejo-Moreno (2022)](/sources/hoy-2022.md)
use the [Vreman model](/methods/vreman-model.md), the
[equilibrium wall model](/methods/equilibrium-wall-model.md) of Kawai &
Larsson matched at $0.1\delta_0$ with four cells below it, and digital-filter
inflow; the run details are on
[turbulent boundary layers](/benchmarks/turbulent-boundary-layer.md).

**High-order implicit LES with a filtered shock indicator.**
[Gao et al. (2022)](/sources/gao-2022.md) run the split-form
[discontinuous Galerkin spectral element method](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin-spectral-element-method.md)
of [FLEXI](/software/flexi.md) as [implicit LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md),
with no subgrid model and the dissipation of the interface Riemann solver,
and capture shocks with the
[subcell finite-volume scheme](/methods/subcell-finite-volume-shock-capturing.md):
a troubled element of degree $N$ becomes $(N+1)^3$ second-order
finite-volume subcells. On the instantaneous field the Jameson pressure
indicator flags large parts of the under-resolved boundary layer, and
Ducros-type indicators either miss the shocks inside it or flag too much,
expansion fans included; evaluated on the spanwise-averaged pressure, the
Jameson indicator confines the subcells to the shocks, inside the layer as
well, a remedy the authors restrict to quasi-two-dimensional configurations
(§3.2, Figs. 2, 7); see [shock sensor](/definitions/shock-sensor.md). A
positivity-preserving limiter stabilizes the frequent switching. Inflow
turbulence comes from a reformulated synthetic-eddy method with
anisotropic linear forcing over $5\delta_r$–$10\delta_r$ to shorten the
recovery (§5.2; see [inflow turbulence generation](/methods/inflow-turbulence-generation.md)).

**Flux reconstruction with limiters.**
[Shi & Yan (2023)](/sources/shi-2023.md) run the ramp as
[implicit LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md) with the
[flux reconstruction](/methods/flux-reconstruction.md) (correction
procedure via reconstruction) method of their in-house solver NFR at
degree $N=2$ on Gauss–Lobatto solution points, the Roe flux, BR2 viscous
terms and third-order SSP Runge–Kutta (§II.A, §IV.A). Troubled cells are
flagged by a KXRCF-type [shock sensor](/definitions/shock-sensor.md)
rewritten without the grid-length scaling (threshold 0.01 on the density
jump across inflow faces) and limited by the P-weighted compact
[WENO](/methods/weno-scheme.md) limiter of Li, Wang & Ren with modified
linear weights and a mode-truncation rule; the Zhang–Shu
positivity-preserving limiter is added because the WENO limiter does not
keep density and pressure positive, and non-physical states crash the
simulation (§II.B, Eqs. 6–18). On the double Mach reflection the sensor
also flags parts of the shear layer and vortex, and the limiters "do not
completely remove the numerical oscillations" (§III.B, Fig. 3). Inflow
turbulence is a digital filter with three wall-normal partitions and a
time correlation in place of the streamwise one; the layer becomes "the
true turbulent boundary layer" near $x/\delta=-8$ ($x$ measured from
the corner), the inlet being at $x/\delta=-18.4$
(§II.C, §IV.B; see [inflow turbulence generation](/methods/inflow-turbulence-generation.md)).

**An entropy-bounded adaptive filter for shocks.**
[Gao et al. (2023)](/sources/gao-2023.md) replace the subcell finite
volumes of Gao et al. (2022) by the positivity-preserving, minimum-entropy
adaptive exponential modal filter of Dzanic & Witherden, applied only in
elements flagged by an improved Ducros indicator (and always above
$y=2\delta_r$); the filter strength is chosen so that the entropy does not
fall below its minimum over the neighbouring elements minus a tolerance of
$10^{-4}$ (§3.1.3, Eqs. 46–48; see
[explicit modal filtering](/methods/explicit-modal-filtering.md) and
[subcell finite-volume shock capturing](/methods/subcell-finite-volume-shock-capturing.md)).
Confining the filter, the authors argue, keeps its dissipation out of the
under-resolved turbulence, which on the split-form DGSEM is left to the
Riemann solver, and removes the need to split hyperbolic and parabolic
operators (§3.1.3). They believe the change of shock capturing between the
rigid and the elastic case has no influence near the wall, where no shock
capturing is active (§5).

**Implicit LES on a coarse wall-normal-resolved grid.**
[Kokkinakis et al. (2023)](/sources/kokkinakis-2023.md) run the RC-19
interaction as implicit LES, which they place under "coarse-grained
simulation" with explicit-model LES (§I): the finite-volume code CNS3D with
a Godunov-type upwind method, the HLLC Riemann solver, high-order WENO
reconstruction of the primitive variables (an "augmented 11th-order WENO"),
second-order central viscous terms, five-stage fourth-order SSP
Runge–Kutta, and digital-filter inflow about $21\delta_0$ ahead of the
wedge (§III B). They quote wall-resolved LES requirements of
$\Delta x^+\approx50$–130, $\Delta z^+\approx15$–30 and 10–30 points across
the layer with the first point at $y^+\lesssim1$ (§III B 2), and select a
grid with $\Delta x^+=717$, $\Delta z^+=634$ and $y^+=4.8$ by a four-grid
study of the planar case whose finest grid has $\Delta x^+=268$,
$\Delta z^+=100$, $y^+=2.7$; mean and RMS wall pressure "converge
gradually" and the selected grid agrees reasonably with the finest
(§IV A, Table III, Fig. 4). See
[grid-point requirements](/results/les-grid-point-requirements.md).

**RANS with a compressibility correction.**
[Zhao et al. (2023b)](/sources/zhao-2023-b.md) discretize the mean-flow
equations with interior-penalty
[discontinuous Galerkin](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin-spectral-element-method.md)
and the two [$k$-$\omega$ SST](/methods/k-omega-sst-model.md) equations with
first-order finite volumes, and capture shocks with an artificial viscosity
driven by the residual of the flux divergence (§3.1–3.2).

## What the case tests

**Agreement of LES with DNS at a fraction of the cost.**
[Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md), §III, validate the LES on
grids coarser than the DNS by about $4\times4\times2$ (32 times fewer points)
with a time step about 2.8 times larger. On the incoming boundary layers at
Mach 3, 7 and 10 the mean velocity and temperature are within 3% (5% at
Mach 10) and the peak turbulent kinetic energy within 2% of the top-hat
filtered DNS; the unresolved kinetic energy, estimated from filtered against
unfiltered DNS, is 14% to 25% at Mach 7 and 10% to 15% at Mach 3 and 10
(Figs. 2–5). On the separated Mach 3 ramp the LES reproduces the
separation length, the multi-inflection skin friction inside the bubble, the
wall-pressure plateau that excess numerical dissipation suppresses, peak
shear-layer intensities within 6%, and separation spectra peaking at
$St\approx0.01$–0.03 as in the DNS (Figs. 8–10). On the attached Mach 7 ramp
the skin friction differs from DNS by about 3% on the ramp, the heat-transfer
coefficient by 4%, and the peak reverse-flow probability at the corner is 31%
in both (Figs. 12–13). The authors state the LES costs "3% of the DNS
computational cost" (§V).

**The span of the domain.** [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md), §III C, Table III,
Fig. 8, report for the Mach 3 ramp a separation length of $2.93\delta$ in
the narrow-span DNS, $3.05\delta$ in the narrow-span LES ($2\delta_o$ span,
$\delta_o$ the reference thickness) and $3.71\delta$ in the wide-span LES
($4\delta_o$); they consider the effect physical (spanwise periodicity on the
order of $\delta$), note that many STBLI simulations use spans of about two
boundary-layer thicknesses, and state that the effect "has not been closely
addressed in the literature". The distributions collapse when plotted
against $x'/L_{\mathrm{sep}}$ ($x'$ the distance along the wall from the
corner). [Dang et al. (2022)](/sources/dang-2022.md), §6.1, §7, Figs. 23–25,
do not compare spans but run the Mach 2.9 ramp with a span of more than 29 boundary-layer
thicknesses and see a series of Taylor–Görtler vortices behind the shock,
with several small co-rotating vortices beneath one large vortex and
streak spacings that grow strongly in the interaction; this run is shown by
visualizations only, with no statistics.

**Hypersonic separated ramps.** For the two new LES datasets
[Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md), §IV, Table IV, report:

- mean separation lengths $6.2\delta$ (Mach 7, averaged over
  $3150\,\delta/U_e=510\,L_{\mathrm{sep}}/U_e$) and $4.1\delta$ (Mach 9.1,
  $450\,\delta/U_e=110\,L_{\mathrm{sep}}/U_e$);
- separation spectra peaking at $St_L=0.1$ with energy down to $St_L=0.03$,
  reattachment spectra broadband about $St_L\approx1$ (Fig. 15);
- maximum reverse flow about 12% of $U_e$; peak mean temperature in the
  bubble $8.7T_e$ and $12.5T_e$, 50% and 40% above the inviscid post-shock
  values (Fig. 21);
- peak $C_f$ 9.3 and 11.3 times the incoming value, the rms of $C_f$ up to
  about 20 times upstream, peak Stanton number 15.2 and 16.8 times the
  incoming value (Fig. 23);
- streamwise intensity in the separation shear layer twice the upstream
  maximum, anisotropy invariants moving at the corner toward the
  axisymmetric-compression branch typical of mixing layers, and a separation
  shear layer whose extra production is balanced by transport, convection,
  pressure diffusion and a dissipation dominated by its subgrid part
  (Figs. 24–27).

A record of $100\,L_{\mathrm{sep}}/U_e$, about three low-frequency cycles at
$St_L=0.03$, converges the separated mean flow to within 3%: ten overlapping
segments of the Mach 7 signal give mean separation positions within 3% of
the full average (§IV A, Fig. 16).

**Sensitivity to the subgrid model.** Removing the scale-similar terms
(dynamic eddy viscosity only) changes the Mach 3 separation length by less
than 5% and the attached Mach 7 corner reverse-flow probability from 31% to
35%, but lengthens the separated Mach 7 bubble by 30% (§IV F, Fig. 30). The
difference is not in the incoming layer, whose profiles are identical
(Fig. 31), but in the separation shear layer, whose spreading rate falls from
0.194 to 0.173 without the similarity term; less spreading means less
entrainment and a larger bubble (Figs. 32–33). The authors attribute the
sensitivity to the convective Mach number of about 2 of that shear layer
(about 1 in the Mach 3 case, from their earlier work) and conclude that the
conservative energy exchange of the scale-similar term is necessary there
(§V). The sensitivity is shown for one separated hypersonic case, with one
numerical scheme. See [scale-similarity and mixed models](/methods/scale-similarity-model.md);
the paper speaks of conservative energy exchange and does not use the term
[backscatter](/definitions/energy-backscatter.md).

**Engineering assumptions.** [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md),
§IV E, Figs. 28–29, test the relations RANS and design practice rely on in
the separated hypersonic flows on cold walls: the Reynolds analogy factor
$2C_h/C_f$ is not near one in the separated region ($C_f/2C_h$ is 0.85
upstream and about 0.7 downstream); the Back & Cuffel power law
$q/q_u=(p/p_u)^{0.85}$, derived for attached interactions, over-predicts the
heat transfer at separation and in the recovering layer; $Pr_t$ is within
13% of one upstream between 0.05 and 0.75 local boundary-layer thicknesses
from the wall, but about 1.4 downstream at Mach 9.1; the strong Reynolds analogies fail on these cold walls and the
modified analogy of Huang et al. holds only in parts of the layers. See
[Morkovin's hypothesis](/definitions/morkovin-hypothesis.md).

**Separation, plateau and unsteadiness of an impinging shock.**
[Gao et al. (2022)](/sources/gao-2022.md), §5.3, compare their implicit
LES with the LES of Morgan et al. that they cite (whose case is at
$M=2.05$ with a larger pressure ratio): separation at $16.7\delta_r$
against $17.6\delta_r$, reattachment at $19.5\delta_r$ against
$19.3\delta_r$, from an impingement point at $20\delta_r$ (Fig. 22). They
infer from Morgan et al.'s grid study and theirs that the separation point
is highly sensitive to resolution and conditions while reattachment is
not. A skin-friction plateau appears in the separated region, which,
following Morgan et al., appears only at sufficient resolution; the
authors take it as evidence of the DG accuracy on a grid much coarser than
the reference LES. The mean interaction length, from the mean
reflected-shock foot to the inviscid impingement point, is
$L_{\mathrm{int}}=3.8\delta_r$ (Fig. 23). Wall-pressure spectra show no
spurious low frequency from the synthetic inflow and a low-frequency peak
at $St=fL_{\mathrm{int}}/U_\infty\approx0.03$ near the reflected-shock
foot, consistent with the experiment of Threadgill & Bruce (§5.3.2,
Fig. 24); the separation spectra of
[Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md) on the Mach 3 ramp peak at
$St\approx0.01$–0.03 in a Strouhal number built on $L_{\mathrm{sep}}$, not
$L_{\mathrm{int}}$. The undisturbed boundary layer agrees with the DNS of
Wenzel et al. (see [turbulent boundary layers](/benchmarks/turbulent-boundary-layer.md)).
The cost was about 196,608 CPU hours on up to 16,384 cores; the
performance index rises 18.7% over the shock-free layer, within the
15–20% degradation of the hybrid scheme that they cite from Krais et al.
(§5.3.1–5.3.2).

**A swept ramp: three-dimensional separation without low-frequency
unsteadiness.** [Zhang et al. (2022c)](/sources/zhang-2022-g.md), §III,
analyse the Mach 6 swept ramp from the DNS fields alone (no experiment or
DNS exists at these conditions):

- *Separation.* There is no region of $C_f<0$ in the analysis plane, so the
  authors call the two-dimensional definition of separation by negative
  skin friction "unsuitable for 3D interactions" and locate separation and
  reattachment by critical-point theory, as converging and diverging wall
  streamlines (Figs. 4, 7).
- *Quasi-conical symmetry.* The extensions of the separation, reattachment
  and corner lines, and of the lines of peak pressure, friction and heat
  flux, meet near a virtual conical origin; separation and reattachment lie
  at azimuths of 32.4° and 51.2° about it, bow-shaped near the origin and
  straight farther away (Figs. 7, 13).
- *Crossflow.* The separated region coincides with a crossflow region in
  which the spanwise velocity exceeds the streamwise one (azimuth 32.4° to
  49°); the separated fluid moves toward the spanwise boundary as a helical
  vortex, not a closed bubble, and the crossflow is supersonic and keeps
  accelerating along the span, which the authors liken to a supersonic
  nozzle (Figs. 8–10).
- *Heating.* Peak skin friction and heat flux behind reattachment come from
  high-energy fluid that passes through the bow-shaped head of the main
  shock and impinges on the ramp, and they grow along the span as the
  main-shock angle grows; the authors call this analysis "qualitative
  rather than quantitative" because the peaks are not grid-converged
  (Figs. 11–17).
- *Unsteadiness.* Over about $1500\,\delta/U_\infty$ of wall pressure, the
  interaction is dominated by a mid-frequency band about one order of
  magnitude below the upstream boundary-layer frequency, about
  $3U_\infty/\delta$, while low-frequency energy at the separation line is
  very low and "can be negligible" against an unswept Mach 5 ramp
  experiment of Erengil & Dolling, in agreement with their later swept
  experiments (Figs. 18–20). The authors give two reasons: the separation
  shock is much weaker than the reattachment and main shocks, and the
  supersonic crossflow carries perturbations to the spanwise boundary, so
  the instability of the separated flow cannot act persistently on the
  separation shock. They add, without a test, that periodic spanwise
  boundaries in two-dimensional simulations let perturbations accumulate
  in the separated region (§III C).

Against the two-dimensional ramps and impinging shocks above, whose
separation spectra peak at $St_L\approx0.01$–0.1, this swept interaction
lacks the low-frequency separation-shock motion; the authors attribute the
difference to the three-dimensional crossflow and the weak separation
shock.

**Scaling of the interaction length.** Souverein et al.'s experimental law,
as assessed by [Gao et al. (2022)](/sources/gao-2022.md), §5.3.3, reads
$L_{\mathrm{sc}}=1.3(Se_{\mathrm{sc}})^{3.0}$, with the scaled interaction length
$L_{\mathrm{sc}}=L_{\mathrm{int}}\sin\beta_s\sin\varphi_s/(\delta_0^*\sin(\beta_s-\varphi_s))$
and the separation criterion $Se_{\mathrm{sc}}=k_{Se}\Delta p_s/q_0$ (page-local: shock
angle $\beta_s$, flow deflection $\varphi_s$, incoming displacement
thickness $\delta_0^*$, pressure rise $\Delta p_s$ across the reflection
system, dynamic pressure $q_0$, and a factor $k_{Se}$ printed as 3 for
$Re_{\theta_0}>10^4$ and 2.5 below). Their LES gives $L_{\mathrm{sc}}=2.92$ at
$Se_{\mathrm{sc}}=1.39$, 16.4% below the law; the experiment of Threadgill & Bruce
lies 7.8% below. Collecting 23 spanwise-periodic DNS and LES (Table 3),
they find the simulations below the law, especially for
$1.3<Se_{\mathrm{sc}}<1.8$, attribute the deficit to the sidewalls that periodic
simulations lack, consistent with Bermejo-Moreno et al. and Wang et al. as
they cite them, state that "modeling sidewall effects is compulsory" to
match experimental interaction sizes and Strouhal numbers, and fit
$L_{\mathrm{sc}}=0.95(Se_{\mathrm{sc}})^{3.15}$ for spanwise-periodic computations, recommending an
inflow 10–20$\delta_r$ upstream of the interaction for $\delta_0^*$
(Fig. 26). In their Table 3 a smaller span "might" give a larger $L_{\mathrm{sc}}$ and
a higher Reynolds number a larger $L_{\mathrm{sc}}$, both too small to matter for the
scaling.

**A laminar interaction as a resolution test.** On Katzer's laminar
interaction ($M=2$, $Re_{x}=3\times10^5$ at impingement, $p_3/p_1=1.4$)
[Gao et al. (2022)](/sources/gao-2022.md), §4, find Katzer's grid not
converged: the converged separation length, reached by DG at $N=4$ with
182,000 degrees of freedom and by second-order finite volumes with
409,500, is 5.87% larger than Katzer's, against 8.8% for the DNS of
Sansica et al. as cited; at equal degrees of freedom DG is closer to the
converged solution, and its run took 7680 CPU-minutes against 10,368
(Figs. 9, 13).

**Wall-modelled LES that keeps the no-slip condition.**
[De Vanna et al. (2022)](/sources/devanna-2022.md) assess the authors'
unified wall-resolved/wall-modelled LES (see
[wall-modelled LES](/methods/wall-modelled-les.md)): a compressible
[equilibrium wall model](/methods/equilibrium-wall-model.md) (mixing length
with van Driest damping in semi-local units, $\kappa=0.41$, $A^+=17$,
$Pr_t=0.9$) returns wall stress and heat flux, which are imposed by adding
a wall subgrid viscosity and conductivity to the molecular ones while the
no-slip and thermal conditions stay active; the model switches off where
the resolved wall spacings satisfy $\Delta x^+<40$, $\Delta y^+<3$ and
$\Delta z^+<15$, and the exchange point moves with the local resolution
(§2.2, §3.2). Convection is a sixth-order energy-preserving split scheme
hybridized with WENO-Z by a modified Ducros sensor (§3.1; see
[kinetic-energy preservation](/properties/kinetic-energy-preservation.md)).
Interaction length from the nominal impingement to the reflected-shock
foot (§4.6):

| Run | interaction length$/\delta_0$ |
|---|---|
| WMLES coarse / medium / fine | 7.0 / 5.2 / 5.0 |
| wall-resolved LES | 3.3 |
| experiment (Dupont et al.) | 4.18 |

- Because no-slip is kept, the wall-modelled runs return negative skin
  friction in the separated region, which the authors say classical
  equilibrium wall modelling "mainly" rules out (§4.4, Fig. 5a); the
  bubble is longer but shallower than in the wall-resolved run, with
  "gentle reverse flow" (§4.4–4.5).
- The averaged activation sensor exceeds 0.95 in the incoming and
  recovering layers and falls to about 0.65 in the bubble, which is more
  often treated as wall-resolved; the ratio of modelled to resolved wall
  stress peaks in the bubble (§4.4, Fig. 6).
- The authors attribute the oversized bubble to the wall model and to the
  subgrid and shock-capturing dissipation that "laminarize" it at low
  resolution (§4.5); scaled by each run's own interaction length, the
  bubble shape and the second-order statistics agree "impressively" with
  the PIV (§4.6, Figs. 9–13).
- Incoming layer at $Re_\tau=840$: the wall-modelled $C_f$ matches the
  wall-resolved one nearly regardless of resolution; for some statistics
  the medium grid fits the references best (§4.3).

They contrast their over-predicted bubble with the strong under-prediction
of separation that Gross et al., as cited, found with a standard
equilibrium wall model (§1).

**Immersed-boundary WMLES at hypersonic Mach numbers.**
[van Noordt et al. (2022)](/sources/vannoordt-2022.md) present what they
call the first hypersonic turbulent simulations with wall-modelled LES on
an [immersed boundary](/methods/immersed-boundary-method.md) (§1): a
Cartesian finite-difference solver (CHAMPS) blending a fourth-order
kinetic-energy- and entropy-preserving central flux with a third-order
WENO flux, the wall stress and heat flux of an equilibrium wall model
applied as viscous fluxes at the immersed wall (§2.3–2.6).

- *Mach 6 transitional impinging shock* (§3.3.1, Fig. 19): on the fine mesh
  the wall heat transfer matches the DNS of Sandham et al. as cited, with a
  marginal over-prediction near the end of the bubble; the coarse mesh is
  less favourable. Rotating the configuration by 0°–12° against the grid
  changes the peak transitional heat transfer by about 5% and grows the
  bubble slightly, which the authors attribute to the streamwise
  appearance of irregular cells (§3.3.2, Figs. 21–24); coarse–fine
  mesh-refinement interfaces reflect acoustic waves back into the layer,
  visible in $\langle v'v'\rangle$ (Fig. 24).
- *Mach 7.2, 8° ramp* (§3.4, Figs. 26–27): the mean flow is attached with
  significant instantaneous separation; the fine grid captures the wall
  pressure, the coarse grid raises the pressure early and over-predicts
  instantaneous separation, the minimum $C_f$ is "notably higher" than the
  DNS, and the post-shock adjustment takes about $4\delta_r$ on the coarse
  mesh; the authors suspect the imbalance of pressure gradient and
  convection that the equilibrium model neglects. The 11M-cell grid costs
  under 5% of the DNS in spatial requirement (§3.4). The attached ramp of
  [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md) (R8-M7), the same
  configuration and reference DNS, reaches 31% peak reverse-flow
  probability at the corner (§III D).
- The heat transfer is "very sensitive" to the artificial dissipation added
  at the immersed boundary (§4).

**Energy transfer in a compression/expansion ramp.**
[Tonicello et al. (2022)](/sources/tonicello-2022.md) validate their DNS
against Priebe & Martín, Zhu et al., Wu & Martín and the experiment of
Ringuette et al. as cited: the separation point is closer to the
experiment than in Priebe & Martín, the wall pressure follows Zhu et al.,
and downstream of the interaction the mean velocity agrees better with
the experiment than the DNS of Wu & Martín (§3.1, Figs. 3–4; incoming layer
in Table 3). The resolved kinetic-energy budget closes to nearly zero at
$y^+\approx30$, so numerical dissipation is negligible, and the artificial
viscosity contributes only of order $10^{-5}\rho_\infty u_\infty^3/\delta$
(§4.1, Figs. 17–18). Used a priori, with Favre time–span averaging and with
a differential filter of width $2h$, $4h$ and $8h$:

- the energy flux between resolved and unresolved motion is mostly
  forward, but even the Favre mean shows a large backscatter region near
  the expansion corner; compression goes with forward transfer and
  expansion with backscatter (§4.1, Fig. 14);
- the correlation of the transfer with the
  [pressure dilatation](/definitions/pressure-dilatation.md) is 0.2775 in
  the mean, 0.1936 and 0.2682 for the $4h$ and $8h$ filters (Table 4), and
  the maximum subgrid Mach number is about 0.32 in the mean and 0.42
  filtered (§4.2);
- split into deviatoric and spherical parts, the deviatoric flux is forward
  everywhere in the mean: the mean backscatter and most of the
  dilatation correlation come from the spherical part,
  $-\tfrac23\bar\rho k_{\mathrm{sgs}}\partial_k\tilde u_k$, which is
  negative in expansions (§5, Figs. 28–29, Table 6).

The authors propose, untested, a bulk-viscosity model for the trace of the
subgrid stress (§5); see [energy backscatter](/definitions/energy-backscatter.md)
and [bulk viscosity](/definitions/bulk-viscosity.md).

**Scale-by-scale turbulence amplification.**
[Yu et al. (2022)](/sources/yu-2022-e.md) derive a transport equation for
the spanwise two-point correlation of compressible flow (density-weighted
after Arun et al., as cited) and its spanwise spectrum (§3), and apply it
to their DNS, whose mild separation runs from $(x-x_{\mathrm{imp}})/\delta_0\approx-2.60$ to $-0.73$
($\delta_0$ the thickness at their reference plane), with separation length
1.87 and interaction length 3.27, consistent with Volpiani et al. as cited
(§2.2, Fig. 3a); Yu et al. (2023) print 1.65 for the same separation
length (see Contradictions).

- In the integrated budget the production by mean-flow deceleration rises
  first and shear production later, as Humble et al. and Fang et al.
  found (§4, Fig. 6); the pressure-dilatation and mass-diffusion terms
  matter only near the shocks.
- Scale by scale, the large scales ($\lambda_z\approx\delta_0$) are produced
  by deceleration alone and the small ones ($\lambda_z^+\approx100$) by
  deceleration and shear together, so the "deceleration first" sequence
  is "an integral effect", which the authors attribute to the low Reynolds
  number (§6.1, Fig. 10).
- The most energetic motions move from near-wall streaks at
  $\lambda_z^+\approx100$ to large scales $\lambda_z\approx0.9\delta_0$ at
  $y\approx0.4\delta_0$; the streamwise spectrum is amplified first, by up
  to 3.5 at $(x-x_{\mathrm{imp}})/\delta_0\approx-1.0$, the cross-stream ones later, through pressure
  strain (§5, Fig. 8; §6.2).
- Inter-scale transfer is forward overall; inverse transfer to large scales
  exists upstream but is small next to production; the spanwise-uniform
  motions inside the layer are fed mainly by inter-scale transfer
  (§6.4, §7, Figs. 13–14). The authors warn that the shock-capturing
  dissipation makes the spectral budget near the shocks unreliable (§7).

**Turbulence amplification over a ramp.**
[Shi & Yan (2023)](/sources/shi-2023.md) define the amplification factor
of a quantity as its wall-normal maximum at a station over that at the
reference station $x/\delta=-5.4$ (Eq. 34; $x$ from the corner) and find (§IV.C, Table IV):

| Quantity | maximum amplification | at $(x/\delta,y/\delta)$ |
|---|---|---|
| $\langle u'u'\rangle$ | 2.82 | $(-2.15, 0.091)$ |
| $\langle v'v'\rangle$ | 3.81 | $(-0.85, 0.384)$ |
| $\langle w'w'\rangle$ | 4.23 | $(-1.05, 0.329)$ |
| $k$ | 3.34 | $(-1.75, 0.164)$ |

(Averages over time and span, Reynolds fluctuations.) They
set the $k$ factor beside 2.97 (Fang et al.) and 2.7 (Pirozzoli & Grasso)
for the 33.2° impinging shock at Mach 2.25, different configurations. At
$x/\delta=-2.15$ the velocity inflection point and the spanwise-vorticity peak
lie at $y/\delta=0.033$ and 0.038, about a third of the height of the
$\langle u'u'\rangle$ peak, which the authors therefore do not attribute to
roll-up of the shear layer but to the negative streamwise convective
acceleration of the adverse pressure gradient (§IV.C, Figs. 24–27).
Splitting $P_k$ into a shear part and the parts from deceleration along
$x$ and $y$ (Eq. 36), and then into ramp-aligned components (Eqs. 37–39),
they conclude that deceleration by the separation shock dominates
production near separation, shear dominates in the separated region, the
acceleration on the ramp weakens production at the start of the
reattached shear layer, and the shear layer keeps amplifying turbulence
downstream (§IV.C, Figs. 28–32). On the ramp $P_k$ does not weaken
markedly against the flat plate, as in Tong et al., whereas downstream
production is much weaker in the impinging-shock case of Fang et al.; the
authors read this as a physical difference between the two
configurations (§IV.C). This agrees with the "deceleration first"
sequence that [Yu et al. (2022)](/sources/yu-2022-e.md) find in the
integrated budget of their impinging shock. The wall-pressure spectrum
moves from a characteristic $St=f\delta/U_\infty\approx1$ upstream to
$St\approx0.01$ near separation and in the separation shock (§IV.B,
Figs. 17–18), a Strouhal number on $\delta$, not on $L_{\mathrm{sep}}$ or
$L_{\mathrm{int}}$ as in the other sources. Validation: the incoming-layer
$C_f=2.28\times10^{-3}$ on the medium grid lies within the references
($2.17$ to $2.56\times10^{-3}$, $2.25\times10^{-3}$ in the experiment),
the corner $C_f$ and wall pressure converge on the medium and fine grids
to the WENO DNS but not on the coarse grid, although the incoming layer is
converged on all three (§IV.B, Table III, Figs. 11–12).

**Recovery downstream of the interaction.**
[Yu et al. (2023)](/sources/yu-2023.md) read the separation bubble of the
Yu et al. (2022) DNS as leaving a mixing layer "hidden" in the downstream
boundary layer, and split the mean velocity, the Reynolds shear stress
and the spanwise spectra into a canonical part, built from the reference
station and a composite van Driest-transformed wall law (Eq. 4.1), and a
mixing-layer residue (§4). Findings, in $X=(x-x_{\mathrm{imp}})/\delta_r$
(page-local) with $\delta_r$ the reference thickness:

- the mixing-layer velocity defect peaks at $y=0.2\delta$, its gradient at
  $y/\delta\approx0.65$, and it contributes under 4% of the mean velocity
  at $X=13.3$ and 16.7; the mean velocity recovers about 13 thicknesses
  downstream, near-wall turbulence (judged by $\langle v'v'\rangle$ and
  $\langle u'v'\rangle$, "detached" variables in the sense of the
  [attached-eddy hypothesis](/definitions/attached-eddy-hypothesis.md)) at
  $X=10$, while the outer stresses are still raised at the end of the
  domain (§3–§4.1, Figs. 5–7);
- in the Renard–Deck [decomposition of the skin friction](/definitions/skin-friction-decomposition.md),
  the canonical turbulent part stays near 50% of $C_f$ downstream as
  upstream, the mixing-layer turbulent part is mostly cancelled by a
  negative spatial-growth term, and the net mixing-layer contribution is
  below 15% of $C_f$ and about 11% from $X\approx10$, which the authors
  call "trivial" (§4.2, Fig. 10);
- the mixing-layer stresses peak at $y=0.5\delta$, with an anisotropy
  (stress over $2k$) of 0.49, 0.23, 0.28 and 0.15 for the $uu$, $vv$, $ww$
  and $-uv$ components, nearly constant from $y/\delta\approx0.3$ to 1, on
  the axisymmetric-expansion line of the Lumley triangle and with stress
  ratios of free mixing layers at convective Mach numbers from 0.32 to 0
  (§5.1, App. A, Figs. 13–14, 19; see
  [barycentric anisotropy map](/definitions/barycentric-anisotropy-map.md));
- in the outer layer dissipation balances about 80% of production, and
  the pressure–strain keeps the axisymmetric-expansion state with
  $\phi_{22}\approx\phi_{33}\approx-0.5\phi_{11}$ (the diagonal
  pressure–strain components, $\phi_{ij}$ as on the [Reynolds-stress model](/methods/reynolds-stress-model.md) page; the source's $\Pi_{ij}$) (§5.2, Figs. 15, 17);
- an integral model of the mixing-layer mean and turbulent kinetic energy
  with assumed self-similar profiles reproduces the DNS decay and predicts
  that the outer layer needs more than 50 boundary-layer thicknesses to
  recover, an extrapolation beyond the domain ($X\le16.7$) (§6,
  Eqs. 6.1–6.8, Fig. 18).

**Beyond RANS.** Two sources agree that RANS is not adequate for separated
interactions: [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md), §I, call RANS
"notoriously unreliable" for separated STBLIs, with large errors in
separation length and wall heat transfer, and
[Hoy & Bermejo-Moreno (2022)](/sources/hoy-2022.md), §1, state that RANS
cannot predict the strong separation and low-frequency dynamics, while DNS
and wall-resolved LES are too costly at experimental Reynolds numbers.
[Thawait et al. (2023)](/sources/thawait-2023.md), §I, likewise call RANS
"not suitable" for "unsteadiness in the shock and separated region, and
shock-boundary layer interaction", citing Sartor & Timme, as the motivation
of the SST-based delayed DES of their control fin (see
[detached-eddy simulation](/methods/detached-eddy-simulation.md)).
[Kiris et al. (2023)](/sources/kiris-2023.md), §I, list
canonical shock/boundary-layer interactions among the academic WMLES of
nonequilibrium boundary layers whose grid spacing per unit span is much
finer than in full-aircraft WMLES.
[Dang et al. (2022)](/sources/dang-2022.md), §1, name the two obstacles of
DNS of such flows as the conflict between robustness at shocks and low
dissipation, and the cost of long domains, small time steps and the long
records of low-frequency shock motion.

On the Settles cavity–ramp at Mach 2.92,
[Zhao et al. (2023b)](/sources/zhao-2023-b.md), §5.4, Figs. 5-14–5-15, find
that the uncorrected SST model "produces excessive turbulent viscosity" in
the compressible shear layer, which widens the shear layer and the shock and
expansion regions, while the model with the compressibility correction of
Suzen & Hoffmann reproduces the measured velocity profiles at three
shear-layer stations; the comparison is of profiles only, with no
separation length or wall heat transfer, the quantities on which Helm &
Martín judge RANS unreliable. On the transonic NACA 0012 at Mach 0.804 the
same solver places the shock near $x/c\approx0.47$ in agreement with the
ONERA data but ends it slightly downstream, which the authors attribute to
an over-estimated shock–boundary-layer interaction (§5.3, Figs. 5-9–5-11);
see [NACA 0012 airfoil](/benchmarks/naca-0012-airfoil.md).

**Transonic buffet on a supercritical airfoil.** At transonic speed the
shock on the suction side of an airfoil interacts with the turbulent
boundary layer and, past a critical incidence, the shock-foot and
trailing-edge separations couple into a self-sustained shock oscillation
(buffet). [Petrocchi & Barakos (2024)](/sources/petrocchi-2024.md) compute
the ONERA OAT15A airfoil of the experiment of Jacquin et al. (chord 0.23 m,
$M_\infty=0.73$, $Re_c=3\times10^6$; as Petrocchi & Barakos cite it, unsteadiness from
$\alpha=3.1^\circ$ and established buffet at $3.5^\circ$ with a single
frequency of 69 Hz, $St=0.06$) in half of the S3Ch wind tunnel, with slip
upper and lower walls shaped from a preliminary RANS, a viscous side wall
and transition fixed at $x/c=0.07$ (§1, §4.1, Table 1):

- [partially-averaged Navier–Stokes](/methods/partially-averaged-navier-stokes.md)
  on [$k$-$\omega$ SST](/methods/k-omega-sst-model.md), with $f_\varepsilon=1$
  and $f_k$ recomputed every step from the ratio of grid size to modelled
  length scale, clipped below at 0.6, predicts buffet at $\alpha=3.5^\circ$
  on all three grids ($5.0$, $10$ and $16.7$ million cells), whereas SST
  URANS converges to a steady state; in two dimensions SST URANS gives no
  buffet even at $3.9^\circ$ (§4.3, §4.5.5, Table 2);
- on the finest grid PANS reproduces the stall cells and undulating shock
  front of the oil-flow and schlieren images, mean and RMS $C_p$ and the
  LDV velocity profiles, with a buffet frequency of 50–60 Hz against 69 Hz
  (§4.5.3–4.5.6, Figs. 8–15);
- the results depend on the tunnel back pressure (an inlet–outlet ratio of
  0.99 raises the oscillation amplitude), the trip location and the lower
  clip of $f_k$ (0.4 puts the shock too far upstream) (§4.5.2, App. C);
- PANS costs about 3% more than URANS per time step (§4.6, Table 3).

The authors attribute the URANS failure to excessive corner separation at
the side wall, which keeps the shock-foot and trailing-edge separations
from merging, and to a too high post-shock eddy viscosity (§4.5.5). Their
literature table (App. A, Table A.4) shows a wide scatter of URANS buffet
predictions on this case across models and solvers. This is a transonic,
two-dimensional-airfoil interaction in a confined tunnel, not the
supersonic flat-wall configurations above.

**Data assimilation into RANS of an impinging shock.**
[Fanizza et al. (2025)](/sources/fanizza-2025.md), §4.3, assimilate their
own DNS of an oblique shock impinging on a turbulent boundary layer
($M_\infty=2.28$, deflection 9.5°, adiabatic wall, domain
$(7,55)\delta_0\times(0,15)\delta_0$) into steady compressible RANS with the
[Spalart–Allmaras model](/methods/spalart-allmaras-model.md) and the
Spalart–Garbaruk low-Reynolds correction, discretized with modal
[discontinuous Galerkin](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin-spectral-element-method.md)
methods. Baseline SA mispredicts the bubble size. Four correction fields are
inferred by adjoint optimization (see
[field inversion](/methods/field-inversion-machine-learning.md)): a
momentum and energy source $\rho\boldsymbol f_{\mathrm{DA}}$, a multiplier
of the eddy viscosity, a source $\rho\tilde\nu g_{\tilde\nu}$ ($g_{\tilde\nu}$ page-local; the source's $f_{\tilde\nu}$) and a
production multiplier in the SA equation. Normalized errors after
assimilation (velocity / $C_p$ / $C_f$; values above 1 are worse than the
baseline):

| Correction | dense velocity data, wall-weighted (Table 1) | sparse wall $C_p$ data (Table 2) |
|---|---|---|
| momentum and energy source | 0.049 / 0.1825 / 0.0998 | 0.488 / 0.00106 / 0.8540 |
| eddy-viscosity multiplier | 0.250 / 0.5788 / 0.5689 | 0.458 / 0.17357 / 1.657 |
| SA source | 0.257 / 0.2076 / 0.8920 | 0.542 / 0.04961 / 1.700 |
| SA production multiplier | 0.339 / 0.2829 / 1.1450 | 0.427 / 0.05982 / 1.559 |

- With dense data only the momentum source reduces the cost by one to two
  orders of magnitude, the others stalling on a plateau (Fig. 9); it alone
  improves quantities absent from the cost (§4.3.1), and its corrected
  profiles match the DNS in the incoming layer, at separation, at the shock
  reflection and downstream, with $C_f$ still wrong in the first part of
  the bubble (Figs. 11–12). The authors give as a second reason that the
  three other corrections "are still constrained by the Boussinesq
  assumption", while the source corrects the divergence of the Reynolds
  stress directly, in an interaction where the Reynolds stress is not
  aligned with the viscous stress (§4.3.1; see
  [Boussinesq hypothesis](/definitions/boussinesq-hypothesis.md)).
- With wall-pressure data only, the momentum source cuts the $C_p$ error by
  three orders of magnitude but leaves $C_f$ wrong, and the three
  Boussinesq-type corrections make $C_f$ worse than the baseline over the
  whole wall (§4.3.3, Figs. 14–15). An $L^2$ penalty on the correction does
  not cure it; the authors call for regularization with physical prior
  knowledge (§4.3.4, §5).
- The lowest cost minimum on each grid is reached at the lowest
  polynomial degree that is grid-converged for the baseline ($N=1$ on the
  fine grid, $N=2$ on the coarse one); converged steady solutions are needed
  at every optimizer iteration, and wall quantities need reference data on
  grids refined near the wall (§4.3.2, Fig. 13).

**Buffet in a transonic compressor cascade.**
[Klose et al. (2025)](/sources/klose-2025.md) (read in the ASME accepted
manuscript) simulate the transonic cascade TCTA of DLR at its design point
(inlet Mach number 1.21, $Re=1.35\times10^6$ on the chord, inlet angle
145.7°), where a normal bow shock stands at mid-chord on the suction side
and oscillates at low frequency over a laminar or transitional boundary
layer with shock-induced separation. The LES is an
[implicit LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md) with the DG solver of
[TRACE](/software/trace.md): nodal DGSEM at $N=3$, an entropy-conserving
split form, and the
[subcell finite-volume shock capturing](/methods/subcell-finite-volume-shock-capturing.md)
of Hennemann et al. The comparison is a second-order finite-volume URANS
with [$k$-$\omega$ SST](/methods/k-omega-sst-model.md) and the
$\gamma$-$Re_\theta$ transition model. Shock position $x_s$ along the chord
(Table 2):

| Run | span | shock frequency $f c/U_{\mathrm{in}}$ | mean $x_s/c$ | max. amplitude of $x_s/c$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LES5 (coarse) | 5% $c$ | 0.165 | 0.537 | 0.278 |
| LES10 (coarse) | 10% $c$ | 0.164 | 0.531 | 0.172 |
| LES20 (coarse) | 20% $c$ | 0.163 | 0.528 | 0.158 |
| LES10-HR (fine) | 10% $c$ | 0.163 | 0.535 | 0.231 |
| URANS | — | 0.161 | 0.504 | 0.072 |
| experiment | 1.68$c$ (tunnel) | about 0.14 | — | 0.13 |

- *Span.* The shock frequency and mean position are almost insensitive to
  the span, while the amplitude and the separation bubble shrink as the
  span grows. The authors read this as partial agreement with Touber &
  Sandham (bubble size sensitive to span), but with a frequency "less
  dependent on the bubble size" (§5); a Strouhal number of 0.03 on the
  separation length, which Klose et al. take from Touber & Sandham, gives
  $L_{\mathrm{sep}}/c\approx0.18$, inside the observed 0.12–0.22.
- *Resolution.* Refining at 10% span changes little upstream of the shock
  but raises the amplitude from 0.172 to 0.231; the authors assume that
  numerical dissipation of the coarse LES and, more, of the URANS damps the
  structures that drive the larger oscillation, and call the coarse runs
  under-resolved (§6).
- *URANS.* It needs $\Delta t=2.8\times10^{-5}c/U_{\mathrm{in}}$ to get the
  shock motion; it captures the frequency but puts the shock upstream,
  gives less than half the LES20 amplitude and a mostly laminar suction
  side with wrong surface distributions (§6, Fig. 7).
- *Mechanism.* SPOD shows a dominant mode at $fc/U_{\mathrm{in}}=0.16$ and
  harmonics at 0.32, 0.48 and 0.64; as the shock moves downstream the bubble
  grows, perturbations travel upstream through the subsonic part of the
  boundary layer and produce intermittent oblique compression waves, driven
  by shocklets running upstream from the pressure side (§7). All runs give
  $St\approx0.16$ against about 0.14 measured. The cascade itself is on the
  [compressor cascade](/benchmarks/compressor-cascade.md) page.

**A learned correction of wall-modelled LES behind a ramp.**
[Zangeneh (2021)](/sources/zangeneh-2021.md), §III.B–C, Figs. 10–19, runs
the Mach 2.9 expansion–compression corner with SST-based improved delayed
DES as a wall-modelled LES (the RANS inner layer solved on an auxiliary
wall-refined grid that returns wall stress and heat flux to the LES) and
corrects it with a random-forest regression of the wall-stress and
heat-flux errors, trained at $Re_\delta=19{,}983.8$ against the DNS of
Fang et al. and tested at $Re_\delta=40{,}934.2$; the corrected wall
values are fed back through the eddy viscosity and the turbulent Prandtl
number (see [machine-learning wall models](/methods/machine-learning-wall-model.md)).
The baseline under-predicts $C_f$ on the plate, on the ramp and on the
plateau of the separation; the corrected run improves the separation
length, the mean reattachment point, the Stanton-number peak downstream of
reattachment and the ramp heat flux, the size of the recirculation under
the λ-shock system, the spanwise spacing of the Görtler-type convergence
lines near reattachment, and the profiles and Reynolds stresses at eight
stations, most in the separation region. A model trained on this flow and
on the supersonic flat plate together (see
[turbulent boundary layers](/benchmarks/turbulent-boundary-layer.md))
improves less, which the author reads as a training set "far from being
representative" (§III.C, §IV). The evidence is the comparison of plotted
curves; no error norm is printed.

**An internal interaction: the shock train.**
[Kumar & Ghosh (2022)](/sources/kumar-2022-b.md), §3.2.3, Figs. 30–36,
run a DNS of a shock train in a plane isolator without sidewalls, fed by a
periodic supersonic channel DNS run concurrently and coupled by MPI, with
the outlet pressure held at about twice the inlet value (see
[turbulent channel flow](/benchmarks/turbulent-channel-flow.md) for the
inflow channel and [WENO scheme](/methods/weno-scheme.md) for the
reconstruction). Several shocks form near the inlet and the train extends
to $x/L_x=0.3$; centreline pressure and density rise in compressions, fall
in expansions and grow slowly in the mixing region, with the Mach number
doing the opposite, as in an LES of a shock train in a pipe that they cite;
the shocks are not always symmetric about the centreline, the flow
separates near the shocks with boundary-layer thickening, and near-wall
sweeps and ejections intensify through them. The centreline streamwise
Reynolds stress, normalized by the inlet wall shear stress, jumps in
compressions, falls in expansions, grows up to $x/L_x=0.78$ in the mixing
region and stays above its inlet value at the outlet. There is no
reference data for this case; the comparison is qualitative.

A shock train also sits in the isolator of the cavity-flameholder
scramjet combustor that [Fureby & Nilsson (2023)](/sources/fureby-2023.md)
run in reacting LES (isolator-entrance Mach number 2.2, subgrid closure the
[localized dynamic $k$-equation model](/methods/subgrid-kinetic-energy-equation-model.md),
which they state "performs better than many other subgrid models with
respect to turbulent boundary layers, mixing and shock-boundary layer
interactions", citing their earlier studies, §3.2). At the intermediate
stagnation temperature the flame switches between a jet-wake and a
cavity-stabilized mode at about 60 Hz, and the shock train moves with it,
retreating almost to the start of the isolator during the high-intensity
phase (§6.4, Figs. 7, 9–10); mean and rms wall pressures agree well with the
experiment of Micka & Driscoll, the rms being highest in the isolator
(§6.7, Fig. 13). The combustion content is on the Source page.

**Wall models in supersonic interactions.**
[Bose & Park (2018)](/sources/bose-2018.md), §2.1, §4.4, Fig. 6b, cite a
nonequilibrium wall model with a wall-normal variation of the von Kármán
constant (Kawai & Larsson 2013) gives the skin friction of a Mach 1.7
oblique-shock interaction (a $6^\circ$ shock generator) closer to the
experiment of Souverein et al. than the same model with constant
$\kappa=0.4$ (Balaras et al. 1996); WMLES with an equilibrium model
over-predicts the secondary corner flow and corner shear stress in a
supersonic square duct, which the review reads as an inadequacy of the
locally one-dimensional equilibrium approximation for secondary flows;
the review also lists a duct interaction at Mach about 2 and
$Re_\theta\approx1.4\times10^4$ (Bermejo-Moreno et al.) and a compression
ramp with a delayed initial pressure rise (Dawson et al.).
See [nonequilibrium wall model](/methods/nonequilibrium-wall-model.md) and
[equilibrium wall model](/methods/equilibrium-wall-model.md). The
experimental interaction-length law of Souverein et al. is the one that
Gao et al. assess above.

**A transonic bump with shock-induced separation.**
[Heinz (2020)](/sources/heinz-2020.md), §5.3, Tables 4–5, Figs. 12–14,
compiles results on the axisymmetric transonic bump of Bachalo & Johnson
($M_\infty=0.875$, $Re_c=2.763\times10^6$ on the bump chord of 20.32 cm,
on a pipe of 15.24 cm diameter extending 61 cm upstream), where the flow
separates under the shock and the adverse pressure gradient at a point
not fixed by the geometry; the measured reattachment is $x_R=1.10$ in
bump chords ($x_R$ registry §11). In the cited simulations, DES on
$4.7\times10^8$ and $1.6\times10^9$ cells and DES with an embedded DNS on
$8\times10^9$ cells (Spalart et al.) give a DNS reattachment of 1.18 (7.3%
error, attributed to a small domain) and an LES of 1.08; wall-modelled LES
with equilibrium and nonequilibrium models (Iyer et al.) cannot match both
the $C_f$ maximum at the shock and the one at reattachment by any choice
of the exchange location (first or third point). The author uses the case to
argue "inconsistent improvements" of hybrid methods; see
[hybrid RANS/LES](/definitions/hybrid-rans-les.md).

[He et al. (2022)](/sources/he-2022-b.md) run steady RANS of the same bump
at six Mach numbers, $M_\infty=0.6$ to 0.925, in SU2 (verified against
CFL3D, grid-independent from $181\times81$ nodes), with the LES of Uzun &
Malik as reference at 0.875 (§2.4–2.5). The Spalart–Allmaras model with the
quadratic constitutive relation (QCR) delays the shock and the
shock-induced separation for $M_\infty\ge0.85$, whereas SST with QCR
predicts the wall pressure well; both under-predict the eddy viscosity
downstream of the shock (Figs. 5–6). Against the LES, the SA-QCR eddy
viscosity is too high in the bubble and too low in the incoming layer, the
shear layer, the reattached layer and at the shock front, the last
attributed to shock oscillation, "conceptually impossible to be modeled by
steady RANS" (§2.7, Fig. 7). A random forest trained on nine bounded local
features predicts the eddy-viscosity discrepancy with an $L_2$ error of
11.0–12.4% when interpolating ($M_\infty=0.9$) and 14.7–16.3% when
extrapolating (0.925), against SST-QCR data, all a priori on frozen mean
fields (§4.3, Table 4); Shapley-value explanations of the model trained on
the LES rank the eddy-viscosity ratio first, then the normalized
Q-criterion, a wall-proximity measure and a pressure-gradient ratio
(§5.1, Fig. 13); see
[field inversion and machine learning](/methods/field-inversion-machine-learning.md)
and [model-form uncertainty](/definitions/model-form-uncertainty.md).

**Interactions in turbomachinery.** In the review of
[Sandberg & Michelassi (2022)](/sources/sandberg-2022.md), §3.3.4, the
interaction on curved transonic blades bifurcates into a λ-shock, its
low-frequency oscillation lies about two orders of magnitude below the
energetic frequencies of the boundary layer, and whether it is a global
mode or triggered by the upstream layer is open; in a high-pressure
turbine, turbulent spots modulate the interaction (Dupuy et al. 2020); in
a transonic compressor the low-frequency shock oscillation moves the shock
by up to 10% of the axial chord, and URANS does not predict the correct
amplitude (Hergt et al. 2019). In an LES of a transonic turbine vane that
they cite, the interaction of the shock with trailing-edge vortex
shedding, not the passage shock itself, adds loss through additional
turbulent-kinetic-energy production in the wake (§3.2.2). On the compressor cascade above,
[Klose et al. (2025)](/sources/klose-2025.md) print a URANS shock
amplitude of 0.072 against 0.158–0.278 for their LES runs (Table 2); see
[compressor cascade](/benchmarks/compressor-cascade.md) and
[turbine cascade](/benchmarks/turbine-cascade.md).

**Shocks in decaying turbulence without a wall.** The supersonic
[Taylor–Green vortex](/benchmarks/taylor-green-vortex.md) at $M_0=1.25$
of [Chapelier et al. (2024)](/sources/chapelier-2024.md) tests the same
shock-capturing families (artificial diffusivity, subcell finite volumes,
TENO, hybrid central/WENO) on shock–turbulence interaction without a
boundary layer.

**Fluid–structure coupling.** In the impinging-shock case of
[Hoy & Bermejo-Moreno (2022)](/sources/hoy-2022.md), §4.3, panel flexibility
moves separation about $3\delta_0$ upstream and lengthens it from
$7.8\delta_0$ to $12.6\delta_0$, lowers the peak wall pressure and shifts the
dominant frequencies down.

[Gao et al. (2023)](/sources/gao-2023.md), §5.3–5.4, find the same trend
with high-order solvers on both sides (Figs. 20–30). The panel oscillates at
3225 Hz with an overall downward deformation; separation and reattachment
move from $-3.38$ and $-0.5$ (rigid) to $-3.42$ and $0.87$ (elastic) in
$(x-x_{\mathrm{imp}})/\delta_r$, a separated area 49.0% larger and a longer
pressure plateau, consistent, as they say, with Hoy & Bermejo-Moreno
(Fig. 26). The mean deformation creates an expansion fan upstream that
postpones separation, then a compression that raises the downstream
pressure, then an expansion at the rear of the panel (Fig. 27). The wall
pressure gains a low frequency at the panel frequency ($St\approx0.01$ on
$L_{\mathrm{int}}=3.8\delta_r$), while the separation-induced low frequency,
$St\approx0.03$ after high-pass filtering, is about that of the rigid case
(§5.4, Fig. 30). The run took $8.0\times10^5$ CPU hours on 16,384 cores
(§5.1, Table 4).

**Static surface deformations.**
[Kokkinakis et al. (2023)](/sources/kokkinakis-2023.md) impose on the panel
six static buckling shapes (sine modes 1 to 3 of either sign along the
panel, one half-wave across it, Eq. 1), with the mode-1 amplitude taken from
the measured panel response, and compare them with the planar panel
(§II A, Table II). Along the midspan the mean and RMS wall pressures change
by about 10% over the whole panel length, the change fading toward the
side edges; where a mode raises the peak pressure at the interaction it
lowers the pressure after it, and vice versa (§IV B, Figs. 5–6). Modes whose
surface rises toward the flow at the interaction raise the peak mean
pressure, by about $\pm10\%$ (§IV C, Fig. 7). The wall-pressure contours
bend concave or convex across the span, and the near-wall mean wall-normal
and spanwise velocities change sign several times but stay small (at most
1.75% and 15% of the streamwise one) (§IV C, Figs. 7–9). In the
[barycentric map](/definitions/barycentric-anisotropy-map.md) the
deformations leave the componentality state unchanged and bend its spatial
topology; the shock drives the near-wall turbulence from two-component
toward one-component, and at $y^+=2063$ the deformations leave no trace
(§IV D, Figs. 10–13). Since mode 3, with about a quarter of the mode-1
amplitude, gives a comparable response, the authors conclude that the
deformation rate matters "just as" much as the amplitude (§V). The paper
compares with no experiment.

## Qualifications

- [Zhang et al. (2022c)](/sources/zhang-2022-g.md) print in the abstract
  that the grid "exceeds $5\times10^6$" points, while §II prints
  $2.06\times10^9$ and $5.51\times10^9$. They define the azimuth from "the
  projection line of the line VCO-P in the x–y plane and the positive z
  axis" (§III A, Fig. 7 caption), and label the horizontal axis of
  Fig. 17(b) $\theta$ where the text speaks of the azimuth. The upstream
  forcing is called a "multi-frequency sine wave disturbance" in §II B and
  "blowing and suction" in §III C.

- Coordinates: [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md) write $z$
  wall-normal and $y$ spanwise, hat for the Favre filter and a second hat for
  the test filter; this page uses $y$ wall-normal, and the subgrid model is
  described in words. They define $u_\tau=(\tau_w/\rho_e)^{1/2}$ with the
  edge density, where the registry uses the wall density, and a recovery
  factor 0.9 in $T_r$ (the sources cited for $T_r$ on
  [turbulent boundary layers](/benchmarks/turbulent-boundary-layer.md) use
  0.89).
- [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md) give the increase of the
  Mach 7 separation length without the scale-similar term as "as much as a
  32%" in the abstract and as 30% in §IV F and §V; their abstract gives the
  LES time step as three times the DNS one, §V as 2.8 times. Their
  Table III prints the wide-span DNS row (R24-M3w-D) identical in every
  column to the narrow-span row, although the text says the two separation
  lengths differ, and several figure captions misname the cases (e.g.
  "R24-M10-L", "R35-M10-L" for R34-M10-L).
- The Mach 7 and Mach 9.1 separated ramps of
  [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md) have no DNS or experimental
  reference; they are validated only through the Mach 3 and attached Mach 7
  cases. §V calls the 32-fold reduction in grid points "equivalent to 3%
  of the DNS computational cost" and discusses the larger time step and the
  model overhead separately. §III B prints the unresolved energy at the wall
  of the Mach 7 layer both as 25% and as "a maximum of 18%".
- [Dang et al. (2022)](/sources/dang-2022.md) were read in an author
  manuscript. Their validation (mean wall pressure, van Driest velocity
  profile against the DNS of Martín et al. and the experiment of Bookey et
  al.) is on the $16100\times240\times200$ grid; the wide-span run has no
  quantitative comparison, and its averaging time is not stated.
- The cavity–ramp of [Zhao et al. (2023b)](/sources/zhao-2023-b.md) is a
  reattaching cavity shear layer, not the boundary layer meeting a ramp or
  an impinging shock of the other configurations; its incoming Reynolds
  number and grid resolution are not printed.
- [Petrocchi & Barakos (2024)](/sources/petrocchi-2024.md) compare only
  with the experiment of Jacquin et al. and do not state the filter width
  $\Delta$ that enters their dynamic $f_k$. Their Table 3 percentages of
  the cost increase (for example "+3.1%" for total times of 2.00 against
  1.95 s, which give +2.6%) cannot be reproduced from the printed times
  *(compiler check, not in the sources)*; the text says "about 3%". Their
  own SST URANS gives no buffet at $3.5^\circ$, while other SST URANS
  results in their Table A.4 do; the scatter is recorded by the authors.

- [Fanizza et al. (2025)](/sources/fanizza-2025.md) do not print the
  Reynolds number of their interaction. Their §4.3.1 says the three
  non-source corrections reduce all errors "by less than one order of
  magnitude", while Table 1 gives the production multiplier a $C_f$ error
  of 1.1450, above the baseline. Their Tables 3 and 4 print different
  penalty weights for two of the corrections, and their §4.3.4 says the
  penalty degrades velocity and wall-pressure errors although Table 3
  against Table 2 shows the velocity error improving for the eddy-viscosity
  multiplier and the SA source at both penalty weights and for the
  production multiplier at one of them.
- In [Klose et al. (2025)](/sources/klose-2025.md) the amplitude is not
  grid-converged (0.172 to 0.231 under refinement at fixed span), so the
  "very good" agreement of the experimental 0.13 with the coarse LES20
  (0.158) is not a converged comparison, as the authors concede for a
  refined LES20. Their resolution is "deemed sufficient for LES" with
  maxima of $\Delta x^+=65$, $\Delta y^+=2.5$, $\Delta z^+=40$ per
  polynomial degree on the coarse grid, which §6 calls under-resolved. No
  inflow turbulence is prescribed (measured level below 1%).

  $L_{\mathrm{int}}=3.8\delta_r$, $\delta_r=0.507$ mm, $\delta_0^*=0.1139$ mm,
  $\beta_s=37.2^\circ$, $\varphi_s=8^\circ$ gives $L_{\mathrm{sc}}=2.917$

  reference station and the reattachment point.

## Contradictions

- **Separation length of the Mach 2.28 impinging-shock DNS.** *Open.*
  [Yu et al. (2022)](/sources/yu-2022-e.md), §2.2, Fig. 3a, place
  separation and reattachment at $(x-x_{\mathrm{imp}})/\delta_0\approx-2.60$
  and $-0.73$, where $C_f=0$, and print $L_{\mathrm{int}}\approx3.27$ and
  $L_{\mathrm{sep}}\approx1.87$ in units of $\delta_0$, the thickness at the
  reference plane $x=15\delta_{\mathrm{in}}$.
  [Yu et al. (2023)](/sources/yu-2023.md), §2, analyse the same database
  with the same reference plane $x_r=15.0\delta_{\mathrm{in}}$ and write
  "we reported that the lengths of the interaction and separation zones
  are $L_{\mathrm{int}}\approx3.27\delta_r$ and $L_{\mathrm{sep}}\approx1.65\delta_r$",
  both papers adding "consistent with" Volpiani et al. (2018, figure 12).
  The interaction lengths agree; the separation lengths printed for the
  same data and the same reference thickness differ.
- **Anisotropy branch of mixing layers.** *Open.* In the Lumley triangle,
  [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md), §IV D, Fig. 26, call the
  left side "axisymmetric turbulence compression", "typical of mixing
  layer data" (citing Pope's *Turbulent Flows*), and the bottom right side
  "the boundary of axisymmetric turbulence expansion which is
  characteristic of the logarithmic layer", with the one-component state
  at the top right corner. [Yu et al. (2023)](/sources/yu-2023.md), §5.1,
  App. A, place the anisotropy of the mixing layer hidden in their
  recovering layer, with $(R_{11},R_{22},R_{33},-R_{12})/2k=(0.49,0.23,0.28,0.15)$,
  "on the line that represents the axisymmetric expansion" and call it
  "the typical state of turbulence in a mixing layer (Pope 2000)". With
  these printed ratios and their invariants (Eq. A2) the third invariant
  $b_{ij}b_{jk}b_{ki}/3\approx2.1\times10^{-3}$ is positive, the
  eigenvalues of $b_{ij}$ being about $0.225$, $-0.053$, $-0.172$ (one
  large, two small) *(compiler check, not in the sources)*. The two papers
  give the same name, axisymmetric expansion, to the side that Helm &
  Martín call characteristic of the logarithmic layer and Yu et al.
  typical of mixing layers, each citing Pope for the mixing-layer state.

# Shock–turbulent boundary layer interaction

A shock–turbulent boundary layer interaction (STBLI) arises when a
[turbulent boundary layer](/benchmarks/turbulent-boundary-layer.md) at
supersonic or hypersonic free-stream Mach number meets a shock, either
generated by the wall itself (a **compression ramp** of angle $\phi_r$,
page-local) or by an external body (an **impinging oblique shock**). A
strong enough interaction separates the layer: in the ramps of
[Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md) the Mach 7, 8° case stays
attached while the 24°, 33° and 34° cases separate, with a separation shock
forming inside the incoming layer ahead of the bubble, a separation shear
layer over it, and reattachment on the ramp (§III C–D, §IV B, Fig. 17). The quantities a simulation must predict are the mean
separation length $L_{\mathrm{sep}}$ (page-local: the distance between the
zero crossings of the mean $C_f$), the wall pressure, skin friction and heat
transfer through the interaction, and the low-frequency unsteadiness of the
separation shock, measured by the Strouhal number $St_L=fL_{\mathrm{sep}}/U_e$
(page-local, $U_e$ the boundary-layer-edge velocity). The case combines the
compressible boundary layer, a separated shear layer and shock motion, and is
expensive because the low-frequency cycle needs long records.

Both sources run a Mach 2.9, 24° ramp: Dang et al. validate against the DNS
of Martín et al. and the experiment of Bookey et al. (§5.1), and Helm &
Martín's R24-M3 DNS is that of Priebe & Martín (their ref. [35]). The
printed incoming conditions are close (edge temperature 108.9 K in Helm &
Martín, Table II; free-stream 108.1 K in Dang et al., Table 8), but neither
paper states that the two set-ups are identical. Helm & Martín's attached
Mach 7 case reproduces, through the DNS of Priebe & Martín, the conditions
of the Bookey et al. experiment (§I).

## Methods the sources use

**LES with a dynamic mixed model.** [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md),
§II, solve the Favre-filtered compressible equations in curvilinear
coordinates with the one-coefficient
[dynamic mixed model](/methods/scale-similarity-model.md) of Martín, Piomelli
& Candler for the subgrid stress and heat flux (a dynamic eddy-viscosity term
plus a scale-similar term built with a top-hat test filter, the coefficients
and the subgrid Prandtl number computed by the
[dynamic procedure](/methods/dynamic-procedure.md) with spanwise averaging),
Knight et al.'s model for the subgrid turbulent diffusion, and the subgrid
viscous diffusion neglected. Inviscid fluxes use a fourth-order
bandwidth-optimized [WENO scheme](/methods/weno-scheme.md) with absolute and
relative limiters and near-wall filtering of the candidate weights, to reduce
its [numerical dissipation](/properties/numerical-dissipation.md); viscous
terms are fourth-order central, time stepping low-storage third-order
Runge–Kutta. The filter width is the cube root of the product of the
computational grid spacings. Inflow comes from an auxiliary boundary layer
with recycling–rescaling over a long box, interpolated onto the ramp inlet,
so that the incoming state stays fixed over the long records the
low-frequency cycle needs (§II, Fig. 1).

**DNS on GPUs.** [Dang et al. (2022)](/sources/dang-2022.md) run the
Mach 2.9 ramp with [OpenCFD-SCU](/software/opencfd-scu.md): Steger–Warming
flux-vector splitting, the seventh-order bandwidth-optimized WENO-SYMBO of
Martín et al. for convection (the scheme of the reference DNS), eighth-order
central viscous terms, and turbulence generated by blowing and suction on
the upstream flat plate rather than by recycling (§5.1, §6.1). The wide-span
grid has $\Delta x^+=1.2$, $\Delta y^+=0.41$, $\Delta z^+=1.2$ at the
reference station 35 mm upstream of the corner, "more than 6000 times the
number [of points] used by Martín et al." (§6.1).

**Wall-modelled LES.** [Hoy & Bermejo-Moreno (2022)](/sources/hoy-2022.md)
use the [Vreman model](/methods/vreman-model.md), the
[equilibrium wall model](/methods/equilibrium-wall-model.md) of Kawai &
Larsson matched at $0.1\delta_0$ with four cells below it, and digital-filter
inflow; the run details are on
[turbulent boundary layers](/benchmarks/turbulent-boundary-layer.md).

**High-order implicit LES with a filtered shock indicator.**
[Gao et al. (2022)](/sources/gao-2022.md) run the split-form
[discontinuous Galerkin spectral element method](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin-spectral-element-method.md)
of [FLEXI](/software/flexi.md) as [implicit LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md),
with no subgrid model and the dissipation of the interface Riemann solver,
and capture shocks with the
[subcell finite-volume scheme](/methods/subcell-finite-volume-shock-capturing.md):
a troubled element of degree $N$ becomes $(N+1)^3$ second-order
finite-volume subcells. On the instantaneous field the Jameson pressure
indicator flags large parts of the under-resolved boundary layer, and
Ducros-type indicators either miss the shocks inside it or flag too much,
expansion fans included; evaluated on the spanwise-averaged pressure, the
Jameson indicator confines the subcells to the shocks, inside the layer as
well, a remedy the authors restrict to quasi-two-dimensional configurations
(§3.2, Figs. 2, 7); see [shock sensor](/definitions/shock-sensor.md). A
positivity-preserving limiter stabilizes the frequent switching. Inflow
turbulence comes from a reformulated synthetic-eddy method with
anisotropic linear forcing over $5\delta_r$–$10\delta_r$ to shorten the
recovery (§5.2; see [inflow turbulence generation](/methods/inflow-turbulence-generation.md)).

**RANS with a compressibility correction.**
[Zhao et al. (2023b)](/sources/zhao-2023-b.md) discretize the mean-flow
equations with interior-penalty
[discontinuous Galerkin](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin-spectral-element-method.md)
and the two [$k$-$\omega$ SST](/methods/k-omega-sst-model.md) equations with
first-order finite volumes, and capture shocks with an artificial viscosity
driven by the residual of the flux divergence (§3.1–3.2).

## What the case tests

**Agreement of LES with DNS at a fraction of the cost.**
[Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md), §III, validate the LES on
grids coarser than the DNS by about $4\times4\times2$ (32 times fewer points)
with a time step about 2.8 times larger. On the incoming boundary layers at
Mach 3, 7 and 10 the mean velocity and temperature are within 3% (5% at
Mach 10) and the peak turbulent kinetic energy within 2% of the top-hat
filtered DNS; the unresolved kinetic energy, estimated from filtered against
unfiltered DNS, is 14% to 25% at Mach 7 and 10% to 15% at Mach 3 and 10
(Figs. 2–5). On the separated Mach 3 ramp the LES reproduces the
separation length, the multi-inflection skin friction inside the bubble, the
wall-pressure plateau that excess numerical dissipation suppresses, peak
shear-layer intensities within 6%, and separation spectra peaking at
$St\approx0.01$–0.03 as in the DNS (Figs. 8–10). On the attached Mach 7 ramp
the skin friction differs from DNS by about 3% on the ramp, the heat-transfer
coefficient by 4%, and the peak reverse-flow probability at the corner is 31%
in both (Figs. 12–13). The authors state the LES costs "3% of the DNS
computational cost" (§V).

**The span of the domain.** [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md), §III C, Table III,
Fig. 8, report for the Mach 3 ramp a separation length of $2.93\delta$ in
the narrow-span DNS, $3.05\delta$ in the narrow-span LES ($2\delta_o$ span,
$\delta_o$ the reference thickness) and $3.71\delta$ in the wide-span LES
($4\delta_o$); they consider the effect physical (spanwise periodicity on the
order of $\delta$), note that many STBLI simulations use spans of about two
boundary-layer thicknesses, and state that the effect "has not been closely
addressed in the literature". The distributions collapse when plotted
against $x'/L_{\mathrm{sep}}$ ($x'$ the distance along the wall from the
corner). [Dang et al. (2022)](/sources/dang-2022.md), §6.1, §7, Figs. 23–25,
do not compare spans but run the Mach 2.9 ramp with a span of more than 29 boundary-layer
thicknesses and see a series of Taylor–Görtler vortices behind the shock,
with several small co-rotating vortices beneath one large vortex and
streak spacings that grow strongly in the interaction; this run is shown by
visualizations only, with no statistics.

**Hypersonic separated ramps.** For the two new LES datasets
[Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md), §IV, Table IV, report:

- mean separation lengths $6.2\delta$ (Mach 7, averaged over
  $3150\,\delta/U_e=510\,L_{\mathrm{sep}}/U_e$) and $4.1\delta$ (Mach 9.1,
  $450\,\delta/U_e=110\,L_{\mathrm{sep}}/U_e$);
- separation spectra peaking at $St_L=0.1$ with energy down to $St_L=0.03$,
  reattachment spectra broadband about $St_L\approx1$ (Fig. 15);
- maximum reverse flow about 12% of $U_e$; peak mean temperature in the
  bubble $8.7T_e$ and $12.5T_e$, 50% and 40% above the inviscid post-shock
  values (Fig. 21);
- peak $C_f$ 9.3 and 11.3 times the incoming value, the rms of $C_f$ up to
  about 20 times upstream, peak Stanton number 15.2 and 16.8 times the
  incoming value (Fig. 23);
- streamwise intensity in the separation shear layer twice the upstream
  maximum, anisotropy invariants moving at the corner toward the
  axisymmetric-compression branch typical of mixing layers, and a separation
  shear layer whose extra production is balanced by transport, convection,
  pressure diffusion and a dissipation dominated by its subgrid part
  (Figs. 24–27).

A record of $100\,L_{\mathrm{sep}}/U_e$, about three low-frequency cycles at
$St_L=0.03$, converges the separated mean flow to within 3%: ten overlapping
segments of the Mach 7 signal give mean separation positions within 3% of
the full average (§IV A, Fig. 16).

**Sensitivity to the subgrid model.** Removing the scale-similar terms
(dynamic eddy viscosity only) changes the Mach 3 separation length by less
than 5% and the attached Mach 7 corner reverse-flow probability from 31% to
35%, but lengthens the separated Mach 7 bubble by 30% (§IV F, Fig. 30). The
difference is not in the incoming layer, whose profiles are identical
(Fig. 31), but in the separation shear layer, whose spreading rate falls from
0.194 to 0.173 without the similarity term; less spreading means less
entrainment and a larger bubble (Figs. 32–33). The authors attribute the
sensitivity to the convective Mach number of about 2 of that shear layer
(about 1 in the Mach 3 case, from their earlier work) and conclude that the
conservative energy exchange of the scale-similar term is necessary there
(§V). The sensitivity is shown for one separated hypersonic case, with one
numerical scheme. See [scale-similarity and mixed models](/methods/scale-similarity-model.md);
the paper speaks of conservative energy exchange and does not use the term
[backscatter](/definitions/energy-backscatter.md).

**Engineering assumptions.** [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md),
§IV E, Figs. 28–29, test the relations RANS and design practice rely on in
the separated hypersonic flows on cold walls: the Reynolds analogy factor
$2C_h/C_f$ is not near one in the separated region ($C_f/2C_h$ is 0.85
upstream and about 0.7 downstream); the Back & Cuffel power law
$q/q_u=(p/p_u)^{0.85}$, derived for attached interactions, over-predicts the
heat transfer at separation and in the recovering layer; $Pr_t$ is within
13% of one upstream between 0.05 and 0.75 local boundary-layer thicknesses
from the wall, but about 1.4 downstream at Mach 9.1; the strong Reynolds analogies fail on these cold walls and the
modified analogy of Huang et al. holds only in parts of the layers. See
[Morkovin's hypothesis](/definitions/morkovin-hypothesis.md).

**Separation, plateau and unsteadiness of an impinging shock.**
[Gao et al. (2022)](/sources/gao-2022.md), §5.3, compare their implicit
LES with the LES of Morgan et al. that they cite (whose case is at
$M=2.05$ with a larger pressure ratio): separation at $16.7\delta_r$
against $17.6\delta_r$, reattachment at $19.5\delta_r$ against
$19.3\delta_r$, from an impingement point at $20\delta_r$ (Fig. 22). They
infer from Morgan et al.'s grid study and theirs that the separation point
is highly sensitive to resolution and conditions while reattachment is
not. A skin-friction plateau appears in the separated region, which,
following Morgan et al., appears only at sufficient resolution; the
authors take it as evidence of the DG accuracy on a grid much coarser than
the reference LES. The mean interaction length, from the mean
reflected-shock foot to the inviscid impingement point, is
$L_{\mathrm{int}}=3.8\delta_r$ (Fig. 23). Wall-pressure spectra show no
spurious low frequency from the synthetic inflow and a low-frequency peak
at $St=fL_{\mathrm{int}}/U_\infty\approx0.03$ near the reflected-shock
foot, consistent with the experiment of Threadgill & Bruce (§5.3.2,
Fig. 24); the separation spectra of
[Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md) on the Mach 3 ramp peak at
$St\approx0.01$–0.03 in a Strouhal number built on $L_{\mathrm{sep}}$, not
$L_{\mathrm{int}}$. The undisturbed boundary layer agrees with the DNS of
Wenzel et al. (see [turbulent boundary layers](/benchmarks/turbulent-boundary-layer.md)).
The cost was about 196,608 CPU hours on up to 16,384 cores; the
performance index rises 18.7% over the shock-free layer, within the
15–20% degradation of the hybrid scheme that they cite from Krais et al.
(§5.3.1–5.3.2).

**Scaling of the interaction length.** Souverein et al.'s experimental law,
as assessed by [Gao et al. (2022)](/sources/gao-2022.md), §5.3.3, reads
$L_{\mathrm{sc}}=1.3(Se_{\mathrm{sc}})^{3.0}$, with the scaled interaction length
$L_{\mathrm{sc}}=L_{\mathrm{int}}\sin\beta_s\sin\varphi_s/(\delta_0^*\sin(\beta_s-\varphi_s))$
and the separation criterion $Se_{\mathrm{sc}}=k_{Se}\Delta p_s/q_0$ (page-local: shock
angle $\beta_s$, flow deflection $\varphi_s$, incoming displacement
thickness $\delta_0^*$, pressure rise $\Delta p_s$ across the reflection
system, dynamic pressure $q_0$, and a factor $k_{Se}$ printed as 3 for
$Re_{\theta_0}>10^4$ and 2.5 below). Their LES gives $L_{\mathrm{sc}}=2.92$ at
$Se_{\mathrm{sc}}=1.39$, 16.4% below the law; the experiment of Threadgill & Bruce
lies 7.8% below. Collecting 23 spanwise-periodic DNS and LES (Table 3),
they find the simulations below the law, especially for
$1.3<Se_{\mathrm{sc}}<1.8$, attribute the deficit to the sidewalls that periodic
simulations lack, consistent with Bermejo-Moreno et al. and Wang et al. as
they cite them, state that "modeling sidewall effects is compulsory" to
match experimental interaction sizes and Strouhal numbers, and fit
$L_{\mathrm{sc}}=0.95(Se_{\mathrm{sc}})^{3.15}$ for spanwise-periodic computations, recommending an
inflow 10–20$\delta_r$ upstream of the interaction for $\delta_0^*$
(Fig. 26). In their Table 3 a smaller span "might" give a larger $L_{\mathrm{sc}}$ and
a higher Reynolds number a larger $L_{\mathrm{sc}}$, both too small to matter for the
scaling.

**A laminar interaction as a resolution test.** On Katzer's laminar
interaction ($M=2$, $Re_{x}=3\times10^5$ at impingement, $p_3/p_1=1.4$)
[Gao et al. (2022)](/sources/gao-2022.md), §4, find Katzer's grid not
converged: the converged separation length, reached by DG at $N=4$ with
182,000 degrees of freedom and by second-order finite volumes with
409,500, is 5.87% larger than Katzer's, against 8.8% for the DNS of
Sansica et al. as cited; at equal degrees of freedom DG is closer to the
converged solution, and its run took 7680 CPU-minutes against 10,368
(Figs. 9, 13).

**Beyond RANS.** Two sources agree that RANS is not adequate for separated
interactions: [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md), §I, call RANS
"notoriously unreliable" for separated STBLIs, with large errors in
separation length and wall heat transfer, and
[Hoy & Bermejo-Moreno (2022)](/sources/hoy-2022.md), §1, state that RANS
cannot predict the strong separation and low-frequency dynamics, while DNS
and wall-resolved LES are too costly at experimental Reynolds numbers.
[Dang et al. (2022)](/sources/dang-2022.md), §1, name the two obstacles of
DNS of such flows as the conflict between robustness at shocks and low
dissipation, and the cost of long domains, small time steps and the long
records of low-frequency shock motion.

On the Settles cavity–ramp at Mach 2.92,
[Zhao et al. (2023b)](/sources/zhao-2023-b.md), §5.4, Figs. 5-14–5-15, find
that the uncorrected SST model "produces excessive turbulent viscosity" in
the compressible shear layer, which widens the shear layer and the shock and
expansion regions, while the model with the compressibility correction of
Suzen & Hoffmann reproduces the measured velocity profiles at three
shear-layer stations; the comparison is of profiles only, with no
separation length or wall heat transfer, the quantities on which Helm &
Martín judge RANS unreliable. On the transonic NACA 0012 at Mach 0.804 the
same solver places the shock near $x/c\approx0.47$ in agreement with the
ONERA data but ends it slightly downstream, which the authors attribute to
an over-estimated shock–boundary-layer interaction (§5.3, Figs. 5-9–5-11);
see [NACA 0012 airfoil](/benchmarks/naca-0012-airfoil.md).

**Transonic buffet on a supercritical airfoil.** At transonic speed the
shock on the suction side of an airfoil interacts with the turbulent
boundary layer and, past a critical incidence, the shock-foot and
trailing-edge separations couple into a self-sustained shock oscillation
(buffet). [Petrocchi & Barakos (2024)](/sources/petrocchi-2024.md) compute
the ONERA OAT15A airfoil of the experiment of Jacquin et al. (chord 0.23 m,
$M_\infty=0.73$, $Re_c=3\times10^6$; as recalled, unsteadiness from
$\alpha=3.1^\circ$ and established buffet at $3.5^\circ$ with a single
frequency of 69 Hz, $St=0.06$) in half of the S3Ch wind tunnel, with slip
upper and lower walls shaped from a preliminary RANS, a viscous side wall
and transition fixed at $x/c=0.07$ (§1, §4.1, Table 1):

- [partially-averaged Navier–Stokes](/methods/partially-averaged-navier-stokes.md)
  on [$k$-$\omega$ SST](/methods/k-omega-sst-model.md), with $f_\varepsilon=1$
  and $f_k$ recomputed every step from the ratio of grid size to modelled
  length scale, clipped below at 0.6, predicts buffet at $\alpha=3.5^\circ$
  on all three grids ($5.0$, $10$ and $16.7$ million cells), whereas SST
  URANS converges to a steady state; in two dimensions SST URANS gives no
  buffet even at $3.9^\circ$ (§4.3, §4.5.5, Table 2);
- on the finest grid PANS reproduces the stall cells and undulating shock
  front of the oil-flow and schlieren images, mean and RMS $C_p$ and the
  LDV velocity profiles, with a buffet frequency of 50–60 Hz against 69 Hz
  (§4.5.3–4.5.6, Figs. 8–15);
- the results depend on the tunnel back pressure (an inlet–outlet ratio of
  0.99 raises the oscillation amplitude), the trip location and the lower
  clip of $f_k$ (0.4 puts the shock too far upstream) (§4.5.2, App. C);
- PANS costs about 3% more than URANS per time step (§4.6, Table 3).

The authors attribute the URANS failure to excessive corner separation at
the side wall, which keeps the shock-foot and trailing-edge separations
from merging, and to a too high post-shock eddy viscosity (§4.5.5). Their
literature table (App. A, Table A.4) shows a wide scatter of URANS buffet
predictions on this case across models and solvers. This is a transonic,
two-dimensional-airfoil interaction in a confined tunnel, not the
supersonic flat-wall configurations above.

**Data assimilation into RANS of an impinging shock.**
[Fanizza et al. (2025)](/sources/fanizza-2025.md), §4.3, assimilate their
own DNS of an oblique shock impinging on a turbulent boundary layer
($M_\infty=2.28$, deflection 9.5°, adiabatic wall, domain
$(7,55)\delta_0\times(0,15)\delta_0$) into steady compressible RANS with the
[Spalart–Allmaras model](/methods/spalart-allmaras-model.md) and the
Spalart–Garbaruk low-Reynolds correction, discretized with modal
[discontinuous Galerkin](/methods/discontinuous-galerkin-spectral-element-method.md)
methods. Baseline SA mispredicts the bubble size. Four correction fields are
inferred by adjoint optimization (see
[field inversion](/methods/field-inversion-machine-learning.md)): a
momentum and energy source $\rho\boldsymbol f_{\mathrm{DA}}$, a multiplier
of the eddy viscosity, a source $\rho\tilde\nu g_{\tilde\nu}$ ($g_{\tilde\nu}$ page-local; the source's $f_{\tilde\nu}$) and a
production multiplier in the SA equation. Normalized errors after
assimilation (velocity / $C_p$ / $C_f$; values above 1 are worse than the
baseline):

| Correction | dense velocity data, wall-weighted (Table 1) | sparse wall $C_p$ data (Table 2) |
|---|---|---|
| momentum and energy source | 0.049 / 0.1825 / 0.0998 | 0.488 / 0.00106 / 0.8540 |
| eddy-viscosity multiplier | 0.250 / 0.5788 / 0.5689 | 0.458 / 0.17357 / 1.657 |
| SA source | 0.257 / 0.2076 / 0.8920 | 0.542 / 0.04961 / 1.700 |
| SA production multiplier | 0.339 / 0.2829 / 1.1450 | 0.427 / 0.05982 / 1.559 |

- With dense data only the momentum source reduces the cost by one to two
  orders of magnitude, the others stalling on a plateau (Fig. 9); it alone
  improves quantities absent from the cost (§4.3.1), and its corrected
  profiles match the DNS in the incoming layer, at separation, at the shock
  reflection and downstream, with $C_f$ still wrong in the first part of
  the bubble (Figs. 11–12). The authors give as a second reason that the
  three other corrections "are still constrained by the Boussinesq
  assumption", while the source corrects the divergence of the Reynolds
  stress directly, in an interaction where the Reynolds stress is not
  aligned with the viscous stress (§4.3.1; see
  [Boussinesq hypothesis](/definitions/boussinesq-hypothesis.md)).
- With wall-pressure data only, the momentum source cuts the $C_p$ error by
  three orders of magnitude but leaves $C_f$ wrong, and the three
  Boussinesq-type corrections make $C_f$ worse than the baseline over the
  whole wall (§4.3.3, Figs. 14–15). An $L^2$ penalty on the correction does
  not cure it; the authors call for regularization with physical prior
  knowledge (§4.3.4, §5).
- The lowest cost minimum on each grid is reached at the lowest
  polynomial degree that is grid-converged for the baseline ($N=1$ on the
  fine grid, $N=2$ on the coarse one); converged steady solutions are needed
  at every optimizer iteration, and wall quantities need reference data on
  grids refined near the wall (§4.3.2, Fig. 13).

**Buffet in a transonic compressor cascade.**
[Klose et al. (2025)](/sources/klose-2025.md) (read in the ASME accepted
manuscript) simulate the transonic cascade TCTA of DLR at its design point
(inlet Mach number 1.21, $Re=1.35\times10^6$ on the chord, inlet angle
145.7°), where a normal bow shock stands at mid-chord on the suction side
and oscillates at low frequency over a laminar or transitional boundary
layer with shock-induced separation. The LES is an
[implicit LES](/definitions/implicit-les.md) with the DG solver of
[TRACE](/software/trace.md): nodal DGSEM at $N=3$, an entropy-conserving
split form, and the
[subcell finite-volume shock capturing](/methods/subcell-finite-volume-shock-capturing.md)
of Hennemann et al. The comparison is a second-order finite-volume URANS
with [$k$-$\omega$ SST](/methods/k-omega-sst-model.md) and the
$\gamma$-$Re_\theta$ transition model. Shock position $x_s$ along the chord
(Table 2):

| Run | span | shock frequency $f c/U_{\mathrm{in}}$ | mean $x_s/c$ | max. amplitude of $x_s/c$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LES5 (coarse) | 5% $c$ | 0.165 | 0.537 | 0.278 |
| LES10 (coarse) | 10% $c$ | 0.164 | 0.531 | 0.172 |
| LES20 (coarse) | 20% $c$ | 0.163 | 0.528 | 0.158 |
| LES10-HR (fine) | 10% $c$ | 0.163 | 0.535 | 0.231 |
| URANS | — | 0.161 | 0.504 | 0.072 |
| experiment | 1.68$c$ (tunnel) | about 0.14 | — | 0.13 |

- *Span.* The shock frequency and mean position are almost insensitive to
  the span, while the amplitude and the separation bubble shrink as the
  span grows. The authors read this as partial agreement with Touber &
  Sandham (bubble size sensitive to span), but with a frequency "less
  dependent on the bubble size" (§5); a Strouhal number of 0.03 on the
  separation length, which Klose et al. take from Touber & Sandham, gives
  $L_{\mathrm{sep}}/c\approx0.18$, inside the observed 0.12–0.22.
- *Resolution.* Refining at 10% span changes little upstream of the shock
  but raises the amplitude from 0.172 to 0.231; the authors assume that
  numerical dissipation of the coarse LES and, more, of the URANS damps the
  structures that drive the larger oscillation, and call the coarse runs
  under-resolved (§6).
- *URANS.* It needs $\Delta t=2.8\times10^{-5}c/U_{\mathrm{in}}$ to get the
  shock motion; it captures the frequency but puts the shock upstream,
  gives less than half the LES20 amplitude and a mostly laminar suction
  side with wrong surface distributions (§6, Fig. 7).
- *Mechanism.* SPOD shows a dominant mode at $fc/U_{\mathrm{in}}=0.16$ and
  harmonics at 0.32, 0.48 and 0.64; as the shock moves downstream the bubble
  grows, perturbations travel upstream through the subsonic part of the
  boundary layer and produce intermittent oblique compression waves, driven
  by shocklets running upstream from the pressure side (§7). All runs give
  $St\approx0.16$ against about 0.14 measured. The cascade itself is on the
  [compressor cascade](/benchmarks/compressor-cascade.md) page.

**Shocks in decaying turbulence without a wall.** The supersonic
[Taylor–Green vortex](/benchmarks/taylor-green-vortex.md) at $M_0=1.25$
of [Chapelier et al. (2024)](/sources/chapelier-2024.md) tests the same
shock-capturing families (artificial diffusivity, subcell finite volumes,
TENO, hybrid central/WENO) on shock–turbulence interaction without a
boundary layer.

**Fluid–structure coupling.** In the impinging-shock case of
[Hoy & Bermejo-Moreno (2022)](/sources/hoy-2022.md), §4.3, panel flexibility
moves separation about $3\delta_0$ upstream and lengthens it from
$7.8\delta_0$ to $12.6\delta_0$, lowers the peak wall pressure and shifts the
dominant frequencies down.

## Qualifications

- Coordinates: [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md) write $z$
  wall-normal and $y$ spanwise, hat for the Favre filter and a second hat for
  the test filter; this page uses $y$ wall-normal, and the subgrid model is
  described in words. They define $u_\tau=(\tau_w/\rho_e)^{1/2}$ with the
  edge density, where the registry uses the wall density, and a recovery
  factor 0.9 in $T_r$ (the sources cited for $T_r$ on
  [turbulent boundary layers](/benchmarks/turbulent-boundary-layer.md) use
  0.89).
- [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md) give the increase of the
  Mach 7 separation length without the scale-similar term as "as much as a
  32%" in the abstract and as 30% in §IV F and §V; their abstract gives the
  LES time step as three times the DNS one, §V as 2.8 times. Their
  Table III prints the wide-span DNS row (R24-M3w-D) identical in every
  column to the narrow-span row, although the text says the two separation
  lengths differ, and several figure captions misname the cases (e.g.
  "R24-M10-L", "R35-M10-L" for R34-M10-L).
- The Mach 7 and Mach 9.1 separated ramps of
  [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md) have no DNS or experimental
  reference; they are validated only through the Mach 3 and attached Mach 7
  cases. §V calls the 32-fold reduction in grid points "equivalent to 3%
  of the DNS computational cost" and discusses the larger time step and the
  model overhead separately. §III B prints the unresolved energy at the wall
  of the Mach 7 layer both as 25% and as "a maximum of 18%".
- [Dang et al. (2022)](/sources/dang-2022.md) were read in an author
  manuscript. Their validation (mean wall pressure, van Driest velocity
  profile against the DNS of Martín et al. and the experiment of Bookey et
  al.) is on the $16100\times240\times200$ grid; the wide-span run has no
  quantitative comparison, and its averaging time is not stated.
- The cavity–ramp of [Zhao et al. (2023b)](/sources/zhao-2023-b.md) is a
  reattaching cavity shear layer, not the boundary layer meeting a ramp or
  an impinging shock of the other configurations; its incoming Reynolds
  number and grid resolution are not printed.
- [Petrocchi & Barakos (2024)](/sources/petrocchi-2024.md) compare only
  with the experiment of Jacquin et al. and do not state the filter width
  $\Delta$ that enters their dynamic $f_k$. Their Table 3 percentages of
  the cost increase (for example "+3.1%" for total times of 2.00 against
  1.95 s, which give +2.6%) cannot be reproduced from the printed times
  *(compiler check, not in the sources)*; the text says "about 3%". Their
  own SST URANS gives no buffet at $3.5^\circ$, while other SST URANS
  results in their Table A.4 do; the scatter is recorded by the authors.

- [Fanizza et al. (2025)](/sources/fanizza-2025.md) do not print the
  Reynolds number of their interaction. Their §4.3.1 says the three
  non-source corrections reduce all errors "by less than one order of
  magnitude", while Table 1 gives the production multiplier a $C_f$ error
  of 1.1450, above the baseline. Their Tables 3 and 4 print different
  penalty weights for two of the corrections, and their §4.3.4 says the
  penalty degrades velocity and wall-pressure errors although Table 3
  against Table 2 shows the velocity error improving for the eddy-viscosity
  multiplier and the SA source at both penalty weights and for the
  production multiplier at one of them.
- In [Klose et al. (2025)](/sources/klose-2025.md) the amplitude is not
  grid-converged (0.172 to 0.231 under refinement at fixed span), so the
  "very good" agreement of the experimental 0.13 with the coarse LES20
  (0.158) is not a converged comparison, as the authors concede for a
  refined LES20. Their resolution is "deemed sufficient for LES" with
  maxima of $\Delta x^+=65$, $\Delta y^+=2.5$, $\Delta z^+=40$ per
  polynomial degree on the coarse grid, which §6 calls under-resolved. No
  inflow turbulence is prescribed (measured level below 1%).

- [Gao et al. (2022)](/sources/gao-2022.md) call 7680 CPU-minutes
  "around 35% less" than 10,368; it is 26% less, and 35% is the excess of
  the finite-volume run *(compiler check, not in the sources)*. Their
  Table 3 lists $Re_{\theta}=1864$ and $L_{\mathrm{sc}}=2.33$ for the present LES,
  against 2188 and 2.92 in the text; their Eq. 20 with the printed
  $L_{\mathrm{int}}=3.8\delta_r$, $\delta_r=0.507$ mm, $\delta_0^*=0.1139$ mm,
  $\beta_s=37.2^\circ$, $\varphi_s=8^\circ$ gives $L_{\mathrm{sc}}=2.917$
  *(compiler check, not in the sources)*. Their $Se_{\mathrm{sc}}$
  values, this one ($p_3/p_1=2.30$, $\gamma=1.4$, $M=2$: 1.39) and those of
  the Table 3 entries at $Re_\theta<10^4$, correspond to $k_{Se}=3$, not to
  the 2.5 they print for that range *(compiler check, not in the
  sources)*. They use $x_r$ for both the
  reference station and the reattachment point.
- The span trends differ: in [Helm & Martín (2022)](/sources/helm-2022.md)
  doubling the LES span from $2\delta_o$ to $4\delta_o$ lengthens the
  Mach 3 ramp separation from $3.05\delta$ to $3.71\delta$ (§III C,
  Table III), while [Gao et al. (2022)](/sources/gao-2022.md)
  read in their compilation of impinging-shock simulations (Table 3) that a
  smaller span "might" give a larger interaction length (§5.3.3). The two
  statements concern different configurations (compression ramp, impinging
  shock) and are recorded side by side.

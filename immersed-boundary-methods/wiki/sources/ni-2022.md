# Summary

Ni, Li, Zhang, Zhou and Cui couple a finite-volume Riemann solver for shocks
to the material point method for fracturing solids through a "Lagrangian
continuous-forcing" immersed boundary ([fluid–structure coupling](/methods/fluid-structure-coupling.md)):
boundary cells found from the particle distribution act as ghost cells whose
velocity is set to the structure's, and the reaction is returned to the
particles. No interface needs to be reconstructed, so fragments and fresh
interfaces are handled ([shock–obstacle interaction](/benchmarks/shock-obstacle-interaction.md)).

# Key claims

1. Penalty-type continuous forcing cannot satisfy the velocity condition exactly; with about eight boundary particles per cell against about five grid points in the kernel support the system is over-constrained and oscillates, erasing Mach reflection (§5.2). **Stated**
2. Discrete forcing (ghost-cell) needs normals, image points and interface reconstruction, impractical with fragments (§1, §5.2). **Stated**
3. A two-step cell classification from the particle distribution identifies boundary cells, including thin fragments and isolated particles (§5.1). **Stated**
4. Setting the fluid velocity in boundary cells to the structure velocity from nodal momentum enforces the condition exactly at each Runge–Kutta stage and equals an Eulerian force; the reaction force goes evenly to the particles in the cell (§5.2.3). **Derived**
5. Shock diffraction on a cylinder reproduces regular and Mach reflection and triple-point trajectories; a shock-deformed panel matches Giordano et al.'s experiment; a detonation-driven steel shell fragments as in Tang's experiment (§6). **Shown**
